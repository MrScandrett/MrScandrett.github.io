01

Plan

Three builds, one idea

Every fidget you print is the same trick.

A 3D printer is a robot that draws with a 0.4 mm pen and stacks the drawings 0.2 mm apart. It has no idea what a hinge is. If two surfaces are close enough for the molten plastic to touch, they weld into one solid piece — so the only thing separating a spinner from a paperweight is whether you left the robot enough room to draw two separate outlines.

You will build three objects. Each one adds exactly one new constraint, and the third one is the fidget everybody actually wants.

BUILD 01

Clearance comb

Six slots, from 0.15 mm to 0.5 mm, cut in one Boolean. Prints in ten minutes and tells you the one number the rest of this lesson depends on.

Boolean · 10 min print
BUILD 02

Captured spinner

A disc that spins inside a cup, printed as one piece. You draw the clearance straight into a cross-section and revolve it with the Screw modifier.

Screw revolve · 40 min print
BUILD 03

Spiral cone fidget

The boss fight. A cone shell sliced into one ribbon that winds eight times up the axis, stretches like a spring, and snaps back.

Solidify + Screw + Boolean · 2 hr print

Pick your tool

TINKERCAD

Free, in a browser, already in millimetres

Adds and subtracts shapes from a fixed library — which is all Builds 01 and 02 need, and it will never let you get the units wrong. The spiral cone needs its Codeblocks mode, because the editor cannot sweep a curve.

BLENDER

Installed, and it can sweep any profile

Revolves a cross-section into a solid and cuts a true helix with a Boolean, so all three builds happen in one place. The price is that you have to tell it you are working in millimetres.

Every build below carries both paths — pick a tab and follow it. The design numbers never change; only the clicks do.

Why the third build needs a different approachTinkercad is a constructive solid geometry modeller: every object is primitives added together or subtracted. Blender is a mesh modeller, where you can draw any profile and sweep it along any path. A helical ribbon is a swept curve — so in Blender it is a two-modifier stack, and in Tinkercad it has to be approximated by a few hundred primitives placed by a loop.
Bring your printer's numbersEverything here is written for a 0.4 mm nozzle at 0.2 mm layers in PLA — the classroom default. If your machine differs, the labs below recompute for it; change the nozzle and layer dropdowns and the safe gaps move with them.
CheckpointYou can explain, in one sentence, why a printed hinge does not need to be assembled — and what physically happens if its gap is too small.
02

Gap

The one measurement that matters

Clearance has two different sizes.

Designers say "leave 0.4 mm clearance" as if a gap were a gap. It is not. A gap between two walls standing side by side is a slot the nozzle has to drive around. A gap between one part and the part above it is a ceiling the next layer has to fly across. Those two situations fail in opposite directions, so they get different numbers.

Clearance Lab

A cross-section of two neighbouring walls, drawn bead by bead

CROSS-SECTION
0.4 mm

Each rounded block is one extruded bead. A bead is always a little wider than the nozzle that made it — that spread is what welds parts together.

      XY clearance ≈ one nozzle width0.4 mm for a 0.4 mm nozzle. Narrower and the slicer either merges the two perimeters or squirts a gap-fill bead into the slot, welding both walls.
      Z clearance ≈ one layer height0.2 mm at 0.2 mm layers. The moving part's first layer sags across the gap, lands, cools, and never bonds — because it was never pressed into hot plastic.
      Too big fails tooPast about 0.6 mm in Z there is nothing close enough underneath. The first layer curls, catches the nozzle, and the print derails. Clearance has a floor and a ceiling.
      Why one nozzle width, exactlyA slicer draws toolpaths, not shapes. It can only place a bead where a whole bead fits. A 0.25 mm slot in your model is a slot the toolpath planner cannot represent at 0.4 mm — so it disappears, and the two "separate" parts are printed as one continuous outline.
      03

      Comb

      Build 01 · measure your own machine

      Stop guessing. Print the comb.

      Nozzle wear, filament brand, and how squished your first layer is all shift the real minimum clearance by a tenth of a millimetre or two. Ten minutes of printing replaces every guess in this lesson with a measured number.

      Blender path

      1. Set Blender to millimetres

        Open Properties Editor → Scene → Units. Unit System: Metric, Unit Scale: 0.001, Length: Millimeters. Now typing 50 into a dimension field means 50 mm, and your STL will export at the size you think it is.

        Units
      2. Add the plate

        Shift+A → Mesh → Cube. In the N sidebar set Dimensions to 50 x 24 x 3 mm and Location Z to 1.5 mm so it sits on the ground plane. Then Ctrl+A → Scale.

        Plate
      3. Add six cutters

        Add a Cube with Dimensions 0.15 x 16 x 5 mm at Location (−20, −4, 1.5). Shift+D to duplicate it five times, moving each to X = −12, −4, 4, 12, 20 and setting its X dimension to 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 mm. Apply scale to every one of them.

        Cutters
      4. Join, then cut once

        Select all six cutters, Ctrl+J to join them into a single object. Select the plate, then Properties Editor → Modifiers → Add Modifier → Generate → Boolean, Operation: Difference, Object: the joined cutters. One modifier, six slots.

        Boolean
      5. Print it flat, no supports

        Export STL, slice at 0.2 mm layers with supports off, and print. Then hold it to a light: the first slot you can see through, and whose finger flexes on its own, is your machine's minimum XY clearance.

        Measure

      Tinkercad path

      1. Start with a Ruler

        New 3D design, then drag the Ruler out of the right-hand toolbar onto the workplane. Every shape you select now shows editable width, length, height, and position boxes — all in millimetres, which is what makes Tinkercad immune to the unit-scale trap.

        Ruler
      2. Drop the plate

        Drag a Box onto the workplane and type its size into the dimension boxes: 50 wide, 24 long, 3 high.

        Plate
      3. Make one slot

        Drag a second Box, size it 0.15 x 16 x 5, and click Hole in the Inspector — it turns transparent. Slide it so it crosses the front edge of the plate and stops 8 mm short of the back, leaving a spine.

        Hole
      4. Duplicate across the plate

        Ctrl+D five times. Set each copy 8 mm further along X and change only its width: 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5. At 0.15 mm the hole is thinner than the line drawn on screen — trust the number box, not your eyes.

        Duplicate
      5. Group once, cut six times

        Select everything (Ctrl+A) and press Ctrl+G. In Tinkercad, Group is the Boolean: solids merge, holes subtract, all in one step.

        Group
      6. Export and print

        Export → .STL, slice at 0.2 mm with supports off, and print flat. Same test as before: the first slot you can see light through is your machine's real minimum clearance.

        Export
      Write the number on the printSharpie your measured clearance onto the comb and keep it by the printer. Every fidget in this lesson — and every hinge, lid, and captive nut you design afterwards — starts from that number.
      CheckpointYour comb has slots that visibly exist at the wide end and visibly vanished at the narrow end. If all six slots opened, your printer is unusually well tuned; if none did, re-check that supports were off and that the slots survived into the slicer preview.
      Every slot printed solid?
      • Look at the slicer preview, not the model. If the preview shows one solid outline, the slots never made it to the toolpath — the gaps are smaller than one extrusion width.
      • Check that you applied scale (Ctrl+A → Scale) before the Boolean. An unapplied scale silently resizes the cutters.
      • Confirm the plate really is 50 mm long in the slicer. If it arrived 0.05 mm long, your unit scale is wrong — see Build 03's export step.
      04

      Revolve

      Build 02 · the skill the cone needs

      Draw the gap into the cross-section.

      Round parts are not modelled by pushing vertices around in 3D. You draw a flat outline — the cross-section of the part, measured from the axis outward — and spin it. In Blender that is the Screw modifier, and it is the same tool that will wind the spiral cone, so learn it on something simpler: a disc captured inside a cup, printed as one piece, spinning on the first try.

      Here is the whole design. Read it as a slice through the middle: the left edge is the axis, and every measurement is a radius.

      Blender path

      Type these into the N sidebar with one vertex selected. X is the radius, Y stays 0, Z is the height.
      PartX (radius)Z (height)What it is
      Cup00Centre of the base, on the bed
      Cup200Outside edge of the base
      Cup2010Top of the outer wall
      Cup13.610Lip — narrower than the disc, so it cannot escape
      Cup16.67.0Ceiling of the groove, at 45° so it self-supports
      Cup16.62.6Outer wall of the groove
      Cup02.6Floor the disc spins on

      Disc86.8Back to the finger hole
      1. Make an outline, not a face

        Add a Plane, Tab into Edit Mode, select all, then X → Only Faces. You are left with four vertices joined by four edges — a closed outline the Screw modifier can sweep into a surface.

        Outline
      2. Place the vertices

        Select one vertex at a time and type its X and Z from the table into the N sidebar. For the cup, select an edge and press E to extrude the extra vertices until all seven are placed and the loop closes.

        Profile
      3. Revolve it

        Properties Editor → Modifiers → Add Modifier → Generate → Screw. Axis: Z, Angle: 360°, Screw: 0 mm, Steps: 64, and turn on Merge Vertices so the seam closes into a watertight surface.

        Screw
      4. Build the disc the same way

        Four vertices, same modifier, same settings. Leave both objects in the scene, exactly where they sit. You are not going to cut anything — the air between them is already the design.

        Disc
      5. Print without touching the slicer's support button

        Supports off. The disc's first layer prints across 0.2 mm of air over the cup's floor, lands, and cools without bonding. That is the whole trick.

        Print

      Tinkercad path

      Tinkercad thinks in diameters and heights above the workplane, so the same design reads like this.
      ShapeSize (mm)Height above workplaneSolid or hole
      CylinderØ40 x 10 high0Solid — the blank
      CylinderØ33.2 x 4.4 high2.6Hole — the groove
      ConeØ33.2 x 16.6 high7.0Hole — the 45° ceiling
      CylinderØ32.4 x 4 high2.8Solid — the disc
      CylinderØ16 x 4 high2.8Hole — the finger hole
      1. Raise the side count first

        Drag in a Cylinder and set Sides to 64 in the Inspector before you size it. Tinkercad's default 20-sided cylinder is a visible polygon, and a polygonal disc grinds instead of spinning.

        Sides
      2. Blank, then groove

        Size the cylinder Ø40 x 10. Add a second cylinder Ø33.2 x 4.4, click Hole, use Align to centre it on the blank, and raise it 2.6 mm above the workplane.

        Groove
      3. Cut the ceiling with a cone

        Add a Cone, size it Ø33.2 x 16.6 high, make it a Hole, centre it, and raise it 7.0 mm. A cone whose radius equals its height has 45° sides — so this one cut gives you a self-supporting ceiling and leaves a Ø27.2 lip at the top.

        Chamfer
      4. Group the cup — and only the cup

        Select the blank and its two holes, then Ctrl+G. Do this before the disc exists. If the disc is in the selection when those holes fire, they will eat straight through it.

        Group
      5. Build the disc separately

        Cylinder Ø32.4 x 4 with a Ø16 hole through the middle, grouped together, raised 2.8 mm, then aligned to the cup's centre. Leave it as its own object so you can still nudge it.

        Disc
      6. Check the three gaps by arithmetic

        Ø33.2 − Ø32.4 leaves 0.4 mm all the way around. 2.8 − 2.6 leaves 0.2 mm underneath. 7.0 − 6.8 leaves 0.2 mm above. Tinkercad will not warn you about any of these — the numbers are the only check you get.

        Verify
      Why the ceiling is chamfered at 45°A flat ledge over the disc would be an unsupported horizontal roof. Sloping it from r16.6 to r13.6 over 3 mm of height makes every layer land on the one below at exactly 45° — the steepest angle a printer can hold without support. Self-supporting geometry is designed, not fixed later.
      CheckpointYour cup and disc are two separate objects that never touch, and you can state the three gaps out loud: 0.4 mm at the side, 0.2 mm below, 0.2 mm above.

      Design

      Design it before you model it

      The spiral cone is five numbers.

      Base radius, height, turns, cutter thickness, ribbon thickness. Every failure mode of this fidget is one of those five numbers being wrong, and you can find out which one before spending two hours of printer time. Drag the sliders and watch the verdicts: they run the same three rules a slicer will run — one nozzle width of air, 45° of lean, two beads of wall.

          The three rules the lab is checking

          ∠

          Can it hold itself up?

          Lean = atan(radius ÷ height)

          A 30 mm radius over a 60 mm height leans 26.6° from vertical — comfortably inside the 45° rule, so every layer lands on the one below and no supports are needed.

          ↕

          Will the windings separate?

          Air = slot · cos(lean)

          The cut is horizontal but the wall is not, so a 0.5 mm cutter leaves only 0.46 mm of real air between the ribbon faces. That is why the cutter is drawn thicker than the clearance you measured on the comb.

          ▮

          Will it survive being pulled?

          Wall = 3 · nozzle

          1.2 mm of ribbon is three solid beads with no infill. Two beads flexes further but snaps near the tip; one bead is not a fidget, it is confetti.

          Rise per turn is your budgetHeight ÷ turns is how much room each winding gets. Split 60 mm across 8 turns and each ribbon gets 7.5 mm of rise: 7 mm of plastic plus the half-millimetre the cutter takes away. Ask for 20 turns in the same 60 mm and each winding drops to 3 mm — thin, floppy, and mostly gap.
          CheckpointFind a setting where all four verdicts are green, then break exactly one of them on purpose and say which physical failure it would cause on the bed.
          06

          Spiral

          Build 03 · the boss fight

          Cut a helix out of a cone.

          The finished fidget is a cone-shaped shell sliced into one continuous ribbon by a single helical cut. So build it that way: make the shell, then make a cutter that spirals up the axis and Boolean it away. Every winding is separated from the next by exactly the thickness of that cutter.

          Blender path

          1. Make the cone surface

            Shift+A → Mesh → Cone. In the operator panel set Vertices: 96, Radius 1: 30 mm, Radius 2: 3 mm, Depth: 60 mm, Base Fill Type: Nothing. Set Location Z to 30 mm so the base sits on the ground plane.

            Cone
          2. Give it wall thickness

            Add Modifier → Generate → Solidify, Thickness 1.2 mm, Offset 0. Solidify measures perpendicular to the surface, so the ribbon ends up 1.2 mm thick everywhere — three beads, no infill.

            Solidify
          3. Draw the cutter's cross-section

            Add a Plane, X → Only Faces, and place its four vertices at X = 1 and 35, Z = 0 and 0.5. It is a flat blade 0.5 mm thick that reaches from near the axis out past the widest part of the cone.

            Blade
          4. Spiral the cutter up the axis

            Add Modifier → Generate → Screw. Axis: Z, Angle: 360°, Screw: 7.5 mm, Iterations: 9, Steps: 64, Merge Vertices on. Set the cutter's Z location to about 4 mm so the bottom winding survives whole. Screw is the same modifier from Build 02 — the only change is that it now climbs 7.5 mm per turn instead of 0.

            Screw
          5. Cut the spiral

            Apply the modifiers on both objects. Select the cone, then Add Modifier → Generate → Boolean, Solver: Exact, Operation: Difference, Object: the cutter. Apply it. The shell is now one continuous ribbon wound eight times, separated by a 0.5 mm helical slot.

            Boolean
          6. Add a base ring so it sticks

            The bottom rim touches the bed along a 1.3 mm annulus — not enough. Revolve a second profile (X = 28.2 to 31.8, Z = 0 to 1.6, Screw: 0, Angle: 360°) into a ring and Boolean Union it to the cone, giving the spring a fixed foot to push against.

            Base
          7. Name it and save

            Save as spiral-cone-fidget.blend before the export step. The next section will send you back here at least once.

            Save

          Tinkercad path

          Tinkercad's 3D editor only adds and subtracts shapes from a fixed library. It has no revolve, no sweep, and no taper — so there is no shape to cut a helix with. What it does have is Codeblocks: a loop that can place a few hundred small ribbon segments along a conical helix and weld them into one body.

          1. Open a Codeblocks design

            From your Tinkercad dashboard choose Create → Codeblocks. You get a block workspace on the left and a live preview on the right that rebuilds every time you press Play.

            Codeblocks
          2. Set the design numbers as variables

            Create variables for turns = 8, perTurn = 24, baseR = 30, tipR = 3, H = 60, ribbon = 1.2, and band = 7. Those are the Spiral Cone Lab's numbers — the same design, driven by arithmetic instead of a modifier.

            Variables
          3. Loop once per segment

            Add Control → Count with i from 0 to (turns · perTurn). Inside it compute t = i ÷ (turns · perTurn), then r = baseR − (baseR − tipR) · t, a = 360 · turns · t, and z = H · t.

            Loop
          4. Place one ribbon segment

            Add a Box and set its width to ribbon, its length to 2 · 3.1416 · r ÷ perTurn · 1.4, and its height to band. Making it 1.4 times the arc it has to cover means neighbouring segments overlap and fuse into one continuous ribbon instead of a string of beads.

            Segment
          5. Rotate first, then move

            Rotate the box by a degrees around Z, then move it by r · cos(a) in X, r · sin(a) in Y, and z in Z. Do it in that order: the segment is still sitting on the origin when it turns, so it lines up with the curve before it is carried out to its radius.

            Transform
          6. Play it, then read the gap

            Rise per turn is 60 ÷ 8 = 7.5 mm and each segment is 7 mm tall, so the loop leaves a 0.5 mm helical gap — exactly the slot the Blender cutter carves. Watch the preview build: if the ribbon spirals off the axis or winds the wrong way, your rotate and move are in the wrong order.

            Play
          7. Add the base ring in the 3D editor

            Send the result to Tinkercad (or export STL and re-import), then add a ring for bed adhesion: a Ø63.6 cylinder with a Ø56.4 hole through it, 1.6 mm tall, grouped to the spiral.

            Base
          Keep the segment count sane24 segments per turn is 192 boxes, which Codeblocks handles comfortably. Push it to 60 per turn for a smoother ribbon and you are asking the browser to boolean 480 solids — it will crawl. This faceting is the honest cost of building a curve out of primitives; Blender's Boolean cuts a true helix at any resolution.
          Why the cutter reaches all the way to X = 1The cone's radius shrinks from 30 mm to 3 mm as it climbs. A cutter parked at a fixed radius would slice the bottom windings and miss the top ones entirely. Sweeping from near the axis outward guarantees the blade crosses the wall at every single height.
          The base ring is not just adhesionA spring needs something to push against. Fusing the bottom winding to a solid ring is what lets you pull the tip and feel it snap back instead of unrolling the whole cone in your hand.
          The Screw modifier produced a mess instead of a helix
          • Check that the profile is edges only. If the plane still has its face, the revolve sweeps a solid that folds in on itself.
          • Keep every vertex at a positive X. A profile crossing the axis turns inside out as it revolves.
          • Angle is per iteration, not total. 360° with 9 iterations gives nine turns; 3240° with 9 iterations gives eighty-one.
          • If the Boolean returns garbage, apply the Screw modifier first, then run 3D-Print Toolbox → Check All on the cutter itself. A cutter with holes in it cuts holes that make no sense.
          07

          Print

          Check, export, slice

          Let Blender find the flaws first.

          Blender ships with a print checker most people never turn on. Enable it once and it will tell you, before you waste filament, whether your mesh is a real solid and whether anything on it is too thin or too steep to print.

          1. Turn on the 3D-Print Toolbox

            Edit → Preferences → Add-ons, search for "3D Print", and enable 3D Print Toolbox (older builds list it as "Mesh: 3D-Print Toolbox"). It appears as a 3D-Print tab in the N sidebar.

            Add-on
          2. Run Check All

            Set Thickness to 0.8 mm and Overhang to 45°, then press Check All. You want zero non-manifold edges and zero intersections. Anything reported gets clicked — the toolbox selects the exact faces at fault.

            Check
          3. Export in millimetres

            File → Export → STL (.stl) with Scale 1.00 and Selection Only ticked. Then verify in the slicer: the object must read roughly 60 x 60 x 62 mm. If it arrives as a 0.06 mm speck, your scene unit scale was never set to 0.001.

            Export
          4. Slice it deliberately

            0.2 mm layers, 3 walls, supports off, brim on if the base ring lifts, seam position Random, cooling fan 100% after the third layer, PLA at the cool end of its range. Vase mode stays off — it would delete the gap entirely.

            Slice
          5. Read the preview before you press print

            Drag the layer slider to the middle of the cone. You should see two separate ribbon cross-sections with visible air between them. One merged blob means the gap did not survive slicing — go back and widen it by 0.1 mm.

            Verify

          If you modelled in Tinkercad

          No unit trapTinkercad works in millimetres and exports millimetres, so Ø60 in the editor arrives as 60 mm in the slicer. Glance at the dimensions anyway — it costs nothing.
          Nothing to make manifoldGrouped solids and holes always come out as a closed shell, so Tinkercad cannot hand you a leaking mesh. What it also cannot do is warn you about a 51° overhang or a 0.6 mm wall. That judgement is entirely yours.
          Export everything, not the selectionThe export dialog offers the whole design or just the selected shape. Choose the whole design, or the disc sitting inside the cup gets left behind and you print half a fidget.
          Supports would destroy this printSupport material is generated wherever the slicer sees overhang — including inside every millimetre of your helical gap. It would print a scaffold in the one place that must stay empty and weld the fidget solid. Design so you never need it; that is what the 45° rule bought you.
          Expect a light tackA print-in-place part often comes off the bed slightly stuck. One firm twist frees it. If it needs pliers and force, your gap was too small.
          Watch the tipThe last few windings are tiny, so each layer returns before the previous one cooled. Turn on minimum layer time or slow the top 20% to about 20 mm/s.
          Elephant's footSquished first layers spread outward and can weld the bottom winding to the base ring in the wrong place. Enable elephant-foot compensation if the lowest turn refuses to move.
          08

          Fix

          Read the failure, then fix the number

          Every failure names its own cause.

          It printed as one solid cone

          The slot never reached the toolpath. Check the slicer preview at mid-height, then make the cutter 0.1 mm thicker — remember the lean costs you the cosine of the wall angle.

          The ribbon snapped when I pulled it

          Under three beads of wall, or a tip that tapers to nothing. Take the Solidify thickness to 1.2–1.6 mm and keep Radius 2 at 3 mm instead of 0.

          Stringy chaos above half height

          The cross-section gets tiny near the tip, so layers stack before they cool. Enable minimum layer time, run the fan at 100%, and drop the top-section speed.

          It fell over mid-print

          The bottom winding alone barely touches the bed. Add the base ring, add a brim, and confirm the ring is actually unioned to the spiral rather than floating beside it.

          The slicer shows a 0.06 mm object

          Scene unit scale. Set Scene → Units → Unit Scale 0.001, Length: Millimeters, re-export, and re-check the dimensions in the slicer every time.

          Tinkercad: the disc is fused to the cup

          Check the arithmetic before you blame the printer. Ø33.2 − Ø32.4 must leave 0.4 mm, and 2.8 − 2.6 must leave 0.2 mm. Tinkercad lets two solids overlap by a tenth of a millimetre without a word, and Group then welds them into one body.

          Tinkercad: the Codeblocks spiral is a string of beads

          Each segment is shorter than the arc it has to cover. Raise the 1.4 length multiplier, or raise perTurn so the segments are smaller and closer together.

          Blender reports non-manifold edges

          Usually the Boolean. Apply scale on both objects (Ctrl+A → Scale), switch the solver to Exact, and re-cut. Stray loose edges left over from a profile also count — select all in Edit Mode and use Mesh → Clean Up → Delete Loose.

          Only the bottom two windings are stuck
          • Classic elephant's foot: the first layers are squished wider than designed and bridge the gap. Enable elephant-foot compensation, or lower the bed temperature slightly.
          • Check your first-layer squish. An over-squished first layer spreads plastic 0.1–0.2 mm past where the model said it should be — enough to close a 0.4 mm gap.
          • Try twisting rather than pulling straight up. The windings shear apart along the helix far more easily than they separate axially.

          Key vocabulary

          Print-in-place
          A design whose moving parts are printed already assembled, separated only by designed gaps.
          Clearance
          The deliberate air gap between two parts that must not fuse.
          Extrusion width
          How wide one deposited bead is — slightly wider than the nozzle that made it.
          Overhang
          A surface leaning far enough from vertical that a layer hangs past the one below it. 45° is the practical limit.
          Manifold
          A watertight mesh with a real inside and outside — the only kind a slicer can fill.
          Screw modifier
          Revolves a profile around an axis, optionally climbing as it turns, producing a solid of revolution or a helix.
          Rise per turn
          Height divided by turns: how much vertical room each winding of a spiral gets.
          Constructive solid geometry
          Modelling by adding and subtracting primitive shapes only — how Tinkercad works.
          Hole (Tinkercad)
          A shape flagged to subtract itself from whatever it is grouped with.
          Codeblocks
          Tinkercad's block-programming mode, where a loop can place hundreds of shapes by arithmetic.
