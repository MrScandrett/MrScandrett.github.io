Emily Roebling

The Brooklyn Bridge Caisson Disease Catenary Curves De Facto Chief Engineer

Emily Warren Roebling (1843–1903) was the woman who kept the Brooklyn Bridge rising when its chief engineer could no longer visit the site. When her husband Washington Roebling was disabled by decompression sickness, Emily taught herself structural engineering, cable construction, and catenary mathematics, then spent roughly a decade managing the project's contractors, engineers, and trustees — becoming, in every way but title, the bridge's chief engineer.

Who Was Emily Roebling?

Born Emily Warren on September 23, 1843 in Cold Spring, New York, she was the daughter of a prosperous family whose brother, Union Army general Gouverneur K. Warren, encouraged her education — unusual for a woman of her era. That encouragement gave her the intellectual grounding and confidence that would later prove essential to one of the 19th century's greatest engineering projects.

In 1865 she married Washington Roebling, son of John A. Roebling, the engineer who had designed a bold new suspension bridge across the East River connecting Brooklyn and Manhattan. John A. Roebling died of tetanus in 1869 after an injury sustained while surveying the bridge site, before construction began in earnest, leaving his son Washington to take over as chief engineer.

Building the bridge required workers to dig its foundations inside massive pressurized underwater chambers called caissons. Around 1872, Washington Roebling suffered a severe case of decompression sickness — known then as "caisson disease" and now understood as the bends — from working in those pressurized conditions. The illness left him largely bedridden and unable to visit the site directly for most of the remaining construction.

Emily Roebling stepped into the gap. She studied higher mathematics, catenary curve calculations, cable construction, and other engineering topics on her own so she could understand her husband's instructions and communicate them accurately to the project. For roughly a decade, through the bridge's completion in 1883, she managed correspondence with contractors and engineers, made site visits, and dealt directly with the board of trustees — serving as the project's day-to-day construction manager in all but title. Some later historians and engineers have argued she deserves recognition as a de facto chief engineer for this period.

The Brooklyn Bridge opened on May 24, 1883, and Emily Roebling was the first person to cross it, riding in a carriage that carried a rooster, a traditional symbol of victory. She went on to earn a law degree from New York University in 1899 and remained active in civic and women's organizations for the rest of her life. She died on February 28, 1903, at age 59.

The Physics Behind the Bridge

Two pieces of engineering shaped Emily Roebling's decade on the project: the hazard that disabled her husband, and the mathematics she had to master to replace him as the project's communicator.

Caisson disease. To dig the bridge's foundations down to bedrock beneath the East River, workers descended into huge sealed chambers called caissons, pressurized with compressed air to keep water and mud out. Spending hours in that high-pressure environment let extra nitrogen dissolve into the bloodstream. If a worker returned to normal atmospheric pressure too quickly, that dissolved nitrogen formed bubbles in the blood and tissues — a real physics and physiology hazard now known as decompression sickness, or "the bends." Washington Roebling's severe case left him with lasting pain, weakness, and sensitivity to light and sound.

Catenary curves. The Brooklyn Bridge is a suspension bridge, meaning its roadway hangs from massive steel cables strung between towers. To calculate how much load those cables could bear and how they needed to be shaped, engineers relied on the mathematics of the catenary curve. Emily Roebling taught herself this material so she could translate her husband's technical instructions to the project's engineers and contractors with confidence.

That same natural hanging shape, inverted and reinforced, describes the load-bearing curve of the Brooklyn Bridge's suspension cables — the math Emily Roebling mastered to help guide the bridge to completion.

Did Emily Roebling actually design the bridge?
The original design was John A. Roebling's, and Washington Roebling remained chief engineer on paper throughout construction. But for roughly a decade, Emily was the only reliable link between Washington's technical direction and the people building the bridge day to day — a role that required real engineering understanding, not just message-passing.
Why was caisson disease so poorly understood at the time?
The mechanism — nitrogen bubbles forming as dissolved gas comes out of solution during rapid decompression — wasn't well understood until later in the 19th century. Workers on the Brooklyn Bridge caissons, including Washington Roebling, paid the price of that gap in knowledge.

Roebling's Legacy Today

When it opened in 1883, the Brooklyn Bridge was the longest suspension bridge in the world and remains one of the great engineering achievements of the 19th century — a structure that still carries traffic across the East River more than 140 years later.

For decades, popular accounts of the bridge credited only the Roebling men — John A. Roebling as designer and Washington Roebling as chief engineer — while minimizing or omitting Emily Roebling's role. Historians and engineers have since worked to correct that record, recognizing the depth of technical understanding and day-to-day leadership her decade on the project actually required. In 1983, on the bridge's centennial, a plaque was added crediting her contribution.

Practice Problems

Think about the Brooklyn Bridge, caisson disease, and Emily Roebling's life.

Easy1. In what year did the Brooklyn Bridge open?

Hint: This is the same year Emily Roebling became the first person to cross the finished bridge.

Easy2. What condition disabled Washington Roebling and forced Emily to take on his engineering duties?

Hint: It struck workers who returned too quickly from the pressurized underwater caissons to normal atmospheric pressure.

Medium3. Emily Roebling effectively led the Brooklyn Bridge project from around when Washington fell ill (1872) until the bridge's completion in 1883. About how many years did that span?

Hint: Subtract 1872 from 1883.

Medium4. What mathematical curve did Emily Roebling study in order to understand the bridge's suspension cables?

Hint: It's the natural curve a cable or chain forms when it hangs freely under its own weight.

Challenge5. What physically causes caisson disease (the bends) in workers who decompress too quickly?

Hint: High pressure inside the caisson lets extra gas dissolve into the blood; returning to normal pressure too fast lets it form bubbles.

Medium6. Emily Roebling was born in 1843 and died in 1903. At what age did she die?

Hint: Subtract her birth year from her death year: 1903 − 1843.

Easy7. What symbol of victory rode along with Emily Roebling on her first crossing of the finished bridge?

Hint: It's a bird traditionally associated with triumph and the crowing of a win.

Challenge8. What did the 1983 plaque added to the Brooklyn Bridge on its centennial recognize?

Hint: It corrected decades of accounts that credited only the Roebling men for the bridge's completion.
