02 · Read a lead sheet

One line of melody, a row of chords

A lead sheet is the shortest useful way to write a song down. A pianist, a guitarist and a bass player can all read the same page. Each one plays the chords their own way.

The melody

The single line of notes on the staff. If the tune starts before bar 1, those first notes are a pickup. “When the Saints” has three of them.

The chord symbols

The chord symbols above the staff tell the band what harmony to play. The symbol lasts until the next one appears.

Key & time

The sharps or flats at the start of each line are the key signature. The two numbers are the time signature: 4/4 has four beats in a bar, 3/4 has three.

Why “fake”?

The band “fakes” the accompaniment: they make it up from the chord symbols. In the 1940s and ’50s, working musicians passed around copied, unlicensed fake books. The Real Book began as a student-made collection in the 1970s and was later published legally.

Transposing instruments read a different key from the one the band hears. A B♭ trumpet playing a written C sounds a B♭, so its part is written a whole step higher than concert pitch. Choose your instrument in panel 1 and the fake book rewrites the melody and the chords for you.

03 · Grow an idea

Composers don’t only invent. They transform.

Most music is built from a few ideas that keep changing. Each button in the fake book is a tool composers have used for centuries. Try one, listen, and ask: is it still the same tune?

Upside down

Melodic inversion mirrors every move: a step up becomes a step down. Bach’s fugues are full of upside-down themes. Folk tunes that move by step, like Frère Jacques, still sound natural flipped.

Backwards

Retrograde plays the pitches from last to first. Bach wrote a “crab canon” that works forwards and backwards at the same time. Here the rhythm stays put, so you hear only the new note order.

Passing tones & skeleton

These two work in opposite directions. Passing tones add notes that walk by step between the main ones, the way singers decorate a tune. The skeleton strips the tune down to its strong-beat notes, the bones the melody hangs on.

Chord tones

Every note moves to the nearest note of the chord above it. This is how jazz players learn to “make the changes”: a solo built from chord tones always fits.

Rhythm

Long-short and short-long turn even notes into dotted pairs. Push the beat moves notes half a beat early. That is syncopation, the push behind ragtime, jazz and pop. Swing plays eighth notes long-short without changing how they are written.

Chord inversions

A chord inversion puts a different note on the bottom. Pianists choose their voicings for smooth voice leading: each finger moves as little as possible to the next chord. A shell voicing keeps only the root, 3rd and 7th.

04 · Practice plan

Fifteen minutes with one tune

  1. Listen (2 min). Melody + band at a slow tempo. Follow the moving highlight and sing along quietly.
  2. Play the tune (3 min). Switch to Band only and play the melody yourself. Loop a hard bar by clicking it, and slow the tempo until it’s easy.
  3. Change the key (2 min). Pick a new key and play it again. The shape of the tune stays the same; only the starting note moves.
  4. Change one thing (4 min). Pick one melody tool and one rhythm tool. Play the variation, then make up your own in between the two.
  5. Trade fours (4 min). The tune plays four bars, then you get four bars to answer. Start with only chord tones and a rhythm you like. The walking bass and the piano comping keep the harmony going. That is trading fours.

Metronome tip: set the click to Beats 2 & 4 for swing tunes, the way jazz drummers play the hi-hat. Then try 2 bars on, 2 bars off: if you’re still in time when the click comes back, your inner pulse is steady.

05 · Why these tunes are free

Old songs belong to everyone

The 95-year rule

In the United States, a song published between 1927 and 1977 is protected for 95 years. So as of 2026, everything published before 1931 is in the public domain. Most of these tunes are much older: Greensleeves was registered in London in 1580.

The tune, not every version

A new arrangement, new lyrics, or a recording can have its own copyright even when the melody is free. That is why these lead sheets were written for this site from the old melodies, with simple chords added.

Made here, free to share

“Classroom Blues” is an original riff written for this site over the twelve-bar blues form. Any class may copy it, play it and change it.

Elsewhere in the world

Copyright terms are different in other countries. The tunes here are traditional or centuries old, so they are free in most places. Check before you publish a recording.

06 · For teachers

Using it with a class or a band

  • Mixed instruments. Pick the concert key once. Each student picks their instrument and gets a correctly transposed part: B♭, E♭, F, or bass clef.
  • Project it. The highlight follows the beat, and the banner announces the count-in, the pickup and each student’s turn during Trade fours.
  • Theory on demand. Click any chord symbol to show its notes on the keyboard. Switch voicings to compare root position, inversions and smooth voice leading.
  • Composition prompt. Students pick one tune and one transformation, then write their own 4-bar variation in the Sheet Music Trainer or play it on the piano.
  • Adding tunes. Tunes live in data/fake-book.json. The format block at the top explains the notation. Add only public-domain or original melodies, and say where each one came from.
  • Link straight to a tune. Add #tune=saints (or any tune id) to the page address.

Learning goals

  • Read a lead sheet: melody, chord symbols, key, time and pickup
  • Transpose a tune and write it for B♭, E♭ and F instruments
  • Change a melody by inversion, retrograde, passing tones and chord tones
  • Change a rhythm with dotted pairs, syncopation and swing
  • Voice chords in root position, inversions and with smooth voice leading
  • Keep steady time with a metronome and a band, and improvise in a trade

Materials

  • This page, with sound on (headphones help)
  • Any instrument or your voice

Extension

Combine three tools: Upside down + Push the beat + a new key. Can a classmate name the original tune just by listening?

Key vocabulary

Lead sheet
Melody plus chord symbols on one staff.
Fake book
A book of lead sheets; players make up the accompaniment.
Chord symbol
A name like G7 or Am written above the melody.
Pickup
Notes before the first full bar.
Transpose
Move a whole tune into a different key.
Concert pitch
The notes as they actually sound.
Inversion (melody)
The tune turned upside down.
Retrograde
The tune played backwards.
Inversion (chord)
A chord with a note other than the root on the bottom.
Voice leading
Moving between chords by the shortest steps.
Syncopation
Notes that push against the beat.
Swing
Eighth notes played long-short.
Walking bass
A bass note on every beat, stepping toward the next chord.
Trading fours
Taking turns improvising four bars each.
