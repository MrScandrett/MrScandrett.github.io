Scott Joplin

Composer & Pianist c. 1868–1917 “King of Ragtime” Pulitzer Prize, 1976

Scott Joplin (c. 1868–1917) was the son of a formerly enslaved father and a freeborn mother, and he became the “King of Ragtime.” His Maple Leaf Rag was one of the best-selling pieces of sheet music of its time, and his syncopated rhythms helped lay the ground for jazz. He saw ragtime as serious art — and wrote an opera to prove it.

Who Was Scott Joplin?

Joplin was born around 1868 in northeast Texas — the exact date is not known — and grew up in Texarkana. His father, Giles, had been enslaved; his mother, Florence, was freeborn and worked cleaning houses. According to accounts of his childhood, Scott taught himself on a piano in one of the homes where his mother worked. A German-born music teacher, Julius Weiss, noticed his talent and gave him free lessons in classical music and opera.

As a young man Joplin travelled as a pianist through the Mississippi Valley, playing in saloons, clubs and dance halls, and he was in Chicago in 1893 during the World’s Fair, where ragtime was reaching big crowds. Around 1894 he settled in Sedalia, Missouri, where he taught, played, and took music classes at the George R. Smith College for African Americans.

In 1899 the Sedalia publisher John Stark printed the Maple Leaf Rag, paying Joplin a royalty of one cent per copy — unusual for a Black composer at the time. It sold steadily for years and gave him a lasting income. More rags followed, including The Entertainer (1902). In 1908 he published School of Ragtime, a set of exercises that warned players that ragtime should never be played fast.

Joplin moved to New York and poured his energy into an opera, Treemonisha, about a young Black woman who leads her community through education. He could not find a producer and published it at his own expense in 1911; it was never fully staged in his lifetime. Seriously ill, he died in New York on April 1, 1917, and was buried in an unmarked grave. A ragtime revival in the 1970s — boosted by the film The Sting — brought him new fame, and in 1976 he received a special Pulitzer Prize.

Syncopation: Accents Between the Beats

Most marches and dances put their strong notes on the beat. Ragtime keeps a steady beat in the left hand but lets the right-hand melody arrive early or late — “ragged” time. Putting emphasis between beats is syncopation.

To count it, split each beat into four sixteenths and say “1 e & a, 2 e & a.” In bar 5 of The Entertainer, the right hand plays E on “1,” then a C that starts on “e” and lasts until “a.” Your ear expects the C on the beat, so arriving early gives it a kick.

Joplin’s own recordings survive because of a 1910s technology: the piano roll. A long paper roll was punched with holes; in a player piano, air flowing through each hole pushed one key down. Each hole’s position across the roll chose the note and its length along the roll chose how long it lasted — music stored as data, just like a MIDI file today.

1 e & a
Left hand: bass on “1,” chord on “&,” bass on “2,” chord on “&” — perfectly even
Right hand, bar 5: E on “1,” C from “e” to “a,” E on “a,” C on “2”…
Result: two rhythms at once — steady below, ragged above

Clap the left hand’s eighths while a partner claps the right hand’s pattern. Where do your claps line up, and where do they disagree?

Is that really Joplin playing?
The Maple Leaf Rag recording below comes from a piano roll Joplin made in 1916, near the end of his life. Because a roll stores the timing of each key press, a player piano can still reproduce his performance today.
Why does the score say “Not fast”?
Joplin felt ragtime was played too fast by showy pianists. In School of Ragtime he wrote that it is never right to play ragtime fast. Try the score at 70% speed.
How is ragtime related to jazz?
Ragtime’s syncopation and its blend of African American rhythm with European forms fed directly into early jazz in the 1910s. Jazz loosened the rhythms further and added improvisation.

Scott Joplin's Legacy Today

Ragtime was the first distinctly African American musical style to sweep the whole United States, and Joplin was its finest composer. Its syncopations flowed into jazz, and from there into most American popular music.

For decades after his death Joplin was nearly forgotten. Then, in the 1970s, musicians recorded his rags again, Treemonisha finally received a full staging in 1972, and Marvin Hamlisch’s arrangement of The Entertainer for the film The Sting became a hit. The special Pulitzer Prize awarded in 1976 recognised what Joplin had believed all along: that ragtime was serious American art.
