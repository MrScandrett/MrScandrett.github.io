Meet Django Reinhardt

Born in Belgium in 1910 into a Sinti musical family, Reinhardt grew up in a travelling community and later lived near Paris. He played banjo-guitar before becoming a leading jazz guitarist. His music brought American jazz into conversation with the sounds and skills of musicians around him.

A caravan fire in 1928 badly injured his left hand. He developed new ways of fingering melodies and chords. His artistry came from musical imagination, practice, and collaboration; his injury alone does not explain his sound.

In 1934 he and violinist Stéphane Grappelli formed the Quintette du Hot Club de France with two rhythm guitarists and a double bassist. Its string-band sound became central to Jazz Manouche, also called Sinti swing. The historical label “Gypsy jazz” is common in record catalogs, but “Gypsy” can be offensive when used for people.

A life in music

  1. 1910 Born in Liberchies, Belgium.
  2. 1928 A fire injures his hand; he adapts his playing technique.
  3. 1934 Forms the Quintette du Hot Club de France with Grappelli.
  4. 1937 The quintet records Minor Swing, credited to Reinhardt and Grappelli.
  5. 1940 Records Nuages during the war years.
  6. 1953 Dies in France; his compositions and recordings continue to inspire guitarists.

A solo can travel while the harmony stays clear

A chord tone belongs to the chord sounding underneath a melody. Over A minor, A, C, and E are chord tones. Play those notes one at a time and you have an arpeggio. An improviser can connect them with other notes, repeat a small idea, leave space, and aim for a chord tone when the harmony changes.

In the quintet, rhythm guitars supply short chord strokes known as la pompe (“the pump”), while the double bass grounds the pulse. Guitar and violin can take turns leading. A steady accompaniment gives the soloist room to stretch a phrase without losing the shared beat.

Swing often makes a pair of eighth notes unequal: longer, then shorter. The balance changes with tempo and performer. A fixed long–short ratio is a useful classroom comparison, not a rule that describes every jazz performance.

Keep the chord, change the timing

Hear an original eight-note A-minor arpeggio over four steady chord strokes. Change only the subdivision: can you keep tapping the same four beats?

    Listen like an arranger

    Use the publisher’s Reinhardt: Nuages recording page to find Minor Swing and Nuages. Playback availability may depend on the service and region. The browser experiment above is an original teaching pattern, not either composition.

    1. Minor Swing — Reinhardt and Grappelli. First listen for the rhythm guitars and bass. Then follow the lead instrument: where does a short idea repeat or get answered? Write one observation about accompaniment and one about the solo.
    2. Nuages — Reinhardt. Compare its lyrical melody with the energy of Minor Swing. Hum a short contour without copying every decoration. Does a slower phrase still need a clear pulse?
    3. Compare performances. Record the performer and recording date you actually hear. Different recordings of the same tune may have different instrumentation and solos.

    A lasting musical voice

    Reinhardt helped establish the guitar as a powerful jazz solo voice. His partnership with Grappelli also shows how a band’s sound grows from relationships between players. Jazz Manouche remains a living tradition, with musicians learning the repertoire and making new music.

    Compare Louis Armstrong’s phrasing and Charlie Parker’s chord-tone lines. Then visit Francisco Tárrega: how does a solo guitarist supply the support that Reinhardt’s band supplies?

    Teacher discussion guide

    Ask students to name the part they changed and the part they kept steady. Accept different phrases when students can explain their choices using pulse, pitch, texture, or balance. Replay a short example to test their explanation.
