Johann Sebastian Bach

Composer & Organist Baroque, 1685–1750 Master of Counterpoint Teacher of His Own Children

Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) wrote music the way an engineer builds a clock: several independent melodies, each one singable by itself, fitted together so precisely that they make harmony at every moment. He worked as a church musician and court composer in Germany, wrote hundreds of pieces for worship, teaching and celebration, and was mostly forgotten by the public for decades after he died — before becoming one of the most studied composers in history.

Who Was Bach?

Bach was born in Eisenach, in central Germany, on March 21, 1685, into a family that had produced professional musicians for generations. Both of his parents died before he turned ten, and he went to live with his older brother Johann Christoph, an organist, who gave him keyboard lessons. As a teenager he won a place at a choir school in Lüneburg and began the organ career that would make him famous.

In 1705, as a young organist in Arnstadt, Bach was given a few weeks off to travel — on foot, about 400 km (250 miles) — to Lübeck to hear the celebrated organist Dieterich Buxtehude. He stayed roughly four months, far longer than his leave, and came home full of new ideas. Over the next years he worked in Mühlhausen and then at the court of Weimar, where he wrote much of his greatest organ music. When he asked to leave Weimar for a better job in 1717, the duke had him jailed for about a month before letting him go.

At Köthen (1717–1723) Bach directed a court orchestra and wrote instrumental music, including the Brandenburg Concertos and the suites for solo cello. His first wife, Maria Barbara, died in 1720; the next year he married the singer Anna Magdalena Wilcke. Between his two marriages Bach had twenty children, about half of whom survived childhood, and several became important composers themselves. Much of his keyboard music, including the two-part Inventions, began as lessons for his own children and students.

From 1723 until his death Bach was Thomaskantor in Leipzig: in charge of music for the city’s main churches and of the boys at the St. Thomas School. For years he wrote a new cantata almost every week. Near the end of his life his eyesight failed, and two eye operations in 1750 left him nearly blind. He died in Leipzig on July 28, 1750. Fashion had moved on to simpler styles, and much of his music went unperformed until a young Felix Mendelssohn conducted the St. Matthew Passion in 1829 and sparked a Bach revival.

  4. 1708–17 Organist and concertmaster at the court of Weimar; writes much of his organ music.

  6. 1722–23 Finishes Book 1 of The Well-Tempered Clavier and a fair copy of the Inventions and Sinfonias.
  7. 1723 Becomes Thomaskantor in Leipzig, where he stays for the rest of his life.
  8. 1727 First performance of the St. Matthew Passion.
  9. 1750 Dies in Leipzig on July 28, after failed eye surgery.
  10. 1829 Mendelssohn revives the St. Matthew Passion, starting a Bach revival.

Counterpoint: Melodies That Fit Together

Counterpoint is the art of combining independent melodies so that each makes sense on its own and together they make good harmony. In Bach’s time it was the core of a composer’s training, and nobody did it better.

A particularly strict kind of counterpoint is a canon: one voice follows a rule to copy another. In a simple round, the copy starts later — the same idea as singing a round like “Row, Row, Row Your Boat.” In this kind of canon, the pattern moves in time (a delay) and sometimes in pitch (a transposition). In Invention No. 8 the delay is one bar and the transposition is one octave down.

A fugue grows the same way but more freely: a short theme enters in one voice after another, then gets developed, turned upside down, stretched or squeezed. Bach’s Well-Tempered Clavier contains 48 preludes and fugues, two in every major and minor key.

Canon
Leader: plays the melody, starting in bar 1
Follower: plays the same melody, starting in bar 2, one octave (8 letter names) lower
Rule: every moment where they overlap must still sound good together

Same notes, shifted in time and pitch. Bach had to choose each new note so it worked as a melody and against the copy.

Why is it called an “Invention”?
In Bach’s day an inventio meant a musical idea worth developing — the way a speaker “invents” an argument. Bach’s title page said the pieces would teach players to handle two (and then three) voices well and to play in a singing style.
What does “well-tempered” mean?
Keyboards have to be tuned with small compromises so that music sounds acceptable in every key. A “well-tempered” tuning makes all 24 major and minor keys usable. Bach proved it by writing a prelude and fugue in each one. Modern pianos use equal temperament, where each half step multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of 2 (about 1.0595).
Is the canon in Bach’s portrait real?
Yes. The sheet he holds is a short puzzle canon for six voices (BWV 1076). It is written as just a few lines; performers have to work out where the other voices come in.

A canon can also transform its melody. In the Crab Canon, two voices read the same line in opposite directions. This backwards order is called retrograde.

Four More Ways to Hear Bach

A chorale, an orchestral movement, a musical puzzle and a keyboard aria: listen for how Bach gives each musical line a purpose. BWV means Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis, the catalogue of Bach’s works; the number identifies the work.

Open a performance below, then try its listening task. These videos come from the Netherlands Bach Society’s All of Bach project; the performers and recordings retain their own rights.

Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring

BWV 147 · Closing chorale, “Jesus bleibet meine Freude” · 1723

This familiar English title belongs to music from Bach’s church cantata Herz und Mund und Tat und Leben. The choir sings a steady hymn melody while the instruments weave a flowing line around it. The cantata’s two parts end with the same chorale music and different words.

Listen for two layersFollow the instruments first, then the choir. Tap the main pulse and notice how the flowing instrumental notes move in groups of three. Does the instrumental line stop when the singers pause?

Air on the G String

BWV 1068 · Air from Orchestral Suite No. 3 in D major

Bach called this movement simply Air. Its long violin melody floats above a steadily moving bass, while the inner string parts fill out the harmony. The performance below uses the orchestral version.

The nickname comes from violinist August Wilhelmj’s later arrangement, which moves the melody lower so it can be played entirely on the violin’s lowest string, the G string. The Boston Symphony explains the title.

Follow the bassListen to the low instruments before following the high melody. How does their regular movement support a melody that often holds a note across the beat?

The Crab Canon

BWV 1079 · Canon a 2 cancrizans from The Musical Offering · 1747

One written melody produces two parts: one reads from beginning to end while the other reads from end to beginning. Reversing the order of the musical events is called retrograde. Bach makes the two directions fit together as counterpoint.

Hear the puzzle being solvedIn Shunske Sato’s film, listen to the line forwards, then backwards, then together. Try reading the sequence A–B–C–D backwards: D–C–B–A. That illustrates the rule; it is not an excerpt from Bach’s score.

Goldberg Variations: Aria

BWV 988 · Opening aria · Published 1741

This gentle keyboard aria opens the Goldberg Variations and returns after thirty variations. Here, aria describes an instrumental piece with a singing character. Jean Rondeau plays it on harpsichord, whose strings are plucked rather than struck as on a piano.

The variations build on the aria’s underlying bass and harmonic pattern. The decorated upper melody is only one part of the design.

Listen beneath the ornamentsStart with the opening Aria in the film, then compare Variation 1. Follow the low notes: what feels familiar even when the rhythm and upper melody change? Use the film’s chapters to return to the Aria.

Watch and listen: Aria, followed by the variations

Jean Rondeau, harpsichord; Netherlands Bach Society. This film contains the complete cycle; begin with the opening Aria. Watch on YouTube · Work and recording credits.

Compare the four: Which piece places a choir inside flowing instrumental music? Which sets a long melody over a steady bass? Which reverses a line? Which supplies the harmonic foundation for a set of variations? Point to something you heard as evidence.

Bach's Key Contributions

🎼
Master of Counterpoint

His fugues and canons set the standard for combining independent melodies. The Art of Fugue works through one theme in more than a dozen ways.

🎹
The Well-Tempered Clavier

Two books of preludes and fugues, one in every major and minor key (Book 1 finished in 1722), showing that a well-tempered keyboard could play in all 24.

⛪
Music for Worship

As Thomaskantor he wrote church cantatas week after week. About 200 survive, alongside the St. Matthew Passion and the Mass in B minor.

🎻
Instrumental Showpieces

The six Brandenburg Concertos, the suites for solo cello, and the sonatas and partitas for solo violin are still core repertoire for those instruments.

📚
Music Written to Teach

The Inventions, the Orgelbüchlein and the notebooks for his family were lessons first. Students around the world still learn from them.

🎵
Four-Part Chorales

His hundreds of chorale harmonizations became the textbook example of how four voices move together, and harmony classes still use them.

Bach's Legacy Today

Bach never travelled far and published only a small part of what he wrote, yet later composers treated his music as a textbook. Mozart studied his fugues; Beethoven learned the Well-Tempered Clavier as a boy; Chopin practised it before his concerts. Musicians still learn harmony from Bach’s four-part chorales.

His music also reaches beyond the concert hall. Three Bach pieces are on the Golden Record carried by the two Voyager spacecraft launched in 1977, chosen to represent humanity to anyone who might find them. And the logic of his counterpoint — patterns copied, shifted and combined by rules — makes him a favourite example for mathematicians and computer scientists exploring how structure can create beauty.

Practice Problems

Review: Biography · The musical idea · Read and play · Four featured works.

Use the scores, biography and listening notes above.

Easy1. In what city did Bach spend the last 27 years of his life?

Hint: He was Thomaskantor there and is buried in St. Thomas Church.

Easy2. Look at the score. How many beats are in each bar of Invention No. 8?

Hint: Read the top number of the time signature (3/4).

Medium3. In the Invention, how many bars later does the left hand start copying the right hand?

Hint: The right hand starts in bar 1. Find the first bar where the left hand plays notes.

Medium4. The right hand begins on F4 and the left hand copies it starting on F3. How far apart are they?

Hint: Same letter name, octave number one lower.

Medium5. How does a simple round work?

Hint: Think of singing a round.

Medium6. The Well-Tempered Clavier has two books, each with a prelude and fugue in all 24 major and minor keys. How many prelude-and-fugue pairs is that altogether?

Hint: 2 books × 24 keys.

Challenge7. In equal temperament each half step multiplies the frequency by about 1.0595. A4 is 440 Hz. About what frequency is A♯4, one half step higher? (Round to the nearest whole hertz.)

Hint: 440 × 1.0595 ≈ 466.2.

Challenge8. Why was much of Bach’s music rarely performed in the decades after his death?

Hint: Think about the 1829 revival — what had happened to tastes in between?

Medium9. In the Crab Canon, how is the second part made from the written melody?

Hint: Retrograde changes the order of events, like reading a sequence from its end back to its beginning.

Medium10. What provides the shared foundation for the Goldberg Variations?

Hint: Listen underneath the decorated upper melody of the Aria.
