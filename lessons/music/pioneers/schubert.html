Arranging with care

A source staff is not always the melody: in an orchestral score, find the staff carrying the theme. A two-part piano study pairs your selected voice with a source bass; it is not a concert reduction and wide leaps may need redistribution. Saxophone notation is transposed while playback stays at concert pitch. Violin parts stay at concert pitch and are moved by octaves to sit above the open G string. Guitar tab preserves pitch and rhythm but may need a different octave or fingering. Chords are prepared for the Ode to Joy theme only; classical harmony elsewhere requires score study rather than automatic chord guesses. Original ensembles are available only where the source contains those parts. Piano reductions and original classroom studies retain their source identities.

Life and context

Franz Schubert (1797–1828).

Schubert lived in Vienna and composed songs, chamber works, piano music, and symphonies. His German art songs, or Lieder, made the relationship between poetry, voice, and piano a powerful form of musical storytelling.

The piano as storyteller

An accompaniment can tell us something beyond the sung words. In Erlkönig, insistent piano triplets create urgency; in Gretchen am Spinnrade, the piano suggests the spinning wheel and the emotional world of the singer.

Guided listening

Choose a performance of one of these works. Listen once for its overall character, then again for the detail in the prompt.

The D. number identifies a work in the Deutsch catalogue of Schubert’s music. Start with one work from each group; for a longer work, choose the movement named below.

Songs: the voice and piano tell a story

  • Erlkönig, D. 328 · Voice and piano. Distinguish the narrator, father, son, and Erlking through changes in the singer’s register and expression. What does the repeated piano rhythm add?
  • Gretchen am Spinnrade, D. 118 · Voice and piano. Follow the turning accompaniment, and notice when its motion pauses. How does the piano reflect Gretchen’s thoughts?
  • Winterreise, D. 911 · Song cycle. Compare “Gute Nacht” with “Der Leiermann,” the opening and closing songs. Describe how the piano changes your sense of the traveler’s surroundings.
  • Die schöne Müllerin, D. 795 · Song cycle. Begin with “Das Wandern.” Listen for a steady sense of movement in the piano and compare it with the walking pulse of “Gute Nacht.”
  • Die Forelle (“The Trout”), D. 550 · Voice and piano. Listen for quick piano figures around the vocal melody. How might those figures suggest the movement of water or a fish?

Piano: songs without words

  • Impromptu in E-flat major, D. 899, No. 2 · Solo piano. Follow the flowing runs, then listen for the more forceful contrasting section. Describe how the character changes even without words.
  • Impromptu in G-flat major, D. 899, No. 3 · Solo piano. Keep your attention on the singing upper melody above the continuous accompaniment. Can you hum the melody without copying the moving notes underneath?
  • Moment musical in F minor, D. 780, No. 3 · Solo piano. Tap the pulse and listen for short, recurring rhythmic gestures. Which details make the music feel like a dance?
  • Fantasia in F minor, D. 940 · Piano four hands: two players at one piano. Listen to the opening theme and its later return. How can the same melody feel different after the intervening music?

Chamber music: a conversation among instruments

  • Piano Quintet in A major (“Trout”), D. 667 · Piano, violin, viola, cello, and double bass. Start with the fourth movement, variations on the song “Die Forelle.” Follow the tune as its accompaniment and instrumental color change.
  • String Quartet in D minor (“Death and the Maiden”), D. 810 · Two violins, viola, and cello. Start with the second movement. Compare its quiet theme with a more intense variation: what changes in rhythm, texture, or volume?
  • String Quintet in C major, D. 956 · Two violins, viola, and two cellos. Start with the second movement. Compare the spacious opening with the agitated middle section, then listen for the opening’s return.

Orchestra: storytelling on a larger scale

  • Symphony in B minor (“Unfinished”), D. 759 · Orchestra. Start with the first movement. Hear the low-string opening, then follow the contrast between darker music and a more lyrical theme. Which orchestral sounds make each character clear?

Try a comparison: hear the song “Die Forelle,” then the fourth movement of the “Trout” Quintet. What can instruments preserve from a song when the words are removed?

Practice and reflection

  1. Choose two works from different listening groups. Name the instruments you hear and explain one way each accompaniment supports its melody.
  2. Play both sound examples. Describe one feature that changes and one that stays the same.
  3. What information could an accompaniment add that a singer’s words do not state?
  4. Create a four-note idea at the piano. Make two versions using today’s musical idea, then explain your choices to a partner.
Check your listening

Compare a melody on its own with the same melody over a repeated pulse. Imagine a different setting for each version. A useful answer names a feature you can hear and explains its effect. More than one interpretation is possible.
