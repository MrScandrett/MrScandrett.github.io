Duke Ellington

Composer, Pianist & Bandleader Jazz, 1899–1974 The Cotton Club Partner of Billy Strayhorn

Duke Ellington (1899–1974) led his orchestra for about fifty years and treated it the way a painter treats a palette. He wrote for the particular sound of each musician, so a melody might be shared between a growling trumpet, a sweet alto saxophone and a low clarinet. He wrote well over a thousand compositions, from three-minute dance records to concert suites and sacred music.

Who Was Duke Ellington?

Edward Kennedy Ellington was born in Washington, D.C., on April 29, 1899. Both of his parents played the piano, and they raised him to have fine manners; a childhood friend nicknamed him “Duke” for his elegant clothes and bearing. He started piano lessons at about seven but cared more about baseball and drawing. As a teenager, hearing ragtime pianists around Washington drew him back to the keyboard. He was soon leading his own small bands, and he turned down an art scholarship to keep playing music.

In 1923 he moved to New York with a band called the Washingtonians, and within a year he was its leader. In December 1927 his orchestra began a residency at Harlem’s Cotton Club, a nightclub where Black performers entertained whites-only audiences. Radio broadcasts from the club made Ellington famous across the country. Hits such as Mood Indigo (1930) showed his gift for unusual blends of instruments.

Ellington kept many of the same players for years, sometimes for decades, and wrote parts to fit their individual sounds. In 1939 the young composer and pianist Billy Strayhorn joined him. Strayhorn wrote Take the “A” Train, which became the orchestra’s theme song, and the two worked so closely that it can be hard to tell who wrote which bars. Ellington also wrote longer works, such as Black, Brown and Beige, a musical history of Black Americans that premiered at Carnegie Hall in 1943.

When big bands faded after World War II, Ellington kept his orchestra going, partly paying for it with his songwriting royalties. A thrilling performance at the 1956 Newport Jazz Festival renewed his fame. In his later years he toured the world and wrote three Sacred Concerts. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1969 and died in New York on May 24, 1974.

Orchestral Color: Writing for Sounds, Not Just Notes

Timbre (TAM-ber) is tone color: the quality that lets you tell a trumpet from a clarinet even when they play the same pitch. Texture is how many layers sound at once and how they relate: one melody alone, a melody with accompaniment, or several lines answering each other.

Most arrangers of his day wrote by section: all the saxophones together, all the trumpets together. Ellington often mixed instruments from different sections and wrote for particular people: Johnny Hodges’s smooth alto saxophone, or the “growl” of trumpeters Bubber Miley and Cootie Williams, who used a rubber plunger mute to make the horn sound almost like a voice. He wrote for those sounds, not just for “a trumpet.”

In Mood Indigo he gave a three-note chord to muted trumpet, muted trombone and clarinet, and turned the usual order upside down: the clarinet sits at the bottom in its low register, and the trombone plays high in its range. The blend sounds like a single new instrument.

Color
Timbre: same pitches + different instruments = a new color
Texture: melody alone sounds thin; melody + an answering line sounds fuller
Ellington’s method: write for the player, not just the instrument

His band kept many of the same musicians for years, so he could plan music around sounds he knew well.

Who wrote Take the “A” Train?
Billy Strayhorn. The title comes from Ellington’s directions for getting to his home in Harlem by the A train on the New York subway. Ellington’s orchestra recorded it in 1941, and it became their theme song. Jazz history includes collaborators, not just the name on the bandstand.
What is a plunger mute?
A rubber cup, sometimes an actual sink plunger from a hardware store, held over the bell of a trumpet or trombone. Moving it opens and closes the sound to make “wah-wah” and growling effects.

Who carries the melody?

An arrangement distributes musical ideas among instruments. Timbre is tone color: two instruments can play the same pitches and still sound different. Compare a melody alone with a melody supported by a lower answering voice.

Listen for Evidence

Teacher: choose an authorized recording through your school’s library or the linked collections. Play a short passage twice. First identify a feature; then note a timestamp and describe what you actually hear. A different performance may make different choices.

  • Mood Indigo — listen for blended instrumental colors.
  • Take the “A” Train — credit Billy Strayhorn; listen for ensemble passages and solo space.
  • C Jam Blues — notice how a small melodic idea supports varied performances.

Compare the recording with the experiment: what can the simple model show, and what does it leave out? Recordings, compositions, and arrangements have separate rights; this lesson does not reproduce their audio or scores.

Play it yourself: open Take the “A” Train (Strayhorn) and Satin Doll (Ellington and Strayhorn) in the Fake Book. Choose an instrument for the melody, then switch it: does the tune change character when a different “voice” carries it?

Ellington’s Key Contributions

🎼
A Huge Body of Work

He wrote well over a thousand compositions: dance tunes, popular songs, film scores, suites and sacred music.

🎨
Writing for Individual Players

He built pieces around the sounds of particular musicians, so his band sounded like no other.

🎹
The Orchestra as His Instrument

Billy Strayhorn said that Ellington played the piano, but his real instrument was his orchestra.

📜
Beyond the Three-Minute Record

Works such as Black, Brown and Beige (1943) and his later suites stretched jazz into long concert forms.

🤝
A Lifelong Collaboration

His nearly thirty-year partnership with Billy Strayhorn produced some of the most admired music in jazz.

⛪
Sacred Concerts

From 1965 he wrote three concerts of religious music, which he called the most important things he had ever done.

Ellington’s Legacy Today

Ellington’s songs, including Sophisticated Lady, In a Sentimental Mood and It Don’t Mean a Thing (If It Ain’t Got That Swing), are jazz standards that musicians still play every night somewhere in the world. In 1999, a hundred years after his birth, he received a special Pulitzer Prize citation.

High school bands across North America compete in Jazz at Lincoln Center’s Essentially Ellington program, playing his original arrangements. Washington, D.C., named its Duke Ellington School of the Arts after him, and he appears on the District of Columbia’s 2009 quarter.

Jazz grew through African American musical communities and exchange among many traditions. Morton, Armstrong, Ellington and Parker show different musical choices; their careers do not form a simple ladder in which one style replaces another.
