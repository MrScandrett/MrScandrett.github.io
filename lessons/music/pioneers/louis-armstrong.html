Louis Armstrong

Trumpeter & Singer Jazz, 1901–1971 Hot Five & Hot Seven “Ambassador Satch”

Louis Armstrong (1901–1971) grew up poor in New Orleans, learned the cornet in a home for boys, and became the musician who turned jazz into a soloist’s art. His trumpet and his gravelly voice bent rhythm the same way, leaning a little behind or ahead of the beat so that a melody seemed to speak. In fifty years he went from riverboats and Chicago dance halls to number-one records and tours around the world.

Who Was Louis Armstrong?

Louis Armstrong was born in New Orleans on August 4, 1901, and grew up in deep poverty in a rough neighborhood known as the Battlefield. He sang on street corners for coins and worked from a young age, selling coal and hauling junk. After he fired a pistol into the air on New Year’s Eve 1912, he was sent to the Colored Waif’s Home for Boys. There he joined the band and learned to play the cornet properly.

Back on the streets, he found a mentor in the cornetist Joe “King” Oliver. When Oliver moved north in 1918, Armstrong took his place in Kid Ory’s band, and he also played on Mississippi riverboats, where he learned to read music. In 1922 Oliver sent for him to play second cornet in his Creole Jazz Band in Chicago. There Armstrong met the pianist Lil Hardin, who became his wife in 1924 and pushed him to step out on his own.

After a year in New York with Fletcher Henderson’s orchestra, he returned to Chicago, and from 1925 to 1928 he made the Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings. Solos such as the opening of West End Blues (1928) showed that one improviser could carry a whole performance. On Heebie Jeebies (1926) he sang wordless syllables and helped make scat singing popular.

He became one of the most famous entertainers in the world: in films, on Broadway, and from 1947 at the head of a small band called the All Stars. Tours abroad, some sponsored by the U.S. government, earned him the nickname “Ambassador Satch.” He lived through segregation and often played for audiences he was not allowed to sit among; in 1957 he publicly condemned President Eisenhower’s slow response when Black students were blocked from Central High School in Little Rock. In 1964 his Hello, Dolly! knocked the Beatles off the top of the American charts. He died at his home in Queens, New York, on July 6, 1971.

Swing and Phrasing: Timing That Speaks

Every piece of music has a beat, the steady pulse you tap your foot to. Smaller subdivisions sit between the beats. In much written music, two eighth notes split a beat into equal halves. In swing, players usually make the first note of each pair longer and the second shorter, so the rhythm rocks: long–short, long–short.

Phrasing is how a musician shapes a melody in time: where it starts, which notes it leans on, where it breathes. Armstrong often placed notes slightly behind or ahead of the beat while the band kept steady time underneath. The pulse never changed, but the melody seemed to talk over it. He phrased the same way whether he played trumpet or sang, which is why his singing and his playing sound like the same person.

Swing
Straight eighths: 1 & 2 & — each beat split into equal halves
Swung eighths: 1–a 2–a — long then short, often close to 2 : 1
Phrasing: the beat stays put while the melody leans, waits and catches up

The 2 : 1 ratio is a classroom model. Real swing changes with tempo and player: fast tunes swing almost straight, slow ones more deeply.

What is scat singing?
Improvising wordless syllables (“doo-bee-doo-bah”) as if the voice were a horn. Armstrong did not invent it, but his 1926 record Heebie Jeebies made it widely popular. He later said he dropped his lyric sheet during the session and had to make up sounds. Historians are not sure that is exactly what happened, but the record changed how jazz singers worked.
Cornet or trumpet?
Armstrong started on the cornet, a shorter and mellower cousin of the trumpet, and switched to trumpet in the mid-1920s. Both play the same notes; the trumpet’s brighter tone carried better in front of a big band.
What is call and response?
One musician states a phrase and another answers it, like a conversation. That is call and response. On St. Louis Blues (in Listen, below) Bessie Smith sings a line and Armstrong’s cornet answers in the gap she leaves.

How can timing make a melody speak?

Phrasing shapes the timing, emphasis, and space within a melody. Swing often gives paired subdivisions unequal lengths, but the balance varies with tempo and performer. Compare equal subdivisions with a simplified long–short pattern while the beat remains steady.

Explain the comparison

The beat and pitches stay fixed; the spacing between notes changes. This 2:1 long–short model is a teaching approximation, not a universal swing rule or an imitation of Armstrong. Real phrasing also involves breath, accents, tone, and subtle timing.

Hear Armstrong’s Early Records

These two records were made in 1923 and 1925, so they are now in the public domain in the United States. Both come from before the Hot Five, when Armstrong was still a sideman in other musicians’ bands. Listen for how he fills the spaces someone else leaves.

Now Playing St. Louis Blues (1925) Bessie Smith, vocal · Louis Armstrong, cornet · Fred Longshaw, reed organ
Columbia recording, 1925 · Public domain in the U.S., via Wikimedia Commons Source details ↗
  • In St. Louis Blues, notice when Armstrong plays: mostly in the gaps after Bessie Smith finishes a line. That is call and response.
  • His cornet answers bend and slide like a voice. Compare them with the steady, even chords of the reed organ.
  • In Dippermouth Blues, the famous muted solo is King Oliver’s, not Armstrong’s. Listen for the second cornet in the ensemble: the 21-year-old student playing beside his teacher.

Armstrong’s Key Contributions

🎺
The Jazz Soloist

His Hot Five and Hot Seven records (1925–28) made the improvised solo, not only the ensemble, the heart of a jazz performance.

🎤
Scat Singing

Heebie Jeebies (1926) made wordless, horn-like singing popular, and jazz singers have scatted ever since.

🎶
Swing Phrasing

His relaxed timing changed how both instrumentalists and singers shaped a melody. Billie Holiday named him as one of her models.

⭐
Raising the Bar on Trumpet

He played higher, with a bigger tone and more daring rhythm than most trumpeters of his day, and later players measured themselves against him.

🌍
“Ambassador Satch”

Tours in the 1950s and ’60s, some sponsored by the U.S. government, carried his music to audiences across Africa, Europe, Asia and South America.

📻
Hits Across Five Decades

From his 1920s records to Hello, Dolly! (1964) and What a Wonderful World (1967), he kept reaching new listeners.

Armstrong’s Legacy Today

Nearly every jazz musician since the 1920s has learned from Armstrong, directly or through someone he influenced. Trumpeters study his solos note by note, and singers such as Billie Holiday took up his way of shaping a line; Ella Fitzgerald recorded three albums with him. Duke Ellington called him “the epitome of jazz.”

His house in Corona, Queens, is now the Louis Armstrong House Museum, which keeps his trumpets, his home tape recordings and the collages he made from photographs and clippings. What a Wonderful World, recorded in 1967, is one of the best-known recordings of the twentieth century.

Jazz grew through African American musical communities and exchange among many traditions. Morton, Armstrong, Ellington and Parker show different musical choices; their careers do not form a simple ladder in which one style replaces another.
