Who carries the melody?

An arrangement distributes musical ideas among instruments. Timbre is tone color: two instruments can play the same pitches and still sound different. Compare a melody alone with a melody supported by a lower answering voice.

Predict: Will adding a second voice change the melody or mainly change the texture?

    Explain the comparison

    Texture changes; the lead melody stays the same. The low answer occupies the gaps. Real orchestration involves individual musicians, articulation, dynamics, and instrument combinations; these electronic tones demonstrate only the placement of parts.

    Listen for evidence

    Teacher: choose an authorized recording through your school’s library or the linked collections. Play a short passage twice. First identify a feature; then note a timestamp and describe what you actually hear. A different performance may make different choices.

    • Mood Indigo — listen for blended instrumental colors.
    • Take the “A” Train — credit Billy Strayhorn; listen for ensemble passages and solo space.
    • C Jam Blues — notice how a small melodic idea supports varied performances.

    Compare the recording with the experiment: what can the simple model show, and what does it leave out? Recordings, compositions, and arrangements have separate rights; this lesson does not reproduce their audio or scores.

    Who was Duke Ellington?

    Edward Kennedy Ellington was born in Washington, D.C. He became a pianist, composer, and orchestra leader whose career lasted more than fifty years. His orchestra rose to national attention through broadcasts from Harlem’s Cotton Club in the late 1920s. The individual sounds of his musicians helped shape his arrangements.

    Billy Strayhorn became a close composing and arranging partner. Take the “A” Train was composed by Strayhorn, although it became a signature of the Ellington orchestra. Jazz history includes collaborators, not just the name on the bandstand.

    Jazz grew through African American musical communities and exchange among many traditions. These three musicians illuminate different musical choices; their careers do not form a simple ladder in which one style replaces another.

    Historical landmarks

    1. 1899 · Born in Washington, D.C.
    2. Late 1920s · Cotton Club broadcasts expand the orchestra’s audience.
    3. 1939 · Strayhorn joins the orchestra’s creative work.
    4. 1974 · Ellington dies; his music continues to be performed.

    Create, compare, reflect

    1. Keep a steady pulse and clap or sing a four-beat original idea.
    2. Change one feature explored above. Have a partner identify the change without seeing your plan.
    3. Explain the evidence: which notes, spaces, entrances, or accents did your partner notice?

    Grades 6–8: describe one audible difference using everyday words. Grades 9–12: connect the difference to texture, chord tones, or rhythmic subdivision, and explain the model’s limits.
