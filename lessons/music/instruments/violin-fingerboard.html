The Violin Grades 6–12 · 3–5 class periods

Hold it. Tune it.
Think in fifths.

Before the first note there is a whole craft: knowing the parts, holding the violin and bow without tension, getting the bow and bridge ready, and tuning four strings by ear. Then you learn to see the fingerboard the way violinists do.

The 15-minute violin ritual

Use this short route with a real violin or the fingerboard explorer below.

  1. 2 minPrepareBalance the violin; release jaw, thumb, and shoulders.
  2. 3 minSoundUse open strings; aim for a straight, steady bow.
  3. 4 minPitch + rhythmHear, sing, place, then check one short pattern.
  4. 4 minMake musicShape one phrase with bow direction and dynamics.
  5. 2 minReflectName one centered note and one next target.

Advance rule: repeat only while the body stays free. Accuracy and tone come before speed or vibrato.

01

Parts

Meet the instrument

A violin is a hollow wooden box that a bow makes sing.

The bow hair grabs the string and lets go hundreds of times a second. The string pushes on the bridge, the bridge rocks the thin spruce top, and the whole box turns that motion into sound. Every part has a job in that chain. Choose a part to see what it does.

Two important parts are hidden inside. The soundpost is a thin spruce dowel standing between the top and back under the E-string foot of the bridge; it is held only by pressure. The bass bar is a strip of wood glued under the top beneath the G-string foot. Together they let the top vibrate strongly without collapsing under about 25 kilograms of string tension.

02

Hold

Balance, not grip

Hold the violin with your body and the bow with a soft hand.

Tension is the enemy of good sound. If any part of you is squeezing, the sound gets thin and your hand gets tired. The goal of both holds is balance: gravity and the weight of your arm do the work.

The violin

  1. Rest position. Tuck the violin under your right arm, scroll forward, strings facing out. This is where it waits between pieces.
  2. Lift it in. Hold the lower bout with your right hand and set the violin on your left collarbone, the back on your shoulder or shoulder rest.
  3. Let your head drop. Turn your head a little left and let the jaw settle onto the chinrest. Don't clamp; the weight of your head is enough.
  4. Point the scroll. The scroll points out to your left and slightly forward, about level with the floor, so the bow has a flat road to travel on.
  5. Left hand last. The neck rests between the thumb and the base of the first finger. Straight wrist, relaxed thumb, elbow under the violin.

Test: let go with your left hand. If the violin stays up for a few seconds, you are holding it with your body, not your hand.

The bow

  1. Make a bunny. Touch the tip of your bent thumb to the middle two fingers. Index and pinky stick up like ears.
  2. Thumb in the corner. Put the bent thumb tip on the stick where it meets the frog. The thumb stays bent, never locked straight.
  3. Drape the middle fingers. The middle and ring fingers hang over the stick opposite the thumb, touching the frog.
  4. Index finger leans in. The index finger touches the stick near its middle joint. It is how you press for a louder sound.
  5. Pinky on top. The curved pinky tip sits on top of the stick. It balances the bow so the tip does not drop.

Test: practise the hold on a pencil first. Your fingers should be curved and soft, with no white knuckles.

Common problems and quick fixes
Bow thumb locked straight
Bend it back into the corner of the frog. A straight thumb makes the whole hand stiff.
Pinky flat on the stick
Stand it up on its tip. A flat pinky cannot balance the bow.
Left wrist collapsed under the neck
Straighten the wrist so the palm is not touching the neck. The fingers need room to drop onto the strings from above.
Shoulders creeping up
Breathe out and drop both shoulders. Check this every time you stop.
Scroll pointing at the floor
Bring the scroll up to level. A drooping violin makes the bow slide toward the fingerboard.
03

Bow ready

Before every practice

Tighten the hair, rosin it, and loosen it when you finish.

Bow hair is stored loose so the stick keeps its curve. Before you play, you tighten it with the screw on the end of the frog, then give it rosin so it can grip the string. Drag the slider to tighten this bow and watch the gap between the hair and the stick.

1 · Tighten

Turn the screw clockwise (righty-tighty) a few turns. Stop when the gap between the hair and the stick at its narrowest point, near the middle, is about the width of a pencil. The stick should still curve slightly toward the hair.

2 · Rosin

Hold the rosin still and draw the whole length of the hair across it, three to five slow strokes. New hair needs more. Too much leaves white dust and a scratchy sound. Never touch the hair with your fingers; skin oil stops rosin from sticking.

3 · Loosen and wipe

When you finish, loosen the screw until the hair hangs slack, then wipe the rosin dust off the strings and the top with a soft dry cloth. A bow left tight for days slowly loses its curve and its spring.

04

Bridge

Check it every week

The bridge is not glued. String pressure is all that holds it up.

Every time you tune up, the strings slide over the bridge toward the scroll and drag its top with them. Little by little the bridge leans toward the fingerboard. Left alone, it can warp or snap flat onto the top. Drag the slider to lean this bridge, then bring it back to standing straight.

What a well-set bridge looks like

  • Seen from the side, the back of the bridge (the side facing the tailpiece) stands straight up from the top, at a right angle.
  • Both feet sit flat on the top, with no gap under either edge.
  • It stands between the little notches cut into the inner edges of the f-holes.
  • The high side is under the G string and the low side under the E string.
  • It is centered under the fingerboard, so the strings are evenly spaced.

Straightening a leaning bridge

  1. Rest the violin on your lap, scroll pointing away from you.
  2. Hold the top of the bridge with both thumbs and first fingers.
  3. Gently ease the top back toward the tailpiece a tiny bit at a time, checking from the side after each nudge.
  4. Never loosen all four strings at once. The bridge and the soundpost inside can fall.

Stop and ask your teacher if the bridge has fallen, is cracked or warped, or if something rattles inside the violin. A loose soundpost has to be set by a teacher or a luthier, with a special tool.

05

Tune

Use your ears

Four strings, each a perfect fifth from the next.

From lowest to highest the strings are G D A E. Neighbouring strings are a perfect fifth apart, so when two neighbours are in tune and played together they sound smooth and ringing. When they are out of tune you hear a wobble called beating. Violinists tune the A first, to a tuning note of A = 440 Hz, then tune each other string against its in-tune neighbour.

Hear each open string

Hear neighbours together

Pegs and fine tuners

Pegs make big changes. Turn the peg while pushing it gently into the pegbox so it holds. The G and D pegs are on the left side of the pegbox, A and E on the right.

Fine tuners on the tailpiece make small changes. Turn clockwise to raise the pitch, counter-clockwise to lower it. Most violins have one on the E string, and student violins often have four.

Always come up to the note from below. If you overshoot, go a little under and come up again. A string tuned down to pitch tends to slip flat.

The order

  1. A to the tuning note (A = 440 Hz).
  2. D: play D and A together and adjust D until the wobble stops.
  3. G: play G and D together and adjust G.
  4. E: play A and E together and adjust E, usually with its fine tuner.

Try it: tune the D string

This D string is out of tune. Play it with the A string, listen for the wobble, and use the peg for big moves and the fine tuner for small ones until the two strings ring together.

06

Think in violin

See the fingerboard like a violinist

No frets, no keys: you find every note by ear and by pattern.

A pianist sees each note as its own key. A violinist sees a few patterns and repeats them everywhere. These four ideas are how violinists think:

Every string is a fifth

Put a finger down and move it to the same spot on the next string up: the note is a perfect fifth higher. The same finger shape works on all four strings, just starting from a different note.

Space or touch

Fingers that are a whole step apart have a gap between them. Fingers a half step apart touch. A finger pattern is just the order of gaps and touches, like “2 and 3 touch” for a major scale on D.

Hear it first

Sing or imagine the note before you place the finger. Your ear guides the finger to the right spot, and you correct a note you can hear is off. This is called intonation.

Two hands, two jobs

The left hand chooses the pitch. The right hand, with the bow, makes the sound: loud or soft, smooth or short. The bow is the violin’s breath.

Now try it on the explorer. Drag along a string to hear the pitch slide, then use Patterns to light up a scale and see the gaps and touches.

Touch a string. Find your note.

How to use the fingerboard

1Choose a string. Tap its open-string label, or drag along the string to hear pitch change continuously.

2Find the center. Drag toward the body to raise pitch. Arrow keys move by semitone; Shift + arrows move a fifth. Release snaps to a note.

🖐️Read the finger numbers

0 = open string; 1 = index, 2 = middle, 3 = ring, 4 = little finger. L/H mean a lower/higher placement than the reference pattern.

On D: D4 (0) → E4 (1) → F♯4 (2) → G4 (3) → A4 (4). E–F♯ is a whole step; F♯–G is a half step, so fingers 2 and 3 sit closer together.

Glossary

🎻Bowing (right hand)
ArcoPlayed with the bow — the default, unless a piece says otherwise.
Pizzicato (pizz.)Plucked with a finger instead of bowed, until "arco" returns.
DétachéSmooth, separate bow strokes — one note per bow direction.
LegatoNotes connected smoothly. A slur often asks a string player to connect several notes in one bow.
StaccatoShort, clearly separated notes. They may use separate bows or several stopped strokes in one bow.
SpiccatoA bouncing bow stroke where the bow leaves the string.
TremoloRapidly repeated bow strokes on one note; notation may specify a measured rhythm or an unmeasured shimmer.
Sul ponticello / sul tastoBowing near the bridge for a bright, glassy color, or over the fingerboard for a soft, flute-like color.

PositionWhere the whole left hand sits on the neck.
ShiftingSliding the hand from one position to another.
VibratoA small, controlled pitch oscillation from rocking the finger.
Glissando (gliss.)An audible slide between two pitches — try it on the fingerboard.
Double stopTwo strings stopped and bowed together, sounding two notes.
HarmonicA bell-like overtone from touching a string lightly, not pressing.
IntonationHow accurately a stopped note matches its true pitch.
Open stringPlayed with no finger down; a tuning reference for nearby notes.
🛠️Instrument & setup
BridgeThe upright piece strings pass over, driving the body's vibration.
SoundpostA fitted dowel inside the body near the treble foot of the bridge; it couples the top and back plates and shapes resonance.
F-holesThe f-shaped openings that help the top plate flex and help set the body's air resonance.
Fine tunersSmall tailpiece screws for precise pitch adjustment.
RosinResin rubbed on the bow hair so it grips the string.
Chin/shoulder restSupports that balance the violin, freeing the left hand.

07

Check

Exit ticket

Eight quick questions.

1. What does the bridge do?
2. In a good bow hold, the thumb is…
3. How tight should the bow hair be?
4. Why loosen the bow after playing?
5. A well-set bridge, seen from the side, has its back face…
6. Which string do violinists usually tune first?
7. What interval apart are neighbouring open strings?
8. Two fingers a half step apart on the same string…

Sources and scope

Holds and setup steps describe common beginner teaching (a Franco-Belgian style bow hold, tuning A to 440 Hz first). Teachers vary in details such as thumb placement and shoulder rests; follow your teacher where they differ. Pitch in the explorer is synthesized in equal temperament.

Learning goals

  • Name the main parts of the violin and bow and what each does
  • Hold the violin and bow in balance, without squeezing
  • Tighten, rosin and loosen a bow; check that the bridge stands straight
  • Tune the strings in fifths, starting from A
  • Find notes on the fingerboard by pattern and by ear

Materials

  • This browser tab, with sound on
  • A violin, bow, rosin and soft cloth, if you have them
  • A pencil for practising the bow hold

Extension

Use the tuning game until you can hear the wobble stop, then try tuning a real violin with a teacher watching.

Key vocabulary

Bridge
The carved maple piece the strings pass over; it carries their vibration into the top.
Soundpost
A spruce dowel inside the body, under the E-string foot of the bridge.
Frog
The block at the end of the bow that holds the hair and is moved by the screw.
Rosin
Hardened tree resin rubbed on the bow hair so it grips the string.
Fine tuner
A small screw lever on the tailpiece for small pitch changes.
Perfect fifth
The interval between neighbouring open strings: seven half steps.
Intonation
How accurately a played note matches its intended pitch.
