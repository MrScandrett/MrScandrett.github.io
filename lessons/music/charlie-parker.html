How does a line find its destination?

A chord tone belongs to the underlying chord. An approach note leads toward a destination pitch. Listen to a simple rising line, then a version with extra approach notes. Both end on the same pitch; the journey changes.

Predict: Will extra notes change the final destination or the route taken to reach it?

    Listen for evidence

    Teacher: choose an authorized recording through your school’s library or the linked collections. Play a short passage twice. First identify a feature; then note a timestamp and describe what you actually hear. A different performance may make different choices.

    • Now’s the Time — follow the blues form and separate the theme from improvisation.
    • Ko-Ko — listen for rapid lines, accents, and the interaction of soloist and rhythm section.
    • Ornithology — compare the composed theme with a solo; co-written with Benny Harris.

    Compare the recording with the experiment: what can the simple model show, and what does it leave out? Recordings, compositions, and arrangements have separate rights; this lesson does not reproduce their audio or scores.

    Who was Charlie Parker?

    Charlie “Bird” Parker was born in Kansas City, Kansas, and developed as a musician in the Kansas City jazz scene. As an alto saxophonist and composer, he became a central figure in bebop during the 1940s. His work with musicians including Dizzy Gillespie helped expand jazz’s rhythmic and harmonic possibilities.

    Bebop improvisers used existing song forms as settings for new melodic lines, unexpected accents, and rich harmony. Speed alone does not define bebop. Parker’s phrasing and choice of destination notes matter as much as the number of notes he plays.

    Jazz grew through African American musical communities and exchange among many traditions. These three musicians illuminate different musical choices; their careers do not form a simple ladder in which one style replaces another.

    Historical landmarks

    1. 1920 · Born in Kansas City, Kansas.
    2. 1930s · Develops in the Kansas City jazz scene.

    4. 1955 · Dies in New York at age 34.

    Create, compare, reflect

    1. Keep a steady pulse and clap or sing a four-beat original idea.
    2. Change one feature explored above. Have a partner identify the change without seeing your plan.
    3. Explain the evidence: which notes, spaces, entrances, or accents did your partner notice?

    Grades 6–8: describe one audible difference using everyday words. Grades 9–12: connect the difference to texture, chord tones, or rhythmic subdivision, and explain the model’s limits.
