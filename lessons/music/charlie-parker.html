Charlie Parker

Alto Saxophonist & Composer Jazz, 1920–1955 Co-creator of Bebop “Bird”

Charlie Parker (1920–1955), nicknamed “Bird,” played the alto saxophone with a speed, precision and harmonic imagination that changed jazz in a few short years. With Dizzy Gillespie and other young musicians in 1940s New York, he helped create bebop: fast, complex jazz made for listening rather than dancing. He lived only 34 years, but nearly every jazz musician since has studied his solos.

Who Was Charlie Parker?

Charles Parker Jr. was born on August 29, 1920, in Kansas City, Kansas, and grew up across the river in Kansas City, Missouri. In the 1930s that city was full of nightclubs where bands such as Count Basie’s played the blues late into the night. Parker took up the alto saxophone as a boy, left school in his mid-teens and began playing professionally.

His start was rough. At a jam session around 1936 he lost his place in the harmony in the middle of a solo, and the drummer Jo Jones threw a cymbal at his feet to stop him. Parker went away and practiced for hours a day: he learned the blues and popular songs in every key and memorized saxophonist Lester Young’s solos from records. By the late 1930s he was playing in Jay McShann’s band, which recorded him in 1940 and 1941.

In New York he met the trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie. In late-night jam sessions at clubs such as Minton’s Playhouse in Harlem, they and other young musicians developed bebop. In 1945 Parker and Gillespie made a series of recordings together, and that November Parker recorded Now’s the Time and Ko-Ko under his own name. Bebop surprised many listeners: it was fast, full of new harmonies, and meant for close listening.

Parker also struggled with drug and alcohol addiction for most of his adult life. In 1946, in Los Angeles, his health collapsed, and he spent about six months in Camarillo State Hospital. He recovered enough to make some of his finest records, to record with a string orchestra in 1949 and to play a famous concert at Toronto’s Massey Hall in 1953, but his health kept failing. He died in New York on March 12, 1955, at the age of 34. A New York jazz club named Birdland, after him, had opened in 1949.

Bebop: Chord Tones and Approach Notes

Jazz musicians improvise over a chord progression, a repeating series of chords. Every chord has chord tones: a C-major chord is built from C, E and G. Notes on those pitches sound stable, like a place to land. Other notes create tension that wants to move to a chord tone.

Bebop players treated chord tones as destinations and filled the space before them with routes: scale steps, leaps and approach notes. An approach note is usually a half step above or below the target, such as B leading up to C. Parker later recalled that around 1939 he found he could build melodies from the higher notes of each chord (the 9th, 11th and 13th), which gave his lines their fresh, complex sound.

Bebop musicians also wrote new melodies over the chords of familiar songs. Parker’s Ko-Ko is built on the chords of the swing-era hit Cherokee, and Ornithology, written with trumpeter Benny Harris, on those of How High the Moon. Musicians call a piece like that a contrafact.

Route
Chord tones (C major): C · E · G — the landing places
Approach note: one half step away, leading in (B → C)
Same destination, different route = a new melodic line

Speed alone does not make bebop. What matters is where each phrase lands and how it gets there.

Why was he called “Bird”?
Stories differ. The most common is that, on the road as a young musician, he asked the driver to stop for a chicken (a “yardbird”) their car had hit. Musicians shortened Yardbird to Bird, and the name stuck: Ornithology (the study of birds) and the Birdland club both play on it.
Did bebop replace swing?
No. Big bands kept playing, and many bebop musicians had learned their craft in them. Bebop was one new direction among several, and it grew out of swing and the blues.

How does a line find its destination?

A chord tone belongs to the underlying chord. An approach note leads toward a destination pitch. Listen to a simple rising line, then a version with extra approach notes. Both end on the same pitch; the journey changes.

Parker’s Key Contributions

🐦
Co-creating Bebop

With Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, Kenny Clarke and others, he helped shape a new style of jazz built for listening.

🎷
A New Voice for the Alto Sax

His bright tone, speed and precision made the alto saxophone a leading solo instrument. Alto players still begin by studying him.

🎼
New Tunes on Old Chords

Bebop melodies such as Ko-Ko and Ornithology borrowed the chords of popular songs, a practice jazz musicians still use.

🎵
Higher Chord Tones

Building lines from the 9ths, 11ths and 13ths of chords widened the harmonic vocabulary of every improviser after him.

📚
Blues for Jam Sessions

He wrote many blues tunes, such as Now’s the Time and Billie’s Bounce, that remain standard material at jam sessions.

🎻
Jazz with Strings

His 1949 recordings with a string orchestra showed that a bebop soloist could also shine in a lush, orchestral setting.

Parker’s Legacy Today

Bebop became the shared language of modern jazz. Students still learn Parker’s solos from transcriptions, collected in books such as the Charlie Parker Omnibook, and practice them in every key, the way he once learned Lester Young’s solos.

After he died, fans wrote “Bird Lives” on walls around New York. The club named after him, Birdland, still operates in Manhattan, and Kansas City has a memorial to him in its historic jazz district at 18th and Vine. His life is also a sobering story: addiction cut short one of the most gifted musicians of the century.

Jazz grew through African American musical communities and exchange among many traditions. Morton, Armstrong, Ellington and Parker show different musical choices; their careers do not form a simple ladder in which one style replaces another.
