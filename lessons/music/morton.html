Jelly Roll Morton

Composer & Pianistc. 1890–1941

How can written parts and improvisation work together? Explore Jelly Roll Morton’s piano music, the New Orleans traditions he drew on, and the carefully arranged ensemble music of his Red Hot Peppers.

Arrangement makes room for conversation

An arrangement decides who plays, when they enter, and how musical ideas fit together. Improvisation adds decisions made during performance. Early jazz can include both: a planned introduction, a solo, a break, and a return of the ensemble.

Predict: Can three different parts sound organized? One student taps a steady pulse, another repeats a short rhythm, and a third invents a two-beat answer. Test: give the third student a silent space after every fourth bar. Explain: does that planned space make the improvised response easier to hear?

Morton described a “Spanish tinge” in jazz: rhythmic influences associated with Caribbean and Latin dance traditions. Explore the accompaniment of New Orleans Blues, and distinguish that rhythmic idea from the melody above it. A piano score can suggest several ensemble roles at once.

Biography and timeline

Who was Jelly Roll Morton?

Ferdinand Joseph LaMothe, known professionally as Jelly Roll Morton, grew up in New Orleans in a Louisiana Creole community. He is generally dated to 1890, although accounts of his birth differ. As a young pianist he absorbed ragtime, blues, dance music, and Caribbean-influenced rhythms, then traveled widely as a performer.

His Original Jelly Roll Blues appeared in print in 1915, among the earliest published jazz compositions. During the 1920s he worked in Chicago and recorded piano solos and ensemble music. The Red Hot Peppers sessions of 1926, including Black Bottom Stomp, show how he combined arranged passages, contrasting textures, and individual instrumental voices.

Morton was a composer and arranger as well as a pianist. His music helps us hear jazz as organized ensemble work: a musician can improvise within a structure that somebody has planned. Printed piano scores preserve one version of a work, but they do not capture every detail of the recorded band arrangement.

In 1938 Alan Lomax recorded Morton’s playing and recollections at the Library of Congress. These interviews are valuable primary sources, but Morton’s claim to have invented jazz should be examined critically. Jazz developed through many musicians and communities. Morton died in Los Angeles on July 10, 1941.

Practice and reflection

  1. Choose one score. Identify its meter, key signature, and one repeat or contrasting section.
  2. Perform a short phrase twice: first as written, then with one deliberate rhythmic change. Keep the pulse steady. Explain what changed.
  3. How can an arrangement help improvisers listen to each other? Describe one planned entrance, break, or repeated section you would use.
Did Morton invent jazz by himself?
No. He was an influential composer, pianist, and arranger, but jazz developed through many musicians and communities. His own claim is evidence of his viewpoint, not proof of sole invention.

Exit ticket: name one observation you can support with the score and one historical claim you can support with a source.
