Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

Composer & Conductor Romantic, 1840–1893 Ballets & Symphonies Master of Melody

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky (1840–1893) began his working life as a government clerk and became the most internationally famous Russian composer of his century. His ballets Swan Lake, The Sleeping Beauty and The Nutcracker are among the most performed in the world, and he had a special gift for melody and for the colours of the orchestra.

Who Was Tchaikovsky?

Tchaikovsky was born on May 7, 1840, in Votkinsk, a mining town in the Ural region of Russia, where his father managed the ironworks. He had piano lessons as a child and was deeply sensitive to music, but his parents did not see music as a respectable career. He studied law in St. Petersburg and in 1859 became a clerk at the Ministry of Justice.

In 1862 he enrolled in the newly founded St. Petersburg Conservatory — among its very first students — and studied with its founder, Anton Rubinstein. In 1866 he moved to Moscow to teach harmony at the new Moscow Conservatory. Over the next decade he wrote symphonies, the First Piano Concerto and the ballet Swan Lake (1877).

From 1877 a wealthy widow, Nadezhda von Meck, gave him a yearly allowance that let him quit teaching and compose full time. They wrote more than a thousand letters to each other but agreed never to meet. Tchaikovsky became a celebrated conductor of his own music; in 1891 he travelled to the United States and conducted at the opening festival of Carnegie Hall in New York. In 1878 he wrote a set of 24 short piano pieces for children, Album for the Young, dedicated to his nephew Vladimir Davydov.

His last years brought The Sleeping Beauty (1890), The Nutcracker (1892) and an honorary doctorate from Cambridge University in June 1893. He conducted the premiere of his Sixth Symphony, the “Pathétique,” in October 1893 and died in St. Petersburg nine days later, on November 6, aged 53 — officially of cholera.

Timbre and the Secret Celesta

Two instruments can play the same pitch and still sound completely different. That difference is timbre: the mix of overtones each instrument produces, plus how the sound starts and fades. A violin’s bowed string produces a steady sound rich in overtones; a struck metal bar rings with a bright, bell-like set of overtones that dies away.

In 1891 Tchaikovsky heard a new instrument in Paris, the celesta, invented by Auguste Mustel. It looks like a small piano, but its hammers strike steel bars mounted over wooden resonators, giving a sparkling, bell-like tone. He wrote it into “Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy” in The Nutcracker — and asked his publisher to keep it secret so that rival Russian composers would not use it first.

The score player above can change sounds. Its “Celesta-ish” setting uses frequency modulation to imitate a bell-like tone; it is only an approximation, but it shows how the same notes take on a new character.

Timbre
Overtones: which higher frequencies sound along with the main pitch, and how strongly
Attack: how quickly the sound starts — a hammer is instant, a bow can swell
Decay: how the sound fades — a struck bar rings and dies, a bowed string sustains

Same pitch + different overtones and envelope = a different instrument. Orchestration is choosing timbres like a painter chooses colours.

Did the 1812 Overture really use cannons?
Yes — Tchaikovsky wrote cannon shots into the score. It celebrates Russia’s defence against Napoleon’s invasion in 1812. Outdoor performances still use real cannons or fireworks; indoors, a bass drum often stands in.
Why is Album for the Young special?
It was one of the first collections by a major composer written for children to play, inspired by Schumann’s similar album. Its 24 pieces describe a child’s day: games, dolls, songs from other countries, and prayers.
Was The Nutcracker a hit at first?
Its 1892 premiere got mixed reviews. Tchaikovsky’s concert suite from the ballet was popular at once, and the full ballet became a holiday tradition, especially in North America, in the twentieth century.

Tchaikovsky's Legacy Today

Tchaikovsky showed that a Russian composer could write music in the big European forms — symphony, concerto, ballet — with a voice that was both personal and Russian. His ballets lifted ballet music from background accompaniment to great music in its own right.

His melodies have travelled far beyond the concert hall: into films, cartoons, figure skating, and the yearly Nutcracker performances that introduce many children to an orchestra for the first time. The celesta he smuggled home is now a standard orchestral instrument — and its bell-like sound has become shorthand for magic in film music.

Practice Problems

Use the score, the recordings and the biography above.

Easy1. Which of these ballets did Tchaikovsky NOT write?

Hint: The odd one out was written by Stravinsky in 1913.

Easy2. How many flats are in the key signature of Old French Song?

Hint: Count the ♭ signs after the clef: B♭ and E♭.

Medium3. What instrument plays the melody in the Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy?

Hint: Tchaikovsky discovered it in Paris in 1891.

Medium4. What job did Tchaikovsky have before becoming a full-time musician?

Hint: He studied law first.

Medium5. The Nutcracker premiered in 1892. How many years ago was that in 2026?

Hint: 2026 − 1892.

Medium6. In a 2/4 bar, a double-dotted quarter note is worth 1¾ beats. How many beats are left for the sixteenth note after it?

Hint: 2 − 1.75.

Challenge7. Two instruments play the same pitch, but you can tell them apart. What is that difference called?

Hint: It comes from each instrument’s overtones and envelope.

Challenge8. Why did Tchaikovsky want the celesta kept secret?

Hint: He named Rimsky-Korsakov and Glazunov as the rivals.
