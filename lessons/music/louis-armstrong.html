How can timing make a melody speak?

Phrasing shapes the timing, emphasis, and space within a melody. Swing often gives paired subdivisions unequal lengths, but the balance varies with tempo and performer. Compare equal subdivisions with a simplified long–short pattern while the beat remains steady.

    Explain the comparison

    The beat and pitches stay fixed; the spacing between notes changes. This 2:1 long–short model is a teaching approximation, not a universal swing rule or an imitation of Armstrong. Real phrasing also involves breath, accents, tone, and subtle timing.

    Listen for evidence

    Teacher: choose an authorized recording through your school’s library or the linked collections. Play a short passage twice. First identify a feature; then note a timestamp and describe what you actually hear. A different performance may make different choices.

    • West End Blues — listen to the opening trumpet passage and the space between phrases.
    • Heebie Jeebies — listen for scat singing and its rhythmic character.
    • What a Wonderful World — contrast vocal phrasing with the earlier jazz recordings; written by Bob Thiele and George David Weiss.

    Compare the recording with the experiment: what can the simple model show, and what does it leave out? Recordings, compositions, and arrangements have separate rights; this lesson does not reproduce their audio or scores.

    Who was Louis Armstrong?

    Louis Armstrong was born in New Orleans in 1901. Mentored by cornetist Joe “King” Oliver, he joined Oliver’s Chicago band in 1922. Pianist Lil Hardin Armstrong encouraged his independent career. His Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings brought improvised solos and expressive musical storytelling to wide attention.

    Armstrong was also an influential singer, using flexible phrasing and scat singing with wordless syllables. He continued touring with the All Stars and became known internationally. His career took place amid racial segregation; in 1957 he publicly criticized the government’s response to the Little Rock school integration crisis.

    Jazz grew through African American musical communities and exchange among many traditions. These three musicians illuminate different musical choices; their careers do not form a simple ladder in which one style replaces another.

    Create, compare, reflect

    1. Keep a steady pulse and clap or sing a four-beat original idea.
    2. Change one feature explored above. Have a partner identify the change without seeing your plan.
    3. Explain the evidence: which notes, spaces, entrances, or accents did your partner notice?

    Grades 6–8: describe one audible difference using everyday words. Grades 9–12: connect the difference to texture, chord tones, or rhythmic subdivision, and explain the model’s limits.
