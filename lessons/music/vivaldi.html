Antonio Vivaldi

Violinist, Teacher & Composer Baroque, 1678–1741 “The Red Priest” The Four Seasons

Antonio Vivaldi (1678–1741) was a red-haired priest from Venice who became one of the most famous violinists in Europe. He taught music at an orphanage whose all-girl orchestra drew visitors from across the continent, wrote hundreds of concertos for them, and published The Four Seasons — four violin concertos that tell stories about birds, storms, sleepy shepherds and icy roads using nothing but instruments.

Who Was Vivaldi?

Vivaldi was born in Venice on March 4, 1678. His father, Giovanni Battista, was a barber turned professional violinist at St. Mark’s Basilica, and almost certainly taught his son to play. Antonio trained for the priesthood and was ordained in 1703. Because of his red hair he became known as il Prete Rosso, “the Red Priest.” He suffered all his life from a tightness of the chest — probably a form of asthma — and soon stopped saying Mass, devoting himself to music instead.

In 1703 he became violin master at the Ospedale della Pietà, a Venetian home for abandoned and orphaned girls. The Pietà trained its most talented residents as musicians, and its concerts, played by young women, became a famous attraction for tourists. Vivaldi worked there on and off for about thirty years, writing concertos, sacred music and teaching pieces for its performers.

He was enormously productive. About 500 of his concertos survive — around 230 of them for violin — along with dozens of operas, which he also produced as a businessman. In 1725 a publisher in Amsterdam printed his Opus 8, Il cimento dell’armonia e dell’inventione (“The Contest between Harmony and Invention”). Its first four concertos were The Four Seasons, each printed with a sonnet describing the scene, and with labels in the score such as “the barking dog.”

Vivaldi’s popularity faded in Venice in the 1730s. In 1740 he moved to Vienna, perhaps hoping for support from Emperor Charles VI, who soon died. Vivaldi died in Vienna on July 28, 1741, and was buried in a simple grave. His music was then largely forgotten until the twentieth century, when a large collection of his manuscripts was rediscovered and scholars and performers brought him back.

Program Music: Telling a Story Without Words

Program music is instrumental music that describes something outside itself — a scene, a story, a feeling. The Four Seasons is one of the earliest famous examples. Each concerto came with a sonnet, and Vivaldi printed lines of the poem right in the score so players knew what they were painting.

Each fast movement is built on a ritornello (“little return”): a theme the whole orchestra plays at the start and keeps coming back to, with solo episodes in between. In “Spring” the solos are the pictures: three violins trilling like birds, a murmuring brook, a quick thunderstorm, then the birds again.

Notice the sudden switch from loud to soft in the score. Baroque composers often used terraced dynamics — whole phrases at one level, then another — instead of slow crescendos.

R · S · R · S · R
R — Ritornello: the full orchestra plays the main theme (or part of it)
S — Solo episode: the solo violin takes over with new, showier music
Back to R: the theme returns, often in a new key, so listeners always find their way home

Like a chorus between verses: the returning theme holds the movement together while the solos tell the story.

How does a violin imitate a bird?
With very fast trills (two neighbouring notes alternating rapidly) and short high notes. A shorter vibrating string makes a higher pitch, so the player presses the string close to the bridge end of the fingerboard for bird-like high notes.
Who wrote the sonnets?
They were printed with the music in 1725 without an author’s name. Many scholars think Vivaldi wrote them himself.
What is the barking dog?
In the slow movement of “Spring,” the violas play the same two notes over and over, loudly. Vivaldi labelled it “the barking dog” — a goatherd’s dog keeping watch while he sleeps.

Vivaldi's Legacy Today

Vivaldi helped fix the shape of the concerto — fast, slow, fast movements, with a soloist set against an orchestra — that composers used for the next three centuries. J. S. Bach learned from his concertos by copying and arranging them.

For almost two hundred years Vivaldi was little more than a name. Once his manuscripts resurfaced and recordings spread in the twentieth century, The Four Seasons became one of the most recorded pieces of classical music, and you can hear it today in films, adverts and phone hold music. His story is also a reminder of the many young women at the Pietà whose playing made his music famous, though most of their names were never recorded.

Practice Problems

Use the score, the recordings and the biography above.

Easy1. What was Vivaldi’s nickname?

Hint: It came from his hair and his job.

Easy2. How many sharps are in the key signature of “Spring”?

Hint: Count the ♯ signs right after the treble clef.

Medium3. The Four Seasons is four concertos, each with three movements. How many movements is that?

Hint: 4 × 3.

Medium4. In the score, what does the p before bar 4 tell the players to do?

Hint: p stands for piano, Italian for “soft.”

Medium5. What is a ritornello?

Hint: The name means “little return.”

Medium6. The Four Seasons was published in 1725. Vivaldi died in 1741. How many years later was that?

Hint: 1741 − 1725.

Challenge7. A violin string vibrating at 330 Hz is shortened to half its length (same tension). What frequency does it make now?

Hint: Halving the vibrating length doubles the frequency — one octave higher.

Challenge8. Why is The Four Seasons called program music?

Hint: Think about the birds, brook and storm.
