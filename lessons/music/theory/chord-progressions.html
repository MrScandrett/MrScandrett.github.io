GIVE ANOTHER CHORD ITS OWN DOMINANT

Secondary dominants: a temporary spotlight

In C major, G7 is the primary dominant, V7. A secondary dominant is a major triad or dominant seventh that points toward a different major or minor chord. To lead to G, use G’s dominant, D7. Write V7/V and say “five-seven of five.” The slash names the target; it does not name a bass note.

  1. Choose a target: G, the V chord in C.
  2. Find its dominant: a perfect fifth above G is D. Build a major triad or dominant seventh: D–F♯–A–C.
  3. Follow the tendency tones: F♯ rises to G; in a conventional resolution to G major, the seventh C falls to B.
  4. Listen to what follows: D7–G can briefly emphasize G. Returning through G7–C brings the original tonic back.

Why change Dm to D7? Dm contains F natural. D7 replaces it with F♯ and adds C: the raised third creates a leading tone to G. A dominant seventh contains a major third and a minor seventh; Dmaj7 has C♯ and is a different chord.

Three slashes, three meanings: V7/V is an applied function; D7/F♯ puts F♯ in the bass; I⁶ is the traditional figured-bass label for a first-inversion tonic triad. The song explorer labels D7 as II7 by root and quality; this lab adds V7/V when its musical context supports that reading.

A brief emphasis is tonicization. A modulation establishes a new tonic across a passage. There is no fixed number of chords that makes the change official: listen for a cadence, phrase structure, melody, and continued harmony in the destination key.

Keep exploring: what each technique contributes
Pivot chord / common chord
Use a chord shared by both keys, then reinterpret it. Am is vi in C and ii in G; D7–G confirms the new destination.
Relative major and minor
C major and A minor share a key signature. E7 introduces G♯ and a dominant-to-tonic arrival in A minor. A shared key signature does not guarantee a shared tonic.
Direct modulation
Begin a new phrase in another key without a shared pivot. Rhythm, a clear phrase boundary, and repeated harmony can make the new tonic convincing.
Common-tone modulation
Hold or repeat one pitch while the harmony changes around it. C major and A♭ major both contain C. Follow with E♭7–A♭ to support A♭ as tonic.
Sequential motion / dominant chains
Repeat a harmonic pattern at new pitch levels. E7–A7–D7–G7–C is a chain of descending-fifth roots. Each link points onward; the chain can still finish in the original key.
Borrowed chords / modal mixture
Borrow Fm (iv) from C minor within C major. F–Fm–C makes A–A♭–G easy to follow. Borrowing color alone does not establish a new tonic.
Secondary leading-tone chord
F♯–A–C (F♯dim, vii°/V) leads to G. It shares D7’s upper three notes and supplies another way to tonicize V.
Advanced: enharmonic reinterpretation
A fully diminished seventh divides the octave into equal minor thirds. The same sounding pitches can be respelled to imply different destinations. Spelling and resolution explain the new function; merely renaming a chord does not establish a key.

Use the circle of fifths to compare key signatures and dominant relationships. Nearby signatures often offer several common chords.

Compose a route, then defend it

  1. Write a four-bar phrase in C and make its tonic clear.
  2. Choose G or A minor as a destination. Plan a pivot or an applied dominant and mark the altered pitch.
  3. Write a new phrase that cadences in the destination. Label the pivot twice and the confirming dominant once.
  4. Perform it, then ask a partner to name the new home. If they hear only a visit, strengthen the cadence or continue in the new key.

A progression is an ordered sequence of chords. A chord's root becomes a scale-degree number relative to the tonic, or home note. In C major, C–F–G–Am becomes I–IV–V–vi, or 1–4–5–6m. In G major, the same pattern is G–C–D–Em.

Roman numerals: uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished. Added symbols keep the chord quality: G7 = V7, Cmaj7 = Imaj7, Dm7 = ii7. The 7 is a chord member, not seven beats.

Nashville numbers: Arabic numbers name roots; m (also written − by some players) marks minor. A number alone means major in this lesson. Nashville charts also communicate form, stops, pushes and rhythm; this lab supplies a numbered harmony chart with explicit beat counts, not every handwritten session symbol.

Minor keys, slash chords and chromatic changes

This engine uses a tonic-based natural-minor reference for Roman numerals: in A minor, Am–F–G–E7 is i–VI–VII–V7. Some books instead write i–♭VI–♭VII–V7 using major-scale references. State your convention.

Nashville here always measures from the major scale on the selected tonic: in A minor those same chords are 1m–♭6–♭7–5⁷ (shown as 57 in plain text). Another band may call A minor “6m in C”; agree before counting in.

C/E is a C chord with E in the bass. The engine writes I/3 and 1/3, a slash-bass shorthand; traditional Roman analysis calls the first-inversion triad I⁶. Do not confuse the engine's C6 → I6 (an added-sixth chord) with that figured-bass convention. In C major, D7 is labeled II7 here; its resolution to G may justify the functional analysis V7/V. Numbers describe the root; musical context explains its job.

Transcription records musical choices you hear. Arranging decides how a group will perform them. Keep the melody recognizable while you change texture, register, rhythm and form.

  1. Map the form: label intro, A, B, repeats and ending with bar numbers. Mark any pickup before bar 1; do not give it an extra full chord bar.
  2. Choose a playable key: check the singer's highest and lowest notes. Use concert-pitch chords for the band; use the Fake Book's instrument setting for a transposing player's written part.
  3. Assign jobs: melody states the tune, bass establishes roots or indicated slash basses, chords provide harmony, drums clarify the pulse. Leave space around the melody.
  4. Try two textures: compare root-position block chords with smooth voice leading or shells in the Fake Book. An inversion changes the bass or voicing; reharmonization changes the harmony.
  5. Mark any key change: name the old and new tonic, the transition bar, any pivot or applied dominant, and the cadence that confirms the destination. Check altered melody notes against the new harmony.
  6. Shape an arc: start sparsely, add a part at B, reduce texture before the final cadence. Agree on a count-in, repeat count and ending cue.

Rehearse once with melody and bass only, then add harmony and rhythm. Record which change helped listeners follow the phrase. Your workspace stays in this browser when storage is available; download a copy to keep or share it.
