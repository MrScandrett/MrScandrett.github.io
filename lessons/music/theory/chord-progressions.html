A progression is an ordered sequence of chords. A chord's root becomes a scale-degree number relative to the tonic, or home note. In C major, C–F–G–Am becomes I–IV–V–vi, or 1–4–5–6m. In G major, the same pattern is G–C–D–Em.

Roman numerals: uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished. Added symbols keep the chord quality: G7 = V7, Cmaj7 = Imaj7, Dm7 = ii7. The 7 is a chord member, not seven beats.

Nashville numbers: Arabic numbers name roots; m (also written − by some players) marks minor. A number alone means major in this lesson. Nashville charts also communicate form, stops, pushes and rhythm; this lab supplies a numbered harmony chart with explicit beat counts, not every handwritten session symbol.

Minor keys, slash chords and chromatic changes

This engine uses a tonic-based natural-minor reference for Roman numerals: in A minor, Am–F–G–E7 is i–VI–VII–V7. Some books instead write i–♭VI–♭VII–V7 using major-scale references. State your convention.

Nashville here always measures from the major scale on the selected tonic: in A minor those same chords are 1m–♭6–♭7–5⁷ (shown as 57 in plain text). Another band may call A minor “6m in C”; agree before counting in.

C/E is a C chord with E in the bass. The engine writes I/3 and 1/3, a slash-bass shorthand; traditional Roman analysis calls the first-inversion triad I⁶. Do not confuse the engine's C6 → I6 (an added-sixth chord) with that figured-bass convention. In C major, D7 is labeled II7 here; its resolution to G may justify the functional analysis V7/V. Numbers describe the root; musical context explains its job.

Patterns you can recognize

I–IV–V–I

Home → departure → tension → home. In C: C–F–G–C. Look for these chord families in “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star.”

I–V–vi–IV

In C: C–G–Am–F. A repeatable four-chord loop. Ending on IV leaves a different feeling from ending on I.

ii7–V7–Imaj7

In C: Dm7–G7–Cmaj7. Follow F → F → E and C → B → B: small voice movements can connect changing roots.

A cadence marks a phrase ending. V–I often gives a strong arrival; a phrase ending on V sounds open. V–vi can delay the expected return home. These are listening clues, not rules that every style must follow.

Transcription records musical choices you hear. Arranging decides how a group will perform them. Keep the melody recognizable while you change texture, register, rhythm and form.

  1. Map the form: label intro, A, B, repeats and ending with bar numbers. Mark any pickup before bar 1; do not give it an extra full chord bar.
  2. Choose a playable key: check the singer's highest and lowest notes. Use concert-pitch chords for the band; use the Fake Book's instrument setting for a transposing player's written part.
  3. Assign jobs: melody states the tune, bass establishes roots or indicated slash basses, chords provide harmony, drums clarify the pulse. Leave space around the melody.
  4. Try two textures: compare root-position block chords with smooth voice leading or shells in the Fake Book. An inversion changes the bass or voicing; reharmonization changes the harmony.
  5. Shape an arc: start sparsely, add a part at B, reduce texture before the final cadence. Agree on a count-in, repeat count and ending cue.

Rehearse once with melody and bass only, then add harmony and rhythm. Record which change helped listeners follow the phrase. Your workspace stays in this browser when storage is available; download a copy to keep or share it.
