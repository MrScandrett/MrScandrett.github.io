Music theory lab · Grades 7–12 · About two class periods

Drop one note. Open up the whole chord.

A seventh chord has four notes, and there are many ways to spread them out. Drop voicings are a simple recipe jazz arrangers, pianists and guitarists use: stack the chord tightly, count voices from the top, and move one voice down an octave. Same four letters, a wider and more playable sound. You will hear it, see it on a staff, a keyboard and a fretboard, and use it to move between chords with almost no travel.

Start here · a 2-minute listening experiment

Same chord. One note moved.

You will hear Cmaj7 twice. Both versions use exactly the same four letters. In the second one, one of those notes has been moved down an octave. Predict which chord tone it is.

Which chord tone moved?

Listen to A, then B, then A again. Where does the bottom of the chord change?

It was the 5th, G. In close position the notes are C E G B. Counting from the top, B is voice 1 and G is voice 2. Moving voice 2 down an octave gives G C E B. That recipe is called a Drop 2 voicing. The chord is still Cmaj7: nothing was added or removed, so only the spacing changed.

01

Close position

The starting point

Stack the four notes as tightly as you can.

A seventh chord has four chord tones: the root, 3rd, 5th and 7th. A voicing is the way those four notes are arranged: which one is on the bottom, and how far apart they sit. Close position packs all four inside a single octave. Move the bottom note to the top and you get an inversion; a seventh chord has four close-position versions in all.

Cmaj7 in close position

● root● 3rd● 5th● 7thPress any key to hear it.

Problem 1 · Mud down low

The human ear blurs close notes more in the low register. A tight stack sounds clear up high and thick or rumbly down low. Spreading the bottom note out gives each pitch room.

Problem 2 · The rub

Some inversions put two chord tones a half step apart. In Cmaj7's 3rd inversion, B and C sit side by side. That crunch can be a color, but you do not always want it on the bottom.

Problem 3 · Hard on guitar

Neighbouring guitar strings are about a fourth apart. Notes crammed within an octave mean big stretches or awkward string skips. An opened-up chord falls under the fingers.

Try it: With Cmaj7 chosen, step through all four inversions and watch the staff. Which inversion has two notes touching? Then switch to D♭maj7 and find the touching pair again. Does it follow the same chord tones?

02

Drop Lab

The recipe

Count from the top. Drop one voice. One octave down.

Start from any close-position inversion. Number the voices from the top: 1, 2, 3, 4. A Drop 2 voicing moves voice 2 down an octave. Drop 3 moves voice 3. Drop 2 & 4 moves both voice 2 and voice 4. The name is the recipe: the number tells you which voice to drop.

Cmaj7 ·

Voicing code, bottom to top

The secret that makes this useful. Look at the top note in Step 1 and again in Step 3. In Drop 2 and Drop 3, the top note never changes, because the dropped voice is always a middle one. So you can put a melody note on top and let the rest of the chord open up underneath it. That is how arrangers harmonize a melody with a four-horn section.

Predict, then check: For Cmaj7 from root position, which note ends up on the bottom of a Drop 3? Say it before you press the button. Then switch to Drop 2 & 4 and list which two notes dropped. Finally, pick From 1st inv. and write the Drop 2 note order. Is it the same chord?

03

Compare

One source, four sounds

Same starting chord, different spreads.

Here is Cmaj7 built from one close-position inversion four ways. The reach is the distance from the lowest note to the highest, in semitones. Gold rings mark the voices that moved.

Close vs. the three drops

Press Play all four, then listen for which spacing feels the most open.

Drop 2

Usually the most compact spread of the drops, with the bottom three notes spaced like a tidy stack and the top note free for a melody. The workhorse for guitar comping and horn sections.

Drop 3

The dropped note leaves a bigger gap above the bass, so it feels like a bass note with a chord floating over it. Common on the lower guitar strings and in the piano's left hand.

Drop 2 & 4

Two voices drop, giving the widest spread and the longest reach. It sounds big and airy on piano and in arrangements, and it is too wide for most guitar grips.

Turn and talk: Which voicing would you choose for (a) a pianist's left hand low on the keyboard, (b) a guitarist comping behind a singer, (c) a sax section playing a melody? Use the reach numbers and what you heard to argue for your choice.

04

Guitar

Why guitarists love these shapes

Four notes on four neighbouring strings.

Number the strings from the thin one: 1 = high E up to 6 = low E. Drop 2 voicings fit on four strings in a row, so each shape is one comfortable grip: 6-5-4-3, 5-4-3-2 or 4-3-2-1. Drop 3 skips one string: 6-4-3-2 or 5-3-2-1. Because the pitches are the same distances apart wherever you play them, a shape with no open strings slides up the neck and turns into another chord.

05

Voice leading

The real payoff

Because each chord has four drop shapes, you can choose the one that moves least.

Every chord has four versions of a drop voicing (one for each chord tone you can put on the bottom), and you can place each in different registers. Voice leading means sending each voice to the nearest note of the next chord. The chart below picks, for every chord, the voicing whose four voices travel the fewest semitones. Pick a progression and compare it with always putting the root on the bottom.

Smoothest path through the changes

● root● 3rd● 5th● 7thNumbers under each chord: semitones each voice moved, low to high.

Guide tones. The 3rd and 7th tell you what kind of chord it is. In a ii–V–I, the 7th of each chord falls by a half step into the 3rd of the next: Dm7's C drops to G7's B, and G7's F drops to Cmaj7's E. Turn on Highlight guide tones and follow the gold lines. The other voices barely move.

Investigate: In ii–V–I with Drop 2, list how far each voice moves from Dm7 to G7. What is the largest move? Then switch to Close position and compare the totals. Why might arrangers still choose Drop 2 when the totals are similar?

07

Check

Exit ticket

Can you drop a voice on demand?

Written exit ticket: In 3–5 sentences, explain to a guitarist friend what a Drop 2 voicing is and why it is easier to play than a close-position seventh chord. Use at least three of these words: voicing, close position, voice, octave, inversion, guide tone.

The takeaway

Same letters, new spacing.

Drop voicings never change what chord you are playing, only how it is spread. Count from the top, move one voice down an octave, and you get something wider, playable and ready for smooth voice leading. Try the recipe on tunes in the Fake Book.

Key vocabulary

Seventh chord
A chord of four notes: root, 3rd, 5th and 7th.
Voicing
How a chord's notes are arranged: which is on the bottom and how far apart they sit.
Close position
A voicing with the notes packed as tightly as possible, inside one octave.
Inversion
A close-position chord with a note other than the root on the bottom.
Voice
One of the notes in a chord, counted from the top (voice 1 is the highest).
Drop 2
Move the second voice from the top down an octave.
Drop 3
Move the third voice from the top down an octave.
Drop 2 & 4
Move the second and fourth voices from the top down an octave.
Reach
The distance from the lowest note of a voicing to the highest, in semitones.
Voice leading
Moving from chord to chord so each voice travels as little as possible.
Guide tones
The 3rd and 7th, the notes that define a chord's quality.

Watch for these misconceptions

  • “Drop 2 means the second note from the bottom.” Voices are counted from the top.
  • “Dropping a note changes the chord.” The four letters stay the same; only the spacing changes.
  • “The bottom note has to be the root.” The chord's name does not depend on the bass note in these voicings; all four chord tones can sit on the bottom.
  • “Drop voicings are only for guitar.” They came up in arranging for horn sections and are just as common on piano.
