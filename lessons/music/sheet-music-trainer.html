02 · Decode a score

Music notation answers four questions at once

A note head gives you pitch and duration, but fluent readers also scan the meter, expression marks, and the shape of the phrase. Read in layers instead of trying to decode every symbol at the same time.

A sound can make a picture

Watch, count, feel, follow

Pitch
Color and height show which bell rings and whether the sound moves up or down.
Time
Four boxes make four steady turns. Point once in each box while you count.
Expression
Start with a tiny sound and let each bell become a little stronger.
Direction
The upward arrow groups the bells into one musical thought.

Match symbols to actions

Every mark tells you what to do

Pitch
The treble clef turns each line and space into a letter name. The notes climb C–D–E–G.
Time
Four quarter notes fill one 4/4 measure: one sound on each numbered beat.
Expression
mf means mezzo-forte: use a comfortably strong sound, not your loudest.
Direction
The phrase arc asks you to connect all four notes like one spoken sentence.

Collect the clues before playing

Key, rhythm, dynamics, route

Pitch
The sharp in the key signature changes every F to F♯ until the key signature changes.
Time
In 4/4, two pairs of eighth notes use two beats; the half note uses the final two.
Expression
p begins softly. The crescendo opens toward a gradually stronger sound.
Direction
The phrase arc groups the idea; the repeat instruction sends you back through it.

Read relationships, not isolated notes

Group the pulse and hear the harmony

Pitch
Bass clef centers the lower register. Read intervals as shapes: steps, skips, and chord outlines.
Time
Six eighth notes group naturally as two large beats: ONE-and-a, TWO-and-a.
Expression
Accents energize selected attacks; f begins strongly, then the decrescendo releases.
Direction
First and second endings change the route through a repeated passage.

Make an interpretation from evidence

Balance independent musical layers

Pitch
Two seventh chords show harmony as vertical spelling: B♭–D–F–A moves to G–B♭–D–F.
Time
Each half-note sonority occupies two beats, so the harmonic rhythm divides the 4/4 bar in half.
Expression
The crescendo asks the second harmony to arrive with greater intensity than the first.
Direction
D.S. al Coda means return to the sign, continue to “To Coda,” then jump to the coda.

Connect notation to structure

Read the score as an argument

Pitch
The engine spells G♯ augmented as G♯–B♯–D𝄪. The double sharp preserves its stack of major thirds.
Time
Five eighth-note pulses can group 2+3 or 3+2; the conductor’s gesture reveals the intended accent pattern.
Expression
sfp asks for a sudden accent followed immediately by softness.
Direction
The spelled chord points beyond sound alone: notation preserves interval and harmonic function.

Before the first note

  1. Locate the clef.It establishes the pitch map.
  2. Scan the key signature.Carry its sharps or flats through the measure.
  3. Count the meter.Find the beat unit and the number of beats.
  4. Preview the route.Notice leaps, repeated patterns, rests, and expressive changes.

03 · Desk bells

One color, one note, one name

Desk bells and handbells are color-coded so a whole class can play a song together: each child holds one or two bells and rings when their color appears. Tap a bell to hear it, then find the same color on the staff.

Here Do is always C (fixed-do). The staff shows eight bells climbing from middle C to the C above: a step is the next line or space, a skip jumps over one.

05 · Practice

Turn correct answers into fluent reading

Accuracy is the first milestone, not the last. Use short, repeatable rounds that move from recognition to notation, sound, and explanation.

Changing this also changes the trainer level, so the printed plan and activities stay matched.

  1. 1

    Decode

    Complete five identification questions slowly. Say the clef, line or space, and note name aloud before answering.

    Goal: 4 of 5 correct without a hint
  2. 2

    Construct

    Switch to the matching construction drill. Place the notation from memory, then compare the answer’s spelling.

    Goal: explain every accidental
  3. 3

    Hear

    Use Hear it, sing or hum the pitch, then find it on bells, piano, guitar, or another instrument.

    Goal: connect symbol, name, and sound
  4. 4

    Transfer

    Find the same idea in a real score. Circle its context: key, interval, scale fragment, or chord function.

    Goal: one discovery in real music

Five-minute warm-up

One level, one topic: three identification questions, two construction questions, then play or sing the last answer.

Fifteen-minute study

Complete the full four-step cycle. Finish by writing one sentence about the rule that made the answer correct.

Ensemble challenge

One student reads, one constructs, and one performs. Rotate roles after each question so notation becomes shared language.

06 · Reference

A reader’s pocket reference

Treble clef

Lines (bottom to top): E G B D F, as in “Every Good Boy Does Fine.” Spaces spell F A C E.

Bass clef

Lines: G B D F A, “Good Boys Do Fine Always.” Spaces: A C E G, “All Cows Eat Grass.”

Alto clef

The middle line is middle C. Violas commonly read alto clef; trombones usually read bass clef and sometimes tenor clef.

Sharps & flats

Sharps arrive F C G D A E B. Flats arrive B E A D G C F. Last sharp, up a half step = the key. Second-to-last flat = the key.

Interval recipe

Count letters for the number, count half steps for the quality. Perfect: 0, 5, 7, 12. Major 2nd 2, 3rd 4, 6th 9, 7th 11. Minor is one less.

Scale patterns

Major: W W H W W W H. Natural minor: W H W W H W W. In these seven-note scales, each letter name appears once before the closing octave.

Chord recipes

Major 4+3, minor 3+4, diminished 3+3, augmented 4+4 (half steps, stacked). A seventh chord adds one more third.

Spelling matters

C♯ and D♭ sound the same but are different notes. A 3rd above C always uses the letter E (E, E♭ or E♯), never F♭.

07 · For teachers

Using it in class

  • Project it. The staff is large and high-contrast; the Hear it button lets the room check each answer by ear.
  • Mix levels. Different students can run different levels side by side; progress is saved per level in each browser.
  • Keyboard friendly. In Note Identification type C D E F G A B to answer. When constructing, use the up and down arrows to move the note on the staff, Enter to place it, Backspace to remove it.
  • Pair with the lab. After Intervals, try the triads in The Piano; after Scales, the Modes lesson.
  • Ask for reasoning. A correct answer shows recognition; a spoken explanation reveals whether the student used the clef, counted letter names, or recognized a pattern.
  • Separate fluency from speed. Build accuracy with hints available, then repeat without color or solfege before adding a timer.
  • Notation font. Clefs and accidentals use the open-licensed Noto Music font, bundled with the site so they draw the same on every device.

Key vocabulary

Staff
Five lines and four spaces where notes live.
Clef
The symbol that tells you which pitches the lines and spaces mean: treble, bass or alto.
Ledger line
A short extra line for notes above or below the staff.
Solfege
Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti: syllables for the notes of a scale.
Accidental
A sharp (♯), flat (♭) or natural (♮) that changes a note by a half step.
Key signature
The sharps or flats at the start of the staff that apply to the whole piece.
Interval
The distance between two notes, named by number and quality.
Quality
Perfect, major, minor, augmented or diminished.
Scale
A set of notes in order, climbing by a pattern of whole and half steps.
Triad
A three-note chord built from stacked thirds.
Inversion
A chord with a note other than the root on the bottom.
Enharmonic
Two spellings of the same sound, like C♯ and D♭.
