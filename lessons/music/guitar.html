01

History

Before the frets: where the guitar came from

Three thousand years of strings over a wooden box.

Every guitar uses one simple plan: strings stretched along a neck, over a hollow body that makes them louder. Musicians in ancient Egypt played long-necked lutes built that way more than 3,000 years ago. Over the centuries the instrument kept changing in three ways: it gained strings, it changed how it was played (picking single notes or strumming chords), and it kept getting louder. The last change finally needed electricity.

Hear the guitar grow

The guitar grew by adding lower strings under the ones it already had. Strum each stage and listen to the chord get deeper and fuller. The highest strings keep the same spacing, a 4th, a 3rd, and a 4th, all the way through.

Each button strums the open strings with the spacing that era used: D–G–B–E, then A added below, then low E.

  1. About 1450 BCE

    Lutes in ancient Egypt

    Long-necked lutes appear in Egyptian and Mesopotamian art. Their players pressed strings against the neck to change the pitch, just as you press a string against a fret. Similar instruments spread across Persia, Central Asia, and the Mediterranean world.

  2. 700s → 1400s

    The oud reaches Spain

    From the 700s to the 1400s much of Spain, called al-Andalus, was ruled by Muslim kingdoms, and the oud came with them. Europeans adapted it into the lute. Even the word “guitar” traveled this road: the Greek kithara became the Arabic qītāra and then the Spanish guitarra.

  3. 1500s · Spain

    Vihuela and the four-course guitar

    Renaissance Spain had two guitar-shaped instruments. The vihuela had six courses, pairs of strings tuned together, and was used for serious solo music; Luis de Milán printed the first book of vihuela music in 1536. The small, cheap four-course guitar was for songs and dances. Its four courses had the same spacing as the top four strings of your guitar.

  4. 1600s → 1700s

    The Baroque guitar learns to strum

    Adding a fifth course gave the guitar enough range to play full chords, and players began to strum them with a sweeping motion called rasgueado. Guitar books printed letter codes for chord shapes, an early version of the chord charts in this lesson. Composers such as Gaspar Sanz wrote for it, and Antonio Stradivari, the famous violin maker, also built guitars.

  5. About 1800 → 1890s

    Six strings and the classical guitar

    Around 1800, makers swapped the paired courses for six single strings and added a low E, giving the standard tuning you use today: E–A–D–G–B–E. Composers like Fernando Sor and Mauro Giuliani wrote concert music for it.

    In the 1850s–1880s the Spanish builder Antonio de Torres Jurado made the guitar bigger and perfected a thin top held up by a fan of wooden strips, called fan bracing. The top could vibrate freely, so the guitar sounded louder and richer. Classical guitars are still built on his design.

  6. 1833 → 1930s · United States

    The American steel-string guitar

    Christian Frederick Martin, a German guitar maker, moved to New York in 1833 and later to Nazareth, Pennsylvania. His shop braced guitar tops in an X shape. In the early 1900s, steel strings replaced gut on many American guitars because they were louder and brighter. The X-brace could hold their extra pull. Big-bodied dreadnought guitars from the 1930s became the sound of folk, country, and bluegrass.

  7. 1920s

    Still too quiet

    In the jazz bands of the 1920s, the guitar was buried under trumpets, saxophones, and drums. Inventors first tried to solve this without electricity. John Dopyera's resonator guitar (1927) used metal cones to make the strings ring louder, and blues players loved its bright, metallic sound.

  8. 1931 → 1940s

    The guitar goes electric

    George Beauchamp built a pickup: a magnet wrapped in copper wire that turns a vibrating steel string into an electric signal. An amplifier and loudspeaker make that signal as loud as you like. His “Frying Pan” lap-steel guitar, built with Adolph Rickenbacker, went on sale in 1932, and Gibson's ES-150 (1936) put a pickup on a regular hollow guitar. Charlie Christian showed the world the guitar could now take a solo.

  9. 1950s → today

    Solid bodies and rock and roll

    Turn a hollow electric guitar up loud and it starts to howl with feedback, because the body vibrates along with the speaker. A solid-body guitar barely vibrates, so it can play much louder. Leo Fender's Broadcaster (1950, soon renamed the Telecaster), the Gibson Les Paul (1952), and the Fender Stratocaster (1954) became the sound of rock and roll. Today you can choose a nylon-string classical, a steel-string acoustic, or an electric, and play the same chord shapes on all three.

How an acoustic guitar makes sound

  1. String You pluck or strum, and the string vibrates.
  2. Bridge The bridge passes that vibration into the guitar's top.
  3. Top The thin wooden top moves like a drum skin and pushes on the air.
  4. Body and soundhole Air inside the hollow body resonates and pours out of the soundhole.

How an electric guitar makes sound

  1. String A steel string vibrates above the pickups.
  2. Pickup The moving steel disturbs the pickup's magnetic field, which makes a small electric current in its coil.
  3. Cable and amplifier The amplifier makes that weak signal much stronger, and can add effects like distortion.
  4. Speaker The speaker turns the signal back into moving air you can hear.

Talk about it: Strumming chords became popular on the Baroque guitar more than 300 years ago, and the electric guitar was invented because guitars were too quiet. Pick one change from the timeline. What music could people play after it that they couldn't play before?

02

Fretboard

Read the grid

Six strings. Twelve frets per octave.

In standard tuning, the six strings ring out (low to high) E, A, D, G, B, e when played open — no finger pressing anywhere. Pressing a string down at a fret shortens the vibrating length, which raises the pitch by one semitone per fret. Twelve frets up from any open string lands you on the exact same note name, one octave higher — the whole system just repeats.

  • Open string No finger touching it. Rings at its tuned pitch: E, A, D, G, B, or e.
  • Fret One semitone step. Fret 1 is a half-step above open; fret 2 is a whole step above open.
  • ✕
    Muted string Not played at all — deliberately silenced with a finger or left out of the chord entirely.

Three kinds of numbers — keep them separate

1–6 String numbers

1 = thin high e; 6 = thick low E. Tune low to high: E2–A2–D3–G3–B3–E4.

0–12 Fret numbers

Where you press. This is the number written in tab. 0 = open.

1–4 Fretting fingers

1 index · 2 middle · 3 ring · 4 little finger, on either fretting hand. T sometimes means thumb. Picking hand: p thumb · i index · m middle · a ring; a pick can alternate down and up.

Hand tip: Place your fingertip just behind the fret wire, not on it. Use only enough pressure for a clear note. A barre uses one finger across several strings. If the hand tenses or hurts, release it and choose a smaller grip. Suggested fingers are starting points, not rules for every hand.

Read tablature from left to right

  • Line = a string (high e on top)
  • Number = fret, never a finger
  • 0 = play the string open
  • Stacked numbers sound together

Tab symbols you will meet next

5h7: hammer on from fret 5 to 7. 7p5: pull off from 7 to 5. 5/7: slide up. 7\5: slide down. b: bend (check the target pitch). ~: vibrato. x: muted/percussive note. These techniques change how a note begins or moves; learn the plain fretted notes first.

03

Intervals

The distance between notes is the whole story

Chords are built from a root, then measured distances.

Pick any note and call it the root. Every other note in a chord is defined by how many semitones above the root it sits — that distance is called an interval. Change the intervals and you change the chord, no matter what root you started from. That's why a major chord "feels" the same whether it's built on C or on F#.

3

Stack them into a triad

A basic major triad is root + major 3rd + perfect 5th — semitone intervals 0, 4, 7. Flip that middle interval to a minor 3rd — 0, 3, 7 — and the chord becomes a minor triad. Everything below builds on this one idea.

Spelling note: Interval names count letter names as well as semitones. C–E♭ is a minor third; C–D♯ sounds the same in equal temperament but is spelled as an augmented second. The fretboard uses compact sharp pitch labels; use the formulas and correctly spelled examples here to learn the harmony.

Builder

Place your own notes

Click frets. We'll tell you what chord you made.

Click any fret on a string to place a note there — you'll hear a synthesized pluck at that pitch. Click a string's label at the far left to toggle it between open (green dot) and muted (×). The lowest sounding pitch is the bass. If it is a chord tone other than the root, you have an inversion, such as C/E. A slash chord can also have a non-chord tone in the bass. Once you've placed some notes, use the playback buttons to hear the whole chord strummed or broken into an arpeggio.

Start from:

Try it: place open E (string 6), fret 2 on A, fret 2 on D, fret 1 on G, open B, open e — that's E major (or press E under “Start from”). Now lower the G-string note to open: which note changed, and what chord is it now? For F♯ major, move every note up two frets, including the open strings: 2–4–4–3–2–2. Triad routes

Find the chord inside the scale.

A scale moves through the key’s seven notes. A triad takes every other scale note: a root, a third and a fifth. An arpeggio plays those chord tones one at a time. Keep the key, but change the three notes you aim for as the harmony changes.

First experiment: leave C major and I–ii–V–I selected. Play the route, then select ii. Notice that D–F–A are 2–4–6 in C major but 1–♭3–5 relative to D. The chord’s root is not always the key’s tonic.

Seven notes, seven triads

Connect the change

“Nearest entry” minimizes the pitch jump into the next arpeggio within this fret window. It may begin on the third or fifth. This is a melodic entry choice; a chord inversion depends on the harmony’s lowest sounding note. It is not a claim that every full chord voice moves by a step.

Find the next target

  1. Hear the difference. Play the scale, then I’s arpeggio. Say which scale notes the arpeggio skips.
  2. Name two systems. Select ii and V. Read each note as a key degree, then as a chord tone.
  3. Trace a route. Play I–ii–V–I at 60. Follow the highlighted tab and say the chord name on beat one.
  4. Connect and transpose. Choose “Nearest entry,” identify shared tones, then move to G or F major. Keep the Roman route and rebuild the notes.
  5. Make a melody. On your guitar, land on a chord tone as each bar changes. Add nearby scale notes between targets; listen for tension and resolution.

In a major key, I can establish home, ii can prepare V, and V can lead back to I. Other routes are possible. Here V is a three-note major triad; adding a seventh makes V7, a separate chord type. Explore Music Lab or the piano chord lesson to transfer the same relationships.

Scales & modes

The palette chords are picked from

A scale is a chord's whole family of notes.

A scale supplies notes for melodies and harmony. Start with major or minor pentatonic, one small position at a time. A mode is more than a starting note: its tonic must sound like home. D Dorian shares C major’s notes, but centers D. Compare modes on the same root to hear their different intervals.

Major and minor pentatonic scales have no half-steps; the blues scale adds a chromatic passing tone and does have half-steps. The library includes all seven major-scale modes plus common pentatonic, blues, harmonic minor and jazz melodic minor scales. The one-octave tab below gives you a concrete route for every selection.

09

Voicings

Same harmony. Different arrangement.

Inversion tells you which chord tone is lowest. Voicing tells you the register, spacing, order and doubling of all the notes. C–E–G and C–G–E are both root position if C is lowest. Triads have three bass positions; seventh chords have four.

Choose a voicing for a musical job

Close

Distinct chord tones fit within an octave.

Try: Learn a three-string triad; compare every bass position.

Spread / open spacing

Chord tones span more than an octave. This does not mean open strings.

Try: Let bass and melody stand apart.

Drop 2 / drop 3

From four close voices, lower the second / third voice from the top by an octave.

Try: Compare the resulting bass position and string skips.

Shell

Use root, third and seventh; omit the fifth.

Try: Leave room for another instrument.

Rootless

Omit the root when a bass player or context supplies it.

Try: Cmaj7 can use E–G–B; alone this also sounds like Em.

Extensions / alterations

9 = 2 + octave, 11 = 4 + octave, 13 = 6 + octave; ♭/♯ alters a tone.

Try: C9 includes B♭; Cadd9 does not. C13 often uses C–E–B♭–A, omitting fifth, ninth and eleventh.

Guitar has six strings and four fretting fingers. A chord symbol describes harmony, not a demand to play every possible extension. Keep the defining third, seventh and requested color where appropriate; label omitted tones honestly.

Practice: choose C major, close voicing. Play root, first and second inversion. Sing the bass note each time. Then try Cmaj7 drop 2. Arpeggiate slowly to check every intended string rings.

10

Progressions

Turn shapes into a piece of music.

In major keys, the triads are I · ii · iii · IV · V · vi · vii°. Capitals mean major; lowercase means minor; ° means diminished. I is home, IV and ii often lead away, and V often leads back. These are useful tendencies, not rules every song must follow.

Start with Em–Am, one chord per bar. Count “1, 2, 3, 4.” Change on the next 1. Then try a numbered pattern, or add exact grips from either chord explorer. Move chords earlier or later and listen to how the ending changes.

    Ready. Each chord lasts one bar of four beats.

    Arrange with your hands: retain a shared finger where comfortable, move the other notes the shortest practical distance, and slow the tempo before a difficult change. A mathematically short path is not always the most comfortable one. Compare two grips for the same chord in your progression.

    Key vocabulary

    Oud
    A round-backed, fretless lute from the Middle East; an ancestor of the European lute.
    Vihuela
    A guitar-shaped Spanish instrument of the 1500s, tuned like a lute.
    Course
    A pair of strings tuned together and played as one, as on early guitars and today’s 12-string.
    Fan bracing
    A fan of thin wooden strips under a classical guitar’s top that keeps it strong but free to vibrate.
    Pickup
    A magnet wrapped in wire that turns a vibrating steel string into an electric signal.
    Solid-body guitar
    An electric guitar with a solid wooden body, so it can play loud without feedback.
    Root
    The note a chord is named after.
    Interval
    The semitone distance between two notes.
    Triad
    A three-note chord: root, 3rd, and 5th.
    Inversion
    A chord with a chord tone other than the root as its lowest sounding note.
    Slash chord
    Notation like C/E — a C chord with E in the bass.
    Barre chord
    A shape where one finger presses multiple strings, movable to any fret.
    Open chord
    A chord that uses at least one unfretted string.
    Diatonic
    Built from the notes of the selected key.
    Arpeggio
    Chord tones played one at a time.
    Key degree
    A note’s place in the key’s scale, counted from the tonic.
    Common tone
    A note shared by two chords.
    Voicing
    One specific arrangement of a chord's notes on the neck.
