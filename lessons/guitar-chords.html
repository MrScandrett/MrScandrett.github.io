Music Theory · 45–60 minutes · Grades 6–12

Every chord is just notes stacked in a pattern.

A guitar has 6 strings and a grid of frets — which means every chord you'll ever play is a set of dots on that grid. Learn the interval pattern behind major, minor, and 7th chords, then place your own dots on a live fretboard and let the lesson tell you what chord you built.

01

Fretboard

Read the grid

Six strings. Twelve frets per octave.

In standard tuning, the six strings ring out (low to high) E, A, D, G, B, e when played open — no finger pressing anywhere. Pressing a string down at a fret shortens the vibrating length, which raises the pitch by one semitone per fret. Twelve frets up from any open string lands you on the exact same note name, one octave higher — the whole system just repeats.

Open string

No finger touching it. Rings at its tuned pitch: E, A, D, G, B, or e.

Fret

One semitone step. Fret 1 is a half-step above open; fret 2 is a whole step above open.

Muted string

Not played at all — deliberately silenced with a finger or left out of the chord entirely.

Every diagram on this page uses tab-style orientation: the thin high e string is drawn on top, the thick low E string on the bottom, and frets run left to right starting at the nut (fret 0).

02

Intervals

The distance between notes is the whole story

Chords are built from a root, then measured distances.

Pick any note and call it the root. Every other note in a chord is defined by how many semitones above the root it sits — that distance is called an interval. Change the intervals and you change the chord, no matter what root you started from. That's why a major chord "feels" the same whether it's built on C or on F#.

0 — Root

The note the chord is named after.

3 — Minor 3rd

A minor-ish, darker color.

4 — Major 3rd

A major-ish, brighter color.

7 — Perfect 5th

The stable "backbone" of almost every chord.

A basic major triad is root + major 3rd + perfect 5th — semitone intervals 0, 4, 7. Flip that middle interval to a minor 3rd — 0, 3, 7 — and the exact same shape becomes a minor triad. Everything below builds on this one idea.

03

Chord types

The formulas this lesson checks against

One interval formula per chord family.

The builder and encyclopedia below both check what you play against these formulas (semitones above the root). Add the 4th note of a 7th chord and the color gets more complex; swap the 3rd out entirely for a 2nd or 4th and you get a suspended chord that feels unresolved on purpose.

Major — 0, 4, 7

Bright, resolved. C, E, G

Minor — 0, 3, 7

Darker, moodier. C, D#, G

Dominant 7th — 0, 4, 7, 10

Wants to resolve somewhere. C, E, G, A#

Major 7th — 0, 4, 7, 11

Dreamy, jazzy. C, E, G, B

Minor 7th — 0, 3, 7, 10

Smooth, mellow. C, D#, G, A#

sus2 / sus4 — 0, 2, 7 / 0, 5, 7

No 3rd at all — neither major nor minor.

Diminished — 0, 3, 6

Tense, unstable. Every interval is a minor 3rd.

Power chord — 0, 7

Just root and 5th — no 3rd, so it's neither major nor minor.

04

Builder

Place your own notes

Click frets. We'll tell you what chord you made.

Click any fret on a string to place a note there — you'll hear a synthesized pluck at that pitch. Click a string's label at the far left to toggle it between open (green dot) and muted (×). The lowest string you play becomes the bass note — if it isn't the chord's root, you've built an inversion (a slash chord, like C/E). Once you've placed some notes, use the playback buttons to hear the whole chord strummed or broken into an arpeggio.

Try it: place open E (string 6), fret 2 on A, fret 2 on D, fret 1 on G, open B, open e — that's the E major shape from the hero diagram above. Then move every dot up two frets and mute the open strings to find F# major using the same shape.

Try it: the diatonic triad ladder

A classic ear-training drill. Walk up the seven triads built from the C major scale — C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B° — and back to C. Play the ladder in root position first and notice how far your hand jumps between chords. Then play the same seven chords in 1st inversion, then 2nd — each ladder barely moves your hand, because only one note changes between neighboring chords. This is the same shape guitarists use to keep chord changes smooth instead of leaping around the neck.

06

Scales & modes

The palette chords are picked from

A scale is a chord's whole family of notes.

Where a chord is 3–4 notes stacked at once, a scale is the full set of notes — usually 5 to 7 of them — a melody or solo draws from over a chord progression. Modes are the seven scales hiding inside every major scale: play the same seven notes but start (and resolve) on a different one, and the whole thing takes on a new mood, from the bright major sound of Ionian down to the unstable Locrian. Pentatonic and blues scales strip that down to 5–6 notes with no awkward half-steps, which is why they're the first scales most guitarists learn to solo with. Click any dot on the fretboard to hear that note, or use the playback buttons to hear the whole scale run ascending, descending, or both.

Key vocabulary

Root
The note a chord is named after.
Interval
The semitone distance between two notes.
Triad
A three-note chord: root, 3rd, and 5th.
Inversion
A chord played with a note other than the root as the lowest note.
Slash chord
Notation like C/E — a C chord with E in the bass.
Barre chord
A shape where one finger presses multiple strings, movable to any fret.
Open chord
A chord that uses at least one unfretted string.
Voicing
One specific arrangement of a chord's notes on the neck.
