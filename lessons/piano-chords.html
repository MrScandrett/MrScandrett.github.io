Every chord is just notes stacked in a pattern.

01

Keyboard

Read the layout

Twelve keys per octave, laid out in a straight line.

An octave is seven white keys — C, D, E, F, G, A, B — with five black keys filling most of the gaps between them. Moving from any key to the very next key, white or black, raises the pitch by one semitone. Notice the two places with no black key between the whites: E→F and B→C. That's why those two pairs are only a semitone apart while every other white-key pair is a whole step (two semitones).

White key

The natural notes: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, repeating every octave.

Black key

The sharps/flats between most white keys — C#, D#, F#, G#, A#.

Octave

12 semitones. Same note name, letter repeats, pitch doubles in frequency.

This lesson's keyboard spans two full octaves plus a closing C, so you have room to build chords low, high, or spread across a wide range.

02

Intervals

The distance between notes is the whole story

Chords are built from a root, then measured distances.

Pick any note and call it the root. Every other note in a chord is defined by how many semitones above the root it sits — that distance is called an interval. Change the intervals and you change the chord, no matter what root you started from. That's why a major chord "feels" the same whether it's built on C or on F#.

0 — Root

The note the chord is named after.

3 — Minor 3rd

A minor-ish, darker color.

4 — Major 3rd

A major-ish, brighter color.

7 — Perfect 5th

The stable "backbone" of almost every chord.

A basic major triad is root + major 3rd + perfect 5th — semitone intervals 0, 4, 7. Flip that middle interval to a minor 3rd — 0, 3, 7 — and the exact same shape becomes a minor triad. On a keyboard you can see this directly: count keys, don't guess by ear.

03

Chord types

The formulas this lesson checks against

One interval formula per chord family.

The builder and encyclopedia below both check what you play against these formulas (semitones above the root). Add the 4th note of a 7th chord and the color gets more complex; swap the 3rd out entirely for a 2nd or 4th and you get a suspended chord that feels unresolved on purpose.

Major — 0, 4, 7

Bright, resolved. C, E, G

Minor — 0, 3, 7

Darker, moodier. C, D#, G

Dominant 7th — 0, 4, 7, 10

Wants to resolve somewhere. C, E, G, A#

Major 7th — 0, 4, 7, 11

Dreamy, jazzy. C, E, G, B

Minor 7th — 0, 3, 7, 10

Smooth, mellow. C, D#, G, A#

sus2 / sus4 — 0, 2, 7 / 0, 5, 7

No 3rd at all — neither major nor minor.

Diminished — 0, 3, 6

Tense, unstable. Every interval is a minor 3rd.

Power chord — 0, 7

Just root and 5th — no 3rd, so it's neither major nor minor.

04

Builder

Place your own notes

Click keys. We'll tell you what chord you made.

Click any key to select it — you'll hear its pitch — click again to deselect. Unlike a guitar, you're not limited to one note per string, so you can stack notes anywhere across both octaves. The lowest note you select becomes the bass note — if it isn't the chord's root, you've built an inversion (a slash chord, like C/E). Once you've selected some notes, use the playback buttons to hear them struck together as a block chord or broken one at a time.

Try it: click C4, E4, and G4 — that's C major. Now move all three up two semitones (D4, F#4, A4) to find D major using the exact same shape. Then try C4, E4, G4, A4 and see what a 6th chord looks like next to a minor 7th built on a different root.

Try it: the diatonic triad ladder

A classic method-book drill. Walk up the seven triads built from the C major scale — C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B° — and back to C. Play the ladder in root position first and notice how far your hand jumps between chords. Then play the same seven chords in 1st inversion, then 2nd — each ladder barely moves your hand, because only one note changes between neighboring chords. This is exactly how pianists learn to connect chord changes smoothly instead of leaping around the keyboard.

06

Scales & modes

The palette chords are picked from

A scale is a chord's whole family of notes.

Where a chord is 3–4 notes stacked at once, a scale is the full set of notes — usually 5 to 7 of them — a melody or solo draws from over a chord progression. Modes are the seven scales hiding inside every major scale: play the same seven notes but start (and resolve) on a different one, and the whole thing takes on a new mood, from the bright major sound of Ionian down to the unstable Locrian. Pentatonic and blues scales strip that down to 5–6 notes with no awkward half-steps, which is why they're the first scales most improvisers learn. Click any key to hear that note, or use the playback buttons to hear the whole scale run ascending, descending, or both.

Key vocabulary

Root
The note a chord is named after.
Interval
The semitone distance between two notes.
Triad
A three-note chord: root, 3rd, and 5th.
Inversion
A chord played with a note other than the root as the lowest note.
Slash chord
Notation like C/E — a C chord with E in the bass.
Voicing
One specific arrangement of a chord's notes on the keyboard.
Closed voicing
All chord tones stacked within a single octave.
Octave
The 12-semitone span between one note and the next with the same name.
