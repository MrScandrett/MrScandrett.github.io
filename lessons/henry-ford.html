Henry Ford

The Moving Assembly Line Interchangeable Parts The Model T Founder of Ford Motor Company

Henry Ford (1863–1947) was an American industrialist and engineer whose Ford Motor Company perfected the moving assembly line, turning the automobile from a hand-built luxury into an affordable, mass-produced machine. The manufacturing methods he pioneered at the Highland Park plant reshaped not just the car industry but factory work worldwide.

Who Was Henry Ford?

Born July 30, 1863, on a family farm in Springwells Township, near Dearborn, Michigan, Ford grew up expected to take over the farm. Machines interested him far more than farming did, and as a teenager he left home for Detroit to apprentice as a machinist, learning to work with the precision tools and metal parts that would define his later career.

Ford eventually took a job as an engineer for the Edison Illuminating Company, where he tinkered with gasoline engines in his spare time. There he met Thomas Edison, who encouraged him to keep pursuing his automobile experiments. In 1896, Ford built his first experimental vehicle, the Quadricycle — a lightweight frame on four bicycle wheels powered by a small gasoline engine. After two earlier automobile ventures failed, Ford founded the Ford Motor Company in 1903.

In 1908, Ford introduced the Model T: simple to drive, durable on rough roads, and priced for ordinary families rather than the wealthy. To build it fast enough to meet demand, Ford's engineers introduced the moving assembly line at the Highland Park plant in 1913, breaking car assembly into small repeated tasks performed as the chassis moved past stationary workers. The change was staggering — the time to build a single Model T dropped from roughly half a day to as little as ninety minutes to a few hours as the line was refined. In January 1914, Ford announced the $5 workday, roughly double the prevailing factory wage, partly to cut punishing worker turnover and partly so his own employees could afford to buy the cars they built.

More than 15 million Model Ts were built before production ended in 1927. Ford also founded The Henry Ford museum and Greenfield Village in Dearborn to preserve America's industrial and everyday history. His record is not without serious blemishes: in the 1920s he published and promoted antisemitic writings that caused real harm and remain a significant, troubling part of his legacy. Henry Ford died on April 7, 1947, at age 83.

How the Assembly Line Actually Worked

Before Ford, automobiles were built by craft production: a small team of skilled workers gathered around one car, fitting and hand-filing parts to make them work together, then moving on to build the next car the same slow way. Two engineering ideas let Ford break out of that model.

The first was interchangeable parts — manufacturing every component to a precise, consistent standard so that any given part would fit any car on the line without hand-fitting. The idea traces back to manufacturing pioneers like Eli Whitney, who applied it to muskets decades earlier, but Ford's factories applied it at an industrial scale never attempted before. The second idea was division of labor along a moving line: instead of one team building a whole car in place, the chassis moved past stationary workers, and each worker performed one small, repeated task — installing a single part, tightening a specific bolt — over and over as cars passed by.

Craft → Line
Craft production: ~12+ hours per car, skilled generalist teams build one car in place
Assembly line (1913+): a few hours per car, specialized workers repeat one task as the line moves

Combining standardized, interchangeable parts with a moving line meant workers no longer needed to hand-fit anything — they just repeated one precise motion, over and over, much faster.

From Shop Floor to Household Budget

Cutting the time and skill needed to build a car cut its cost. As the assembly line matured, Ford was able to lower the Model T's price repeatedly even as sales climbed, putting car ownership within reach of millions of American families for the first time.

Did Ford invent the assembly line?
Not entirely from scratch — moving production lines existed earlier in meatpacking plants and elsewhere, and interchangeable parts predate Ford by a century. Ford's engineers combined and refined these ideas specifically for automobile manufacturing at a scale and speed no one had achieved before.
Why pay workers more if the work became simpler?
Repetitive assembly-line work was exhausting and workers quit constantly, which was expensive to manage. The $5 workday of 1914 cut turnover dramatically and, as a side effect, gave many of Ford's own employees enough income to buy the cars they were building.

Ford's Legacy Today

"Fordism" became the term for assembly-line mass production itself, and manufacturers far beyond the auto industry — from appliances to aircraft — adopted its core ideas: standardized parts, divided repetitive tasks, and a moving line. Making cars affordable also reshaped where and how Americans lived, accelerating the growth of suburbs, highways, and a car-centered way of life that still defines much of the country's landscape.

Ford's legacy is not purely celebratory. Alongside his manufacturing achievements, he published and promoted antisemitic writings in the 1920s that caused real and lasting harm — a serious blemish that any honest account of his life and influence has to include, not set aside.

Practice Problems

Think about manufacturing, the assembly line, and Ford's life.

Easy1. In what year did Ford introduce the moving assembly line at the Highland Park plant?

Hint: This was five years after the Model T launched, and one year before the $5 workday was announced.

Easy2. What engineering idea let any manufactured part fit any car without hand-fitting?

Hint: Each part was manufactured to a precise, consistent standard, an idea with roots going back to Eli Whitney-era manufacturing.

Medium3. At its slowest, craft production took about 12 hours to build a car. If the assembly line cut that time by 75%, how many hours did it take?

Hint: 75% of 12 is 9. Subtract that savings from the original 12 hours: 12 − 9 = 3.

Medium4. What was the main purpose of the 1914 $5 workday?

Hint: Repetitive line work led to exhausting turnover; roughly doubling wages helped keep workers and let them afford Ford's own product.

Challenge5. If Ford's plant could produce one finished car every 93 minutes at a certain point, approximately how many cars could theoretically be produced in a 24-hour period?

Hint: 24 hours is 1,440 minutes. Divide 1,440 by 93 minutes per car, and round to a whole number.

Medium6. What was the name of Ford's first experimental automobile, built in 1896?

Hint: It was a lightweight frame on four bicycle wheels powered by a small gasoline engine.

Easy7. Roughly how many Model Ts were built before production ended in 1927?

Hint: It's a two-digit number of millions, one of the best-selling cars of the 20th century.

Challenge8. Why is Henry Ford's legacy considered complicated rather than purely celebratory by historians?

Hint: In the 1920s, Ford published writings promoting antisemitic views, a serious and troubling part of his historical record.
