Benjamin Franklin

Electricity The Lightning Rod Printer & Inventor Founding Father of American Science

Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790) was a printer, writer, diplomat, and self-taught scientist whose 1752 kite experiment helped prove that lightning is electricity. He turned that discovery into a life-saving invention — the lightning rod — and went on to invent bifocals, the Franklin stove, and a system of positive and negative charge that scientists still use today.

Who Was Benjamin Franklin?

Born January 17, 1706 in Boston, Franklin was the fifteenth of seventeen children of a candlemaker. With only two years of formal schooling, he apprenticed at his brother's print shop at age 12 — the trade that shaped his entire life. At 17 he ran away to Philadelphia with almost no money, and within a decade owned his own successful printing business and newspaper.

Franklin made himself wealthy enough by his early 40s to retire from printing and devote himself to science — what people at the time called "natural philosophy." In June 1752, he flew a kite with a metal key attached during a thunderstorm, drawing a spark from the key to prove that lightning and the electric spark produced in a laboratory were the same phenomenon. It was extraordinarily dangerous; at least one scientist repeating similar experiments was killed by lightning the following year.

Beyond electricity, Franklin charted the Gulf Stream, invented bifocal glasses, designed the efficient Franklin stove, and helped found the first public lending library and volunteer fire department in America. He was also a Founding Father: the only person to sign all four key documents establishing the United States — the Declaration of Independence, the Treaty of Alliance with France, the Treaty of Paris, and the Constitution.

Franklin died on April 17, 1790, in Philadelphia at age 84. Some 20,000 people attended his funeral. He remains the only American to appear on U.S. currency (the $100 bill) who was never president.

What the Kite Experiment Actually Proved

Before Franklin, "electricity" made in a lab (by rubbing a glass rod, for instance) and lightning in the sky were assumed to be different phenomena. Franklin proposed a testable idea: if lightning is electricity, a pointed metal rod raised into a storm cloud should draw a spark, just like a charged object does in a lab.

He built a kite with a pointed wire at the top and flew it into a thunderstorm, standing under a shed roof to stay dry, holding a dry silk ribbon tied to the damp kite string, with a metal key at the junction. When the storm's charge traveled down the wet string to the key, Franklin brought his knuckle near it and drew a visible spark — the same kind of spark he'd already produced in laboratory experiments.

Franklin proposed electricity as a single fluid
a surplus he called positive (+)
a deficit he called negative (−)

This single-fluid, plus/minus naming convention is still how every physics and electronics textbook describes charge today.

From Discovery to Invention

Franklin didn't stop at proving the theory — he used it. If lightning was electricity, and electricity flows through metal, then a grounded metal rod on top of a building could safely conduct a lightning strike into the earth instead of through the building's wood and people. The lightning rod, first installed in 1752, is still built essentially the same way today: a pointed conductor above the roofline, a thick metal cable, and a connection to the ground.

Wasn't the kite experiment incredibly dangerous?
Yes. Franklin was fortunate the kite string was only weakly charged rather than being struck directly. In 1753, Swedish scientist Georg Wilhelm Richmann was killed attempting a similar experiment in St. Petersburg — a reminder that Franklin's method, while brilliant, was genuinely hazardous.
Did Franklin invent electricity?
No — electrical phenomena like static sparks were already studied in labs across Europe. Franklin's contribution was showing that lightning was the same phenomenon at a vastly larger scale, and giving the field its lasting vocabulary: battery, conductor, charge, positive, and negative.

Franklin's Key Contributions

⚡
Lightning Rod (1752)

A grounded metal rod that safely channels a lightning strike away from a building — still used on skyscrapers, ships, and homes today.

📚
Electrical Vocabulary

Coined or popularized "battery," "conductor," "charge," and the "positive/negative" convention still used in every circuits class.

👁️
Bifocal Glasses (1784)

Split a single lens into two prescriptions — near and far vision — so he wouldn't need to swap glasses back and forth.

🔥
The Franklin Stove

A cast-iron heater that radiated more warmth into a room while using less wood than an open fireplace.

🌍
Charting the Gulf Stream

Working with ship captains, Franklin mapped the Atlantic current, cutting weeks off mail-ship crossing times.

📄
Civic Institutions

Founded the first public lending library, a volunteer fire department, and a university in colonial America.

Franklin's Legacy Today

The lightning rod remains one of the most direct applications of pure science to public safety in history — a single testable idea that saved an untold number of buildings and lives, decades before anyone understood what electrons actually were.

Franklin's habit of treating science as something to test, publish, and share freely — rather than protect as a trade secret — set an example for the open scientific culture that followed. He refused to patent the lightning rod or the Franklin stove, writing that "as we enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours."

Practice Problems

Think about electricity, invention, and Franklin's life.

Easy1. In what year did Franklin fly his kite during a thunderstorm?

Hint: This was one year before he received the Royal Society's Copley Medal for his electrical work.

Easy2. What invention let Franklin safely channel a lightning strike into the ground?

Hint: It's a pointed metal conductor mounted above a roofline, connected by cable to the ground.

Medium3. Franklin's stove used less wood than an open fireplace while heating a room more evenly. If an open fireplace burns 8 logs a night and the Franklin stove cuts that by 25%, how many logs does the stove burn?

Hint: 25% of 8 is 2. Subtract that savings from the original 8 logs: 8 − 2 = 6.

Medium4. Which naming convention did Franklin introduce that's still used in physics today?

Hint: Franklin described electricity as a single fluid — a surplus of it in one place, a deficit in another.

Challenge5. Why was the kite experiment so dangerous, based on what happened to another scientist the following year?

Hint: A Swedish scientist attempting a similar experiment in St. Petersburg was killed by a direct strike.

Medium6. Franklin's bifocals combined two lens prescriptions into one pair of glasses. If a single-prescription lens costs $40 and Franklin's design saves the cost of buying a second pair entirely, how much does the wearer save compared to owning two separate pairs of glasses at $40 each?

Hint: Two separate pairs would cost $80 total; one pair of bifocals costs $40. The savings is the difference.

Easy7. Which document did Franklin help sign as the only person to sign all four founding documents of the United States?

Hint: The other three were the Treaty of Alliance with France, the Treaty of Paris, and the Constitution.

Challenge8. Franklin refused to patent the lightning rod or the Franklin stove. What was his stated reasoning?

Hint: He wrote that since he benefited from others' inventions, he should be glad to serve others through his own.
