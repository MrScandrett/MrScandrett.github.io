How the bits traveled

Frequency shift

Both binary states were transmitted. A 1 was not a pulse and a 0 was not silence; the transmitter shifted between two closely spaced frequencies.

A message still in transit

The transmission is only about 0.2% of the way to M13's present distance. Stars in the cluster will have moved before the beam reaches that region.

Binary Primer

How it works

The message contains 1679 bits. Each bit was encoded by shifting a continuous radio carrier between two frequencies about 10 Hz apart. Because 1679 factors into 23 × 73, a receiver could try those two rectangular orientations; one produces the intended pictorial arrangement, conventionally shown as 73 rows × 23 columns.

Why Prime Numbers?

1679 = 23 × 73. Both 23 and 73 are prime, which means there are only two non-trivial ways to arrange 1679 bits into a rectangle: 23 × 73 or 73 × 23 Drake and his collaborators hoped a receiver would recognize the factorization and try both orientations. One produces the intended pictorial layout, usually described as 73 rows × 23 columns. That is a decoding clue, not proof that an unfamiliar recipient would interpret every symbol as humans do.

The Story

  • 📡Sent on November 16, 1974 to celebrate the upgrade of the Arecibo Observatory in Puerto Rico — then the world's largest single-dish radio telescope (305 m diameter).
  • 👨‍🔬Written by Frank Drake, with input from Carl Sagan and other colleagues at Cornell.
  • 🌌Aimed at Messier 13, a globular cluster of ~300,000 stars in the constellation Hercules, about 25,000 light-years away.
  • 🔮The solar system part of the image includes Pluto because it was still classified as a planet in 1974.
  • 💬The beam was symbolic rather than a practical attempt at conversation. M13 will move substantially during the roughly 25,000-year trip, and a round-trip reply would take about 50,000 years.

The famous characters were printed vertically in one 10 kHz-wide frequency channel. They are measurement codes, not a six-character alien message.

Why 72 seconds?

The match to the telescope's beam shape made the event interesting, but 72 seconds is the observing window—not proof that the source itself transmitted for exactly 72 seconds. Big Ear's second horn did not detect it a few minutes later.

The Story

  • 📡Detected by Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope during a SETI survey on August 15, 1977.
  • ✏️Astronomer Jerry Ehman circled the readout and wrote “Wow!” in the margin — giving the signal its famous name.
  • 🔭The signal came from one of two possible positions in Sagittarius; Big Ear's two feed horns created a location ambiguity.
  • ❓Follow-up observations have not recovered the signal. Natural and human-made explanations remain possible, so it is an unconfirmed candidate, not evidence of extraterrestrial intelligence.

Most Uncertain Variable?

L is highly influential because it directly measures how long a civilization remains detectable, but it is not the only deep uncertainty. We have no extraterrestrial examples with which to measure how often life, intelligence, or detectable technology arise. The equation organizes assumptions; it does not produce a measured census.

The Fermi Paradox

The Great Silence

In the summer of 1950, physicist Enrico Fermi was having lunch with colleagues at Los Alamos National Laboratory. The conversation turned to flying saucers and the probability of extraterrestrial life. After some discussion, Fermi suddenly asked: “Where is everybody?”

The Milky Way is roughly 13.6 billion years old and contains an estimated 100–400 billion stars. Planets are common, so even slow interstellar expansion could, under some assumptions, spread through the galaxy on a timescale much shorter than its age.

Yet no extraterrestrial technosignature has been confirmed. That is not the same as searching everywhere and finding nothing: observations have sampled only a tiny fraction of possible locations, frequencies, signal types, sensitivities, and times.

The Fermi Paradox is the tension between arguments that technological life or its traces could be widespread and the absence of confirmed evidence so far. The probability of other civilizations is not yet known.

Proposed Solutions

Speculative hypotheses
This hypothesis proposes that complex life may require an uncommon combination of planetary and stellar conditions. Several proposed requirements remain debated, and we currently have only one inhabited planet to compare.
One or more transitions between lifeless worlds and long-lived technological societies may be extremely improbable. The idea does not identify which transition is difficult, or show whether it lies in humanity's past or future.
This hypothesis imagines that advanced civilizations could observe Earth while avoiding obvious contact. It is difficult to test and has no supporting observational evidence.
This fiction-inspired hypothesis proposes that civilizations might hide because the intentions of others are unknowable. It is a strategic thought experiment, not an evidence-backed conclusion that broadcasting is dangerous.
A simulation could be defined to contain no visible extraterrestrial life, but this proposal does not currently provide a testable astronomical explanation.
Some technological civilizations might have short detectable lifetimes because of collapse, deliberate silence, or changing technology. There is no evidence that self-destruction is inevitable.
Complex life and technology may require unusually long chains of events. Humanity could be early relative to the era in which habitable environments remain available, though this is not yet testable with a single known biosphere.
Space is unfathomably vast. Even advanced civilizations may never cross interstellar distances. The nearest star is over 4 light-years away. At current rocket speeds, it would take tens of thousands of years to reach it.
