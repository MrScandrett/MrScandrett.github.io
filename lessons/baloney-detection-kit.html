    Is It Real or Make-Believe?

    How do we know when something is true?

    Big Idea

    Before we believe or share something, we can ask: Who said it, and can someone else check it?

    Think About It

    If a friend said eating grass would make you fly, would you believe it? What could you do to find out if it was true?

    Kind Questions

    We can question an idea without making fun of the person who shared it.

    The Four-Question Check

    Carl Sagan shared a group of critical-thinking tools that he informally called a “baloney detection kit.” Here we use a more welcoming name and one simple rule: stronger claims need stronger evidence.

    Ask About the Claim, Not the Person

    A horoscope may feel meaningful, but feeling meaningful is different from being supported by repeatable evidence. We can question the claim while treating people who enjoy astrology with kindness.

    Sources

    Find where the claim began. Several posts repeating one source do not count as several sources.

    Modern Health Claims

    A person asking for money for a cure should provide independent medical evidence and must never blame someone who remains ill.

    Science

    Scientific results become dependable when other people can test them, find limits, and correct mistakes.

    How Do We Know?

    Epistemology is simply the study of how we know things. It helps us slow down before reacting to viral content.

    Cognitive Bias

    Every brain uses shortcuts. Confirmation bias draws our attention to information we already agree with. The bandwagon effect can make popularity feel like proof. Having a bias does not make someone foolish; noticing it helps all of us make better choices.

    Different Questions Need Different Evidence

    A chemistry experiment, a historical event, and a personal opinion are different kinds of claims. First identify the kind of question; then choose evidence suited to it.

    Uncertainty Is Honest

    “I do not know yet” is a responsible answer. We can name what is known, what is inferred, and what still needs checking.

    We Use What We Don't Understand

    We all use tools—such as medicine, GPS, and smartphones—that no one person fully understands. That is why reliable systems use teams, testing, transparency, and correction. Asking for help is a strength.

    What Kind of Mind Does a Healthy Society Require?

    Healthy communities need people who can evaluate information, revise their views, and disagree without demeaning one another.

    Epistemology

    Observation gathers evidence. Reason compares explanations. Testimony records what people report. Each can contribute useful information, and each has limits that careful thinkers should name.

    Responsible Expertise

    Experts deserve attention when they work within their field, explain their evidence, acknowledge uncertainty, and allow qualified peers to review their work. Expertise should guide inquiry, not end it.

    Modern Media

    Algorithms may promote outrage because it drives engagement. Echo chambers can create an illusion of consensus. Deepfakes make visual evidence harder to trust. These conditions make source-checking especially important.

    The Technology Gap & Democratic Risk

    A democracy needs people who can evaluate evidence and resist manipulation. Because modern tools are complex, we also need trustworthy experts, transparent institutions, independent review, and the humility to ask what we do not yet understand.

    What History Shows

    Many cultures and communities have contributed to science, moral reasoning, human-rights traditions, and representative government. Across those traditions, evidence and reason can help hold power accountable. A firm foundation joins truth-seeking with humility and care for our neighbors.

    The same structure repeats: pseudoscience borrows scientific or professional language, avoids falsifiability, leans on authority or anecdote, resists correction, and attaches itself to power, identity, fear, or profit.

    Patterns to Notice

    Only One Source

    A surprising claim is repeated, but every version leads back to the same unverified post.

    Strong Feelings

    Fear, hope, or anger can make us want to react before we have checked the details.

    No Room for Questions

    The speaker treats respectful questions as disloyal instead of explaining the evidence.

    Only the “Hits”

    Successful predictions are counted, while misses and counterexamples disappear.

    Occam's Razor — William of Ockham

    Prefer the Simplest Explanation

    William of Ockham was a medieval philosopher. His helpful rule was: when two explanations fit the facts equally well, begin with the one that needs fewer unsupported assumptions. “Simpler” does not automatically mean “correct,” so keep checking.

    When Christianity Is Challenged: Defending Our Faith with History

    As Christians, we should not answer every objection with “just believe,” and we should not place the Bible in a test designed for scientific experiments. We can first clarify the challenge: Is the person asking about what a passage means, whether a person or place existed, how a text was preserved, or why Christians believe a theological claim?

    For questions about Bible history, use historical methods: read passages in context, compare manuscript evidence and translations, consult archaeology and relevant ancient sources, and learn how qualified historians distinguish strong evidence from uncertainty. Do not exaggerate what a discovery proves.

    History can investigate texts, people, places, customs, and testimony. It cannot repeat a past event in a laboratory or settle every theological conclusion by itself. We can explain the reasons for our faith clearly while listening carefully, admitting what we do not know, and treating the other person with gentleness and respect.

    Check Before You Share

    A healthy society is built by people who seek truth with courage, humility, and compassion. This toolkit is not about winning arguments. It helps us care for our neighbors by checking information before we act or share.

    Grade Band
    K–2 Discussion

    The True Story Test

    How do you know when a story is true? What questions could you ask to find out?

    Source Detective

    A classmate repeats a surprising story. What kind questions could help you find where it started?

    Kind Disagreement

    Practice saying, “I am not sure yet. Can we check together?” How does that sound different from calling someone wrong?

    3–5 Discussion

    Who Could Check?

    For each claim in the analyzer, ask: who else could verify this? How exactly would they check it?

    Astrology vs. Astronomy

    What is the difference between astrology (reading horoscopes) and astronomy (the science of planets)? Why does the difference matter?

    One Story or a Pattern?

    Why can a personal story be important without proving that the same thing happens to everyone?

    Occam's Razor Practice

    Your pencil is missing. Option A: your sister took it. Option B: a spy organization stole it in the night. Which explanation needs fewer assumptions? Why is that important?

    6–8 Discussion

    Evidence Standards

    Why might a health claim require different evidence than an opinion about a movie?

    Compare Explanations

    Choose a surprising claim you have heard. List two possible explanations. What evidence would help you compare them fairly?

    Fact, Inference, or Opinion?

    Choose a short news post and mark which statements are observable facts, interpretations, or opinions.

    Social Media Echo Chambers

    If an algorithm shows you only content you already agree with, what happens to your thinking over time? How is this similar to Plato's cave?

    Pattern Spotting

    Pick two examples from the pattern library. How do both borrow professional language, resist correction, or attach to identity, fear, power, or profit?

    9–12 Discussion

    Epistemic Foundation

    Sagan argued that a civilization that uses science without understanding it is dangerously fragile. What are two historical or contemporary examples where this gap had political consequences?

    Accountable Institutions

    Choose an institution and identify one practice that helps it acknowledge mistakes. What evidence would show that the practice is working?

    Categories of Evidence

    Compare the evidence used for a repeatable experiment, a past event, and a value judgment. What can each method establish?

    The Ethics of Sharing

    You reach 500 people online and encounter an unconfirmed health claim. What is your ethical obligation before sharing it?

    Evidence and Power

    History includes cases where leaders used scientific-sounding claims to support prejudice or political power. What safeguards help researchers challenge bias and correct error?

    Personal Reflection

    Think of a time you changed your mind after learning more. What helped you feel safe enough to reconsider? Which of the four questions could help next time?

    Four Thinking Shortcuts

    Confirmation Bias

    We seek evidence confirming what we already believe and dismiss what doesn't fit.

    Anecdotal Evidence

    A personal story can matter, but one story alone cannot show how often something happens.

    Appeal to Authority

    Expertise matters, but responsible experts explain evidence, limits, and uncertainty.

    False Dilemma

    A claim gives only two choices when other reasonable possibilities exist.
