Why this works

The lab uses a real ratio relationship. If the output gear has more teeth, it turns more slowly and gains torque. If the input gear is larger than the output gear, the winch gets faster but weaker.

  • A ratio above 1.00 : 1 is a reduction. That favors force over speed.
  • A ratio below 1.00 : 1 is a speed-up train. That favors rpm over lifting power.
  • If the output torque is lower than the required drum torque, the motor stalls and the crate stays put.
  • If the winch rpm climbs too high, the crate lifts unsafely fast and the system fails even if the torque is sufficient.

What gears actually do

Gears do not create energy out of nowhere. They redistribute it. A small driver gear pushing a larger driven gear makes the output spin more slowly, but with greater torque. Reverse that pairing and the system spins faster, but the lifting force drops. That trade is the heart of mechanical advantage.

Driver gear The motor gear is the input. Its tooth count sets how much leverage it has over the next gear.
Driven gear The output gear receives motion. A larger driven gear increases torque and lowers rpm.
Opposite rotation Two meshed gears always rotate in opposite directions. The lab visualizes that sign flip directly.
Mechanical advantage A reduction trades speed for torque. Friction uses some of that gain, so a ratio only slightly above 1:1 may still deliver less torque than the motor.

Beyond one pair: idlers and compound trains

A single pair rarely gives a big enough ratio. Engineers chain gears together, and two rules cover almost every gearbox you will meet.

Idler gear A gear between the driver and driven changes direction but not the ratio: its teeth cancel out (A→I→B: I/A × B/I = B/A). Use one when the output must turn the same way as the motor.
Compound train Two gears fixed on one shaft turn together, so the stage ratios multiply: 12→36 then 12→36 is 3 × 3 = 9 : 1, from gears that fit in your hand.
Efficiency multiplies too Each mesh loses about 2 %. Two stages at 98 % give 0.98 × 0.98 ≈ 96 %. This lab's 92 % covers the whole hoist: gears, bearings and the drum.
Direction count Every external mesh flips direction. Count the meshes: an odd number reverses the output, an even number does not.

Total ratio = (driven₁ ÷ driver₁) × (driven₂ ÷ driver₂) × …

Worked example. The crate needs 883 Nm at the drum, the motor gives 420 Nm, and the efficiency is 92 %. The ratio must be at least 883 ÷ (420 × 0.92) ≈ 2.29 : 1. One 12→28 pair (2.33 : 1) just does it. For the 900 kg mission (1589 Nm) you need about 4.1 : 1: a single 8→36 pair, or a compound 12→24 then 12→28 (2 × 2.33 = 4.67 : 1).

When a gear pair is not the answer

Gears are only one family of mechanical drives. When the shafts are far apart, at a corner, or the job is a straight-line push, engineers reach for something else. Open each one running in the Drive Explorer:

Worm gear 40 : 1 in one stage, and many hold the load when the motor stops. A real hoist would likely use one, so the crate cannot fall.
Planetary gears Sun, planets and ring share the load in a small round box, with input and output on one line. Inside every cordless drill.
Chain and timing belt The same ratio maths with sprocket or pulley teeth, but the shafts can be a metre apart.
Rack and pinion and screws Turn rotation into a straight line: steering racks, 3D printer axes, car jacks.

Design sequence

Machine design is usually a loop: define the job, estimate the force, choose a mechanism, and check the trade-offs. The same loop is happening inside this lesson.

1. Estimate the load Start by asking how much force the machine needs to move the object at all.
2. Choose the ratio Match the gear sizes so the output shaft has the right balance of torque and rpm.
3. Check the motion Even a strong design can fail if it moves too fast for the task or for the material being handled.
4. Retune the system If the machine stalls or overspeeds, adjust the ratio and test again until the mechanism matches the job.

Check your understanding

1. A 10-tooth driver turns a 40-tooth driven gear. What is the ratio, and which way does the output turn?
40 ÷ 10 = 4 : 1 reduction. The output turns the opposite way at a quarter of the speed, with about four times the torque (less friction).
2. You add a 25-tooth idler between them. What changes?
Only the direction: the output now turns the same way as the motor. The ratio is still 4 : 1, because the idler's 25 teeth cancel out.
3. A compound train has stages 12→36 and 15→45. Find the total ratio and the output speed from a 1800 rpm motor.
3 × 3 = 9 : 1. 1800 ÷ 9 = 200 rpm.
4. Why might a real crane designer choose a worm gear instead of this gear pair?
A single-start worm gives a big reduction in one stage and is often self-locking: if power fails, the crate cannot drive the motor backwards and fall. The cost is efficiency (often 50–70 %). Try the hold test in the Drive Explorer.
