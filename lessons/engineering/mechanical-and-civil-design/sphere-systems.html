Engineering · Math in a real venue

Engineering an immersive sphere

Imagine designing a curved display big enough to surround an audience. How much surface must you cover? How much room is inside? Then how do images, air, and sound reach people in the seats?

Case study: Sphere's “The Science” and Sphere Studios' canvas descriptions. Venue figures below are the company's published figures. Our simulator is an ideal sphere, not a scale model or construction drawing.

Lab 2 · Information

How much can one channel carry?

Grades 9–12

Shannon–Hartley sets a theoretical capacity for a noisy channel: C = B log₂(1 + S/N). Here B is bandwidth and S/N is the signal-to-noise power ratio. Try doubling bandwidth, then doubling the ratio.

Ideal upper bound

At fixed S/N, doubling bandwidth doubles this bound. Doubling S/N gives a smaller gain because the ratio sits inside a logarithm.

This models one idealized channel with additive white Gaussian noise. It does not predict a venue's Wi-Fi throughput, number of simultaneous users, or latency. Formula reference: NASA technical report, equation 1.

Where the other formulas belong

Each route groups formulas around a question students can investigate. The named formulas come from Sphere's overview; the explanations here describe what the mathematics models, without claiming access to its engineering calculations.

01 · Structure

Will the shell stand?

Area, volume, π set the geometric scale. Geodesic triangulation approximates a curved skin; the law of sines helps solve triangle angles. A finite element model divides a structure into pieces to estimate displacement and stress under specified loads. A constitutive stress–strain relation supplies material behavior; it is not an airflow limit.

02 · Image

How does an image wrap?

Stereographic projection and other lens projections map directions to a flat image; spherical coordinates map that image to a curved screen. Visual acuity helps judge whether pixels are resolvable at a seat. Snell's law describes refraction through lens surfaces, the Sellmeier equation models how refractive index changes with wavelength in a material, and a Laplacian highlights rapid image changes such as edges. Focusing also needs lens geometry and distance.

03 · Air and comfort

What will an audience feel?

Venturi effects relate pressure and speed in a constricted flow. Navier–Stokes tracks fluid motion; the Cauchy momentum equation is its broader force balance. Fanger's comfort model estimates average thermal sensation from air, clothing, activity, and other inputs. Each needs real measurements and boundary conditions before it can predict a venue.

04 · Sound and data

What reaches each seat?

The wave equation describes propagation; Huygens–Fresnel adds contributions from wavefronts. The Helmholtz equation describes single-frequency fields. The Kirchhoff–Helmholtz integral and Kirchhoff integral theorem connect fields at boundaries to fields elsewhere. Shannon–Hartley gives an ideal noisy-channel capacity bound. Hartley's information measure counts choices; by itself it does not predict transmission across distance.

Exit ticket

A design team doubles the radius of its ideal shell but keeps display coverage at 60%. Explain what happens to display area and interior volume. Name one reason your prediction might differ from an actual venue.

Area becomes four times as large because radius is squared; volume becomes eight times as large because radius is cubed. A real venue is not a complete mathematical sphere, and actual display coverage and structural dimensions must be measured.
