Hydra-Matic: the gearbox that shifted itself

In 1939 General Motors put a gearbox in the 1940 Oldsmobile that no driver had to work. Take the wheel, floor the pedal, and watch fluid, steel and oil pressure decide when to shift.

The problem every gearbox solves

An engine is picky. It makes good torque only inside a band of speeds, it cannot start under load, and it must never stop turning while the wheels are still. A car, on the other hand, goes from 0 to 70 mph and sits at red lights. Something has to sit between them and trade speed for torque (the same trade you made in Mechanisms and Gear Systems).

Until 1939, that "something" was a gearbox you ran yourself: a clutch pedal, a shift lever, and a lot of practice. Hydra-Matic took all three jobs away from the driver. To do that it needed three separate ideas.

1A fluid coupling

Replaces the clutch pedal. The engine spins a fan of oil; the oil spins a second fan connected to the gearbox. No metal touches, so the car can sit still with the engine running.

2Two planetary gearsets

Replace the sliding gears. Each set has two modes, "reduce" or "locked", so two sets give 2 × 2 = four speeds, and gears change by squeezing bands and clutches, never by sliding teeth.

3A hydraulic brain

Replaces the driver's judgment. A speed-driven pressure and a pedal-driven pressure push on opposite ends of a valve. Whichever wins decides the gear. No electronics.

Job 1: the fluid coupling

Imagine two electric fans facing each other. Turn one on and the air it throws turns the other, with nothing touching. A fluid coupling is that, with oil in a sealed donut. The pump is bolted to the engine; the turbine is bolted to the gearbox. The pump flings oil outward by centrifugal force, the oil crosses to the turbine, pushes its vanes, and curls back inward to be flung again.

Torque on the turbine = K × (ωpump² − ωturbine²)
Torque on the pump = the same number. In = out. A coupling cannot multiply torque.

That equation holds three surprises. At idle the pump is slow, so the oil barely pushes and the car sits still. Rev the engine and the push grows with speed squared, so the car pulls away. And when both halves match speed the push is zero, so the coupling always runs with a little slip (the lost energy heats the oil).

Job 2: four speeds from two planetary gearsets

A planetary (epicyclic) set has three members: a sun gear in the middle, a ring gear with teeth on the inside, and a carrier holding three planet gears that mesh with both. Spin any two members and the third is forced to a speed. The rule that ties them together is the Willis equation:

S·ωsun + R·ωring = (S + R)·ωcarrier   (S, R = teeth on sun and ring)

Hold one member still, drive another, take the output from the third, and you get a fixed ratio. There are six ways to do it, and the planetary lab below will find all six. Hydra-Matic uses only two of them, one per set, plus a clutch that locks a set solid for a 1:1 "direct" drive.

Job 3: the hydraulic brain

Nothing in a 1940 Oldsmobile measured speed with a sensor or chose a gear with a computer. Two oil pressures did the arguing.

The governor says "faster"

Spinning flyweights on the output shaft let oil escape less as the car speeds up. Pressure climbs with speed squared: 20 mph makes 1.6 psi in our model; 40 mph makes 6.4 psi, four times as much.

The throttle valve says "wait"

A linkage from the accelerator makes a pressure that rises with the pedal. It adds to the shift valve's spring, so it holds the valve back: a foot down means "I want power, don't shift up yet".

The shift valve decides

When governor pressure beats spring + throttle pressure, the valve slides and redirects oil to the next band and clutch. Three valves, three shifts. Pressure to let go is lower than to grab, so the box does not hunt.

Kickdown. Push the pedal past the detent (about 90%) and the valve lets go of a gear at almost the pressure that grabbed it, so the box drops a gear the moment you need passing power. It is the same trick as a screen door's latch: the force to close it is not the force to open it.

The numbers behind the sim

How fast is the engine turning at 60 mph in 4th?
Road speed 60 mph = 26.8 m/s. The 0.34 m wheel turns 26.8 ÷ 0.34 = 78.9 rad/s ≈ 753 rpm. The final drive is 3.9:1 and 4th is 1:1, so the transmission turns 753 × 3.9 ≈ 2,940 rpm. The coupling slips about 3%, so the engine itself is near 3,030 rpm. (1940s cars cruised fast and high: this is why overdrive became popular later.)
How much torque reaches the wheels in 1st?
Engine peak 250 N·m. The coupling passes it on unchanged (no multiplication), then 3.97 × 3.9 = 15.5 multiplies it: 250 × 15.5 ≈ 3,870 N·m, or about 11,400 N of push at a 0.34 m wheel. In 4th the same engine gives 250 × 3.9 ≈ 975 N·m. That ratio of 4 to 1 is why the box needs a deep first gear.
Why did Hydra-Matic need four speeds when a modern converter car can do with fewer?
A fluid coupling cannot multiply torque; a modern torque converter can (up to about 2× at stall, by adding a stator that redirects the returning oil). Without that boost, the gearbox has to supply all of the launch multiplication, which takes a 3.97 first gear, and then the speed range up to 80 mph takes more gears after that.
A planetary set has a 30-tooth sun and a 70-tooth ring. What are its two reductions?
Planet teeth P = (70 − 30) ÷ 2 = 20. Sun in, ring held: ratio = 1 + R/S = 1 + 70/30 = 3.33 : 1. Ring in, sun held: ratio = 1 + S/R = 1 + 30/70 = 1.43 : 1. A small sun in a big ring gives the big ratio.
The governor makes 1.6 psi at 20 mph. What does it make at 60?
Pressure goes with speed squared and 60 is three times 20, so it is 3² = 9 times as much: 1.6 × 9 = 14.4 psi. This is why a flyweight governor is so good at distinguishing speeds: doubling the speed quadruples the signal.
The coupling is slipping 3% at 3,000 rpm and passing 46 N·m. What heat is made?
Slip speed = 3% of 314 rad/s = 9.4 rad/s. Heat = torque × slip speed = 46 × 9.4 ≈ 430 W. At a stall launch (turbine held, 250 N·m, 178 rad/s) it is 250 × 178 ≈ 44 kW: the oil turns the whole engine's output into heat, so a coupling should not sit at stall for long.
Where was Reverse — and why was there no Park?
The 1940 Oldsmobile selector read N – Dr – Lo – R. There was no P (Park) position until 1956 (GM added a mechanical parking pawl that locks a toothed wheel on the output shaft); drivers in 1940 set the handbrake or shut off the engine in R. Inside the box, a third planetary gearset behind the rear set held its carrier against the case with a reverse cone/pawl when you shifted to R, making the output turn backward (just like the negative ratios in Job 2's planetary table).

About the model

Tooth counts (front 22 / 40, rear 32 / 50) are teaching numbers chosen so the two sets reproduce the early-production ratios often quoted for Hydra-Matic: 3.97, 2.55, 1.55 and 1.00. The engine (about 90 hp, 250 N·m), car mass, final drive (3.9) and shift speeds are a teaching model loosely based on a 1940 flat-head six, not measurements of a real Oldsmobile. The physics (the Willis equation, the coupling law, pressure ∝ speed²) is the real thing. Real Hydra-Matics changed in detail from year to year.

The story

  1. Hermann Föttinger, a German engineer building ship turbines, invents the hydrodynamic transmission. It is the ancestor of every fluid coupling and torque converter.
  2. The Ford Model T ships with a two-speed planetary transmission worked by foot pedals and bands. It proves planetary gearsets can be built cheaply and by the million.
  3. General Motors offers Hydra-Matic on the 1940 Oldsmobile: the first fully automatic transmission in a mass-produced car. Four speeds, a fluid coupling, and no clutch pedal.
  4. The M5 Stuart light tank enters production with two Cadillac V8 engines and two Hydra-Matic transmissions. Automatics prove themselves under fire.
  5. Buick's Dynaflow uses a torque converter, which multiplies torque and needs fewer gear changes. Pontiac adopts Hydra-Matic the same year.
  6. GM's Turbo Hydra-Matic 400 combines a torque converter with a three-speed planetary gearbox. It becomes the model for automatics for decades.
  7. Eight-speed automatics such as ZF's 8HP reach production cars, controlled by computer rather than hydraulic valves, but still built from planetary gearsets.

The lineage is the lesson: the Willis equation and the pressure-balance valve in your sim are still inside the automatic transmission of a car built this year. What changed is the computer that now moves the valves.

Check your understanding

1. A fluid coupling has no clutch plates. Why can a Hydra-Matic car still stand still at a red light with the engine running in Drive?
At idle the pump is slow and the coupling's torque (K × ω²) is tiny. The brakes hold that small torque easily. Nothing is slipping against friction: the oil is just circulating with little force.
2. Name the parts that are engaged in 3rd gear.
The front set's band holds its sun (front reduction 1.55), and the rear set's clutch locks it solid (direct). 1.55 × 1 = 1.55 : 1.
3. You floor the pedal at 40 mph in 4th. Which of the two pressures changes, and what happens?
Throttle pressure rises. The valve's holding force (spring + throttle) is now much more than the governor's, so the shift valve slides back and a lower gear is selected: kickdown.
4. Why do the shift curves for a wide-open throttle sit at higher speeds than those for a light throttle?
With the pedal down, throttle pressure adds to the spring, so the governor must make a larger pressure, which takes a higher speed (pressure is 0.004 × mph²), before the valve moves.
5. In the sim, the turbine needle passes the engine needle when you coast. Explain it with the coupling law.
On the overrun the wheels turn the turbine faster than the engine, so ωturbine² − ωpump² is positive and the torque reverses: the wheels now drive the engine. That is engine braking, and it is weak, because the oil only passes torque in proportion to the speed difference squared.
6. Design challenge: how would you change the valve spring so 1→2 happens at 25 mph at wide open throttle?
Governor pressure at 25 mph = 0.004 × 625 = 2.5 psi. At wide open (10 psi of throttle pressure in the model) the valve needs spring + k × 10 = 2.5, so with k = 0.1152 the spring pressure would be 2.5 − 1.15 ≈ 1.35 psi, up from 0.144. In a real car the redline must also allow it: 1st gear at 25 mph is about 4,900 rpm, well beyond this engine's cutoff, so the engine's range, not just the valves, limits the schedule.
