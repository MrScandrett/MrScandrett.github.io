Mechanical Drives: how motors move things

A motor spins fast and weak. The thing you want to move is usually slow, heavy, far away, at an angle, or needs to slide in a straight line. The drive in between fixes all of that.

What a drive does

A mechanical drive carries power and motion from a source (an electric motor, an engine, your legs on a bike) to a driven load. On the way it usually changes one or more of four things:

Speed

A 3:1 reduction turns 1200 rpm into 400 rpm.

Torque

The same 3:1 reduction roughly triples the twisting force.

Direction

Reverse it, or turn the shaft 90° round a corner.

Kind of motion

Turn rotation into a straight push (a screw, a rack, a piston).

The one thing no drive can do is make more power. Power is torque times speed, so whatever you gain in torque you pay for in speed, and a little more is lost as heat on the way through:

Power = torque × angular speed   (P = T·ω)
Output speed = input speed ÷ ratio
Output torque = input torque × ratio × efficiency (η)

Try this, in order

  1. Spur gears: set the driven gear to 36 teeth. Predict the output speed before you look. Then check the torque: why is it not exactly 3 × 2 N·m?
  2. Switch to Helical gears at the same 36 teeth. Same ratio, so what did you gain, and what does the "side thrust" label warn you about?
  3. Worm gear, 40 teeth, 1 start. Run the hold test. Now change to 4 starts and run it again. What changed, and what happened to the efficiency?
  4. V-belt on "Loose": raise the motor torque until it says SLIPPING. Is slipping a bug or a safety feature? Try the same torque on the Roller chain.
  5. Find the drive that gives the biggest reduction in a single stage. (Hint: look in the gear family for one with a flexible part.)
  6. Ball screw versus Leadscrew at the same lead: compare efficiency, heat and the hold test. Which would you put on a 3D printer's Z axis, and why do some printers use each?

The four families

1 · Gear drives

Toothed wheels. Positive (no slip), compact, efficient.

  • Spur gears: straight teeth, parallel shafts, simple but noisy at speed.
  • Helical gears: angled teeth, smoother, quieter and stronger.
  • Bevel gears: cones that turn power through an angle, usually 90°.
  • Worm gear: a screw turning a wheel; huge reduction, resists back-driving.
  • Planetary gears: sun, planets and ring; compact, balanced, high torque.
  • Rack and pinion: a gear on a toothed bar, rotary to straight-line.
  • Harmonic (strain-wave) drive: a flexing gear, near-zero backlash, for robots.
  • Cycloidal drive: a wobbling lobed disc round pins; rugged and shock-proof.

2 · Belt & chain drives

Flexible links for shafts that are far apart.

  • V-belt: wedges into grooves; absorbs shock, slips safely when overloaded.
  • Timing belt: toothed, so shafts stay exactly in step.
  • Flat belt: smooth belt on crowned pulleys; fast, light, low torque.
  • Roller chain: links on sprockets, like a bike; strong, zero slip, long distances.

3 · Linear & screw drives

Rotation in, a straight push out.

  • Leadscrew: threaded rod in a plain nut; cheap, quiet, often self-locking.
  • Ball screw: recirculating balls; far less friction, more precision.
  • Planetary roller screw: threaded rollers; extreme loads and speeds.

4 · Friction, direct & fluid

No teeth at all.

  • Direct drive: motor straight to the load; no backlash, no wear.
  • Friction / traction drive: smooth wheels pressed together; stepless, as in some CVTs.
  • Hydraulic drive & pneumatics: oil or air pushes pistons; huge force, safe where sparks are not.

Quick comparison

Backlash is the little bit of free play between teeth: turn the input back and forth and the output does not move at first. It is what makes a cheap robot arm wobble at the end. Slip means the output falls behind the input.

Why some drives hold a load (and others drop it)

Unroll one turn of a screw thread and you get a ramp. Its slope is the lead angle λ. Friction gives every surface a friction angle ρ: the steepest ramp a block can sit on without sliding. If the thread's ramp is gentler than that (λ ≤ ρ), the load cannot push its way down. The drive is self-locking.

lead angle: tan λ = lead ÷ (π × diameter)
friction angle: tan ρ = μ (the coefficient of friction)
driving efficiency: η = tan λ ÷ tan(λ + ρ)    self-locking when λ ≤ ρ

The catch: a thread gentle enough to lock always wastes at least half its power to friction. That is why a single-start worm gear or a fine leadscrew holds a gate, a car jack or a printer bed, and why a 95 %-efficient ball screw on a vertical axis needs a brake. You cannot have both "very efficient" and "holds by itself" in one thread.

Narrow down the best drive

Engineers rarely ask "which drive is best?". They ask "best for what?". Answer two questions and see which drives fit, with the trade-off you are signing up for.

    Print a gear pair

    Make a 12:36 spur pair in Forge, mount it on two dowels, and measure the torque gain with a spring scale on a lever. Is your measured efficiency nearer 98 % or lower? Why?

    Lock test

    Hang a water bottle from a vise-style leadscrew and from a toy car's worm gearbox. Remove the handle or motor. Which hold? Measure the lead and diameter and check the lead-angle rule.

    Belt versus chain

    On a bike, count the chainring and sprocket teeth in each gear. Work out the ratio and how far one pedal turn moves you. Why would a belt-drive bike need a different frame?

    Print the whole family

    Forge's Drives shelf prints working herringbone gears, a bevel pair, a worm and wheel, a planetary set, a rack and pinion, a cycloidal drive and a leadscrew and nut. Print two, then test the explorer's numbers on the real thing.

    Choose for a robot

    Your robot arm must lift 1 kg at 20 cm with a 0.2 N·m motor. What ratio do you need (ignoring losses, then with η = 0.8)? Which drive gives that in one stage?

    Check your understanding

    1. A 12-tooth gear drives a 48-tooth gear. The motor turns at 1600 rpm with 1.5 N·m. Find the output speed and torque (η = 0.98).
    Ratio = 48 / 12 = 4. Speed = 1600 / 4 = 400 rpm. Torque = 1.5 × 4 × 0.98 = 5.88 N·m. The output turns the opposite way.
    2. Why can a worm gear give 40:1 in one stage when a spur pair would need a 480-tooth gear?
    A single-start worm moves the wheel by only one tooth per turn, so the ratio is simply the wheel's tooth count. A spur pair's ratio is the tooth ratio, so 40:1 from a 12-tooth driver needs 480 teeth.
    3. A planetary set has a 24-tooth sun and a 72-tooth ring. The ring is held. What is the ratio, and how many teeth do the planets have?
    Ratio = 1 + 72/24 = 4:1, sun in, carrier out, same direction. Planets = (72 − 24) / 2 = 24 teeth.
    4. Your 3D printer's bed drops when you switch it off. It uses a 2 mm-lead ball screw. Suggest two fixes.
    A ball screw is too efficient to self-lock. Fixes: add a brake (or keep the stepper motors powered), or swap to a fine-lead leadscrew (for example TR8×2, which self-locks), accepting more friction and some backlash.
    5. Why does a V-belt slipping count as a safety feature, while a timing belt jumping a tooth is a failure?
    The V-belt limits the torque so a jam does not stall the motor or break a shaft, and it recovers when the jam clears. A timing belt exists to keep two shafts in step; once it jumps a tooth the timing is wrong (in a car engine, valves can hit pistons) until someone resets it.
    6. A hydraulic pump makes 150 bar. What force does a 20 cm² piston push with? A 50 cm² piston?
    150 bar = 15 MPa = 15 N/mm². 20 cm² = 2000 mm² → 30 000 N (about 3 tonnes). 50 cm² → 75 000 N. The bigger piston moves 2.5 × slower for the same oil flow.
    Learning goals
    Source → drive → loadExplain what a drive changes (speed, torque, direction, kind of motion) and why it cannot change power.
    Four familiesName and recognise gear, belt & chain, screw, and friction/direct/fluid drives, with one real use of each.
    Ratios and lossesCalculate output speed and torque from tooth counts or pulley sizes, including efficiency and heat.
    Trade-offsUse backlash, slip, efficiency and self-locking to choose a drive for a stated job, and say what you give up.
