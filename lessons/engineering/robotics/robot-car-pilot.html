Engineering · Robotics · Teleoperation

Pilot the Robot Car From a Browser

Our robot car can't hear you. It only understands short typed messages. This page sends those messages for you. Push the joystick and the car rolls. Let go and it stops. Practice on the pretend car first, then take turns driving the real one!

Driving a robot from far away is called teleoperation. People use it to steer underwater robots. Mars missions need more autonomy because messages take minutes to arrive. This page is a remote control for the class's ELEGOO Smart Robot Car V4.0. It connects over Bluetooth or Wi-Fi and sends the same short JSON messages the official app sends. It also shows every message, so you can learn the robot's language.

Explorer · Story time

I only understand messages

I'm Bolt! I have four wheels, two eyes that hear echoes, and a little brain called an Arduino.

I can't hear your voice. When you push the joystick, the computer sends me a tiny message, like “go forward, medium fast.” I read it and spin my wheels.

When you let go, the computer sends “stop.” The Wi-Fi bridge can stop me when driving messages stop. Other connections need different safeguards. Keep a grown-up near my power switch!

  • Pilot · the person who drives
  • Message · a short note sent to the robot
  • Signal · how the note travels through the air
  • Sensor · a part that helps the robot notice things

Play “Human Robot Car” first

  1. Make message cards. Write FORWARD, BACK, LEFT, RIGHT, and STOP on cards.
  2. One robot, one pilot. The robot walks only when the pilot holds up a card. No talking! The robot does exactly what the card says.
  3. Steer through a course. Guide the robot around two chairs to a goal. What happens when the pilot is slow to show STOP?

Meet the car

What's on the ELEGOO V4.0

Our car has a brain, wheels, eyes, and a camera. The brain reads the messages. The other parts do the work.

The car is a stack of modules, each with one job. Every command you send ends up at the same place: the Arduino UNO's serial port. The UNO decodes it and drives the motors, servo, or light, or reads a sensor and sends back an answer.

Brain: Arduino UNO

Runs ELEGOO's factory program. It reads JSON messages at 9600 baud and acts on them.

Muscles

Four DC motors in two sides (left and right), run by a TB6612 motor driver. Speed is a PWM number from 0 to 255.

A servo motor turns the “head” left and right.

Senses

An ultrasonic sensor on the head measures distance with echoes. Three infrared line sensors look at the floor. A gyro keeps it driving straight.

Radios

The ESP32 camera board makes its own Wi-Fi network and streams video. Some kits also have a Bluetooth Low Energy module plugged into the shield.

Predict. Drive. Explain.

1 · Get your bearings

The simulator connects automatically. The car starts at x = 40 cm, y = 100 cm, facing right. Hold ▲ briefly, release, then press STOP. “Forward” means toward the car’s blue nose, not up the screen.

2 · Change one thing

Predict: will doubling speed double distance? Reset. Make a mission with just Forward for 0.5 s. Run at speed 100, note “from start,” then reset and repeat at 200. Keep duration and delay unchanged.

3 · Defend your result

Record speed, duration, distance, and one explanation. This ideal model is approximately proportional. Real motors have friction, battery changes, and wheel slip. Test the real car on a clear floor with a spotter and a ruler.

Crew roles: pilot holds controls; spotter watches the route and power switch; recorder notes evidence. Rotate each run. Before hardware driving, test STOP with wheels raised on a stable stand.

    How it connects
    Four ways to reach the car
    
            
    The message can fly through the air by Bluetooth or Wi-Fi, or go down a USB cable. All roads lead to the same brain.
    
            
    Every path delivers the same text to the UNO's serial port. The differences are range, setup, and which browsers can do it.
    
            
    
              
    Bluetooth
    Needs: a kit with the ELEGOO BLE module (BT16/HM-10 style), and Chrome or Edge on a laptop, Chromebook, or Android.
    The page talks to the module directly with Web Bluetooth. No extra software. iPhones and iPads can't do this.
    
              
    Wi-Fi
    Needs: a laptop on the car's ELEGOO-xxxx network running the bridge program.
    Gives you the live camera. Web pages can't open the plain network socket the car uses, so the bridge passes messages through.
    
              
    USB cable
    Needs: Chrome or Edge, the car's USB cable, and the car on a box with its wheels in the air.
    Good for testing commands at your desk. Unplug the camera board's cable if replies look scrambled.
    
              
    Simulator
    Needs: nothing.
    Runs the same commands on a pretend car. Twenty students can practice while one team drives the real car.
    
            
    
          
    

          
    
            
    The robot's language
    One message, start to finish
    
            
    The car speaks a small communication protocol written in JSON. N picks the command, D1–D4 and T are its settings, and H is a tag. The car puts that tag in its answer, so the page knows which question an answer belongs to.

    Engineer deep dive
    Safety, timing, and a bug in the factory codeAges 13+
    
            
    
              
    Dead man's switch
    A drive command keeps the car moving until something replaces it. If the laptop crashes mid-drive, the car keeps going. So the page repeats the drive command every 250 ms while you hold a control. The bridge sends {"N":100} if a full second goes by with none. This is a software dead man's switch, like the pedal a train driver must keep pressed.
    
              
    Heartbeat
    The ESP32 sends {Heartbeat} every second and expects one back. After about three missed beats it hangs up and stops the car. It also stops the car when no device is on its Wi-Fi. That's a heartbeat signal: silence means trouble.
    
              
    Why a bridge?
    The car listens on raw TCP port 100. For security, web pages may only use HTTP, WebSockets, WebRTC, and a few others, never raw sockets. The bridge is about 300 lines of Node: a WebSocket server on one side and a TCP client on the other. Read scripts/robot-car-bridge.mjs.
    
              
    Bandwidth
    9600 baud with 10 bits per byte is 960 bytes/s. A drive message is about 32 bytes, so four a second uses about 13% of the link. BLE modules accept 20 bytes per packet, so the page splits each message into two writes. The UNO waits for the closing }.
    
              
    Left is right
    In the firmware, N:4 calls its first value “L”. But it sends it to motor group A, which the driver code labels Right. Test it on blocks: send {"N":4,"D1":200,"D2":0} and see which wheels spin. Engineers trust tests over labels.
    
              
    Reading pauses driving
    Asking for a line sensor value (N:22) switches the firmware into “programming mode”. That stops line-following and any drive command. The page reads line sensors only while parked. The ultrasonic read (N:21) doesn't have this problem. Could you fix it in ApplicationFunctionSet_xxx0.cpp?
    
            
    
          
    

          
    
            
    Challenges
    Pilot's license
    
            
    Do these in the simulator first, then on the floor with the real car. Use tape and cardboard to build the course.
    
            
    Build a floor course with masking tape, black electrical tape, and cardboard walls. Each team practices in the simulator, then gets a timed run on the real car.
    
            
    
              
    Parking garage
    Turn on Read sensors. Park nose-in to a wall as close as you can without touching. Aim for a teacher-marked 20 cm clearance. Score the difference from 20 cm; touching the wall ends the run. The sensor is an estimate, not a collision guarantee.
    
              
    Slalom
    Weave through five cups placed 40 cm apart and back. Every cup knocked over adds 5 seconds.
    
              
    Human vs. robot
    Drive one lap of a black-tape loop by hand, then press Follow line. Who's faster? Who stays on the line better?
    
              
    Mars mode
    In the simulator, set the 3 s command delay. Plan a short move, wait for it to arrive, then observe before sending another. STOP bypasses this teaching delay. Mars rovers use planned commands and onboard autonomy; this is a scaled analogy.
    
              
    Blind mission
    Write a mission program that leaves the start box, goes around a cone, and returns. You get one run with no touching the controls.

    Tablets & many cars
    
                
    Each car has its own Wi-Fi, so pair one laptop with one car. To drive from a tablet, run the bridge with --lan on a laptop on the car's Wi-Fi. Then open http://<laptop-ip>:8787 on the tablet. Only one pilot drives each car at a time. A second device is turned away.
    
                
    If you reflash the camera board onto the school Wi-Fi, use --car <its address>.
    
              
    
              
    Safety & troubleshooting
    
                
      
                  
    • Drive on the floor, away from stairs and table edges. Start with Top speed at 150 or less.
      • 
                  
    • The page attempts STOP when it loses focus or becomes hidden. The bridge also has a watchdog. A frozen browser or broken BLE/USB link may prevent STOP from arriving: keep a spotter at the power switch.
      • 
                  
    • Car ignores commands? Check the shield's upload switch isn't on “upload”, and charge the batteries. Low batteries cause odd resets.
      • 
                  
    • The page on the class site (https) can reach ws://localhost. Chrome may ask to allow local network access. Choose Allow.

    Model & evidence
    What practice can tell you
    
            
    The simulator accepts the same command format, but its motion, line following, obstacle avoidance, and distance readings are simplified teaching models. It has ideal wheels, a flat room, and a single echo ray; it does not model battery sag, noisy echoes, or real firmware timing. A successful simulated mission does not certify a real car.
    
            
    Speed 150 is a PWM setting, not 150 cm/s. A timed square is an open-loop program: it never checks whether a corner really reached 90°. Use the position readout to measure the final error, then explain how sensor feedback could improve it.
    
            
    Space connection: NASA describes Ingenuity as an autonomous aircraft; NASA’s communication-delay analysis explains why Mars operations need autonomy. Hardware compatibility and stopping behavior must be checked on the classroom’s exact kit and firmware.

            Teaching notes
            
    One car, whole classEveryone practices in the simulator while one team drives the real car. Rotate every 5 minutes. The simulator obeys the same commands, so skills transfer.
    
            
    LaunchPlay Human Robot Car with message cards. Then ask: what if the STOP card arrives late? That's latency.
    
            
    SetupSee “Setting up the real car” on the page. For Wi-Fi: node scripts/robot-car-bridge.mjs on a laptop on the car's network. Try --mock first.
    
            
    LevelsExplorer: drive, follow line, run the dance. Builder: read the log, program a square. Engineer: deep dive, bandwidth math, and the left/right label bug.

    ExtensionStudents edit the factory firmware so N:22 no longer stops line-following, and test it on blocks.
