Gustave Eiffel

The Eiffel Tower Wind Load Wrought-Iron Lattice Prefabrication

Gustave Eiffel (1832–1923) built bridges, railway viaducts, and the hidden iron skeleton of the Statue of Liberty before the tower that carries his name. The Eiffel Tower went up in just over two years from 18,038 pre-made iron parts held by about 2.5 million rivets. Its biggest enemy was not its own weight but the wind, and Eiffel shaped every curve of it to beat the wind. It has stood since 1889.

Who Was Gustave Eiffel?

Alexandre Gustave Eiffel was born in Dijon, France, on December 15, 1832. His mother ran a coal business, and his uncle, a chemist, introduced him to science. He trained at the École Centrale in Paris, one of France's top engineering schools, and graduated in 1855, just as railways were spreading across Europe and needed bridges by the hundreds.

At 26 he was put in charge of building an iron railway bridge across the Garonne River at Bordeaux. He used compressed-air caissons to sink its foundations into the riverbed and finished on time, which made his name. In the 1860s he set up his own company. It became famous for designing structures as kits of standard iron parts, made and drilled in a workshop, then shipped and riveted together on site. Eiffel's firm built bridges in Portugal, Hungary, Peru and Vietnam, the great Garabit Viaduct in southern France, and, in 1881, the internal iron framework that lets the copper skin of the Statue of Liberty survive the harbour winds of New York.

For the 1889 World's Fair in Paris, marking 100 years since the French Revolution, two engineers in his company, Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, sketched a 300-metre iron pylon. Architect Stephen Sauvestre added the arches and decoration, and Eiffel bought the rights and threw his firm behind it. Famous artists signed a public letter attacking the "useless and monstrous" tower. Eiffel answered that a structure's beauty comes from fitting the forces it must resist, and that the curves of his tower's legs were the curves the wind dictated.

The tower opened on March 31, 1889, and for 41 years it was the tallest structure humans had built. Eiffel then turned to science: he used the tower for weather measurements and radio experiments, and built a wind tunnel where he studied air resistance on wings and propellers, helping early aviation. A financial scandal over the failed French Panama Canal project hurt his reputation in 1893, though his conviction was later overturned. He died in Paris on December 27, 1923, at 91.

The Engineering: Beating the Wind

Weight is the easy part. The tower's iron weighs about 7,300 tonnes (about 10,100 tonnes with everything else). Spread across four huge feet on concrete and masonry foundations, that load presses on the ground about as hard as a person sitting on a chair. What could knock a 300 m tower over is the sideways push of the wind, the wind load, acting high up, where it has the most leverage.

Wind pressure grows with the square of speed. Moving air pushes on a surface with a pressure of about q = ½ρv², where ρ (rho) is the density of air, about 1.2 kg/m³. Double the wind speed and the push is four times as big. Engineers multiply that pressure by the area the wind hits to get the force, and by the height it acts at to get the overturning moment, the turning effect that tries to tip the tower over one edge of its base.

Two tricks. First, a lattice: most of the tower is holes. Wind blows straight through the open grid, so it catches only a fraction of the force a solid tower that shape would. Second, the curve of the legs. Eiffel calculated the wind force on every level and shaped the legs so that, at each height, the lines of the edges point toward where the wind force on everything above them acts. The tower is wide at the bottom, where the overturning moment is biggest, and slender at the top, where it hardly matters. That is why its profile curves the way it does.

F = ½ρv² · Cd · A
ρ = air density ≈ 1.2 kg/m³ · v = wind speed (m/s)
Cd = drag coefficient (how blunt the shape is) · A = area facing the wind
Overturning moment = F × height where F acts

If the overturning moment is less than the weight × the half-width of the base, the tower stays put. The ratio of the two is the safety factor.

How much does the tower actually sway?
Very little. The tower's operators say the top moves only a few centimetres in strong wind. The sun has a bigger effect: iron expands as it warms, so on a hot day the side facing the sun grows and the top leans away from the sun by up to about 18 cm, and the whole tower can be around 15 cm taller in summer than in winter.
Why iron and not steel?
In the 1880s, puddled wrought iron was a material Eiffel's firm knew deeply and could trust; steel was newer and its quality less certain. Wrought iron also resists fatigue and rust well when painted, and the tower is repainted about every seven years with around 60 tonnes of paint.
Why build from 18,038 small parts?
Prefabrication. Every part was designed and drilled in Eiffel's workshop at Levallois-Perret, none heavier than about 3 tonnes, so small cranes could lift them. On site, gangs of four riveters (one heats the rivet, one holds it, one shapes the head, one hammers) closed about 2.5 million rivets. Doing the hard work in the factory is why the tower rose in about 26 months, and the same idea drives modern modular and 3D-printed construction.

Eiffel's Key Contributions

🗼
The Eiffel Tower (1889)

Tallest structure in the world for 41 years, built in about 26 months with a strong safety record for its day: guard rails, screens and moving platforms for the workers.

🌬️
Designing for Wind

Shaped a tall structure by its wind loads, a method behind every skyscraper and transmission tower since.

🔩
Prefabricated Kits

Standard parts made and drilled in the factory, then riveted on site: faster, safer and more precise.

🗽
Statue of Liberty's Skeleton

An iron pylon with a flexible framework so the thin copper skin can move in wind and heat without tearing.

🌉
Railway Bridges and Viaducts

Garabit, Maria Pia and dozens more, carrying railways across valleys on four continents.

✈️
Aerodynamics Research

Drop tests from the tower and wind-tunnel experiments that helped early aircraft designers.

Eiffel's Legacy Today

More than 135 years later, the tower still stands, now 330 m tall with its broadcast antennas, and it's among the most visited paid monuments on Earth. It was saved from scrapping by science: Eiffel let the army and radio pioneers use its height for wireless telegraphy, and in World War I its station intercepted enemy messages. It still carries radio and TV transmitters today.

The ideas outlived the iron. Engineers still design tall buildings by asking what the wind will do, still prefer open lattices where they can (look at any power-line pylon or crane), and still prefabricate parts so they fit together on site. In a parametric CAD model, changing one number and watching every part update is the same idea as Eiffel's drawings: one set of numbers controls all 18,038 pieces. Try it in Tinkercad Codeblocks.

Practice Problems

Think about wind, iron, and Eiffel's life.

Easy1. In what year did the Eiffel Tower open?

Hint: It was built for the World's Fair marking 100 years since the French Revolution (1789).

Easy2. What was the main force Eiffel shaped the tower to resist?

Hint: It pushes sideways, and it pushes hardest high up.

Medium3. Wind pressure is about ½ρv². If the wind speed doubles, how many times bigger is the pressure?

Hint: (2v)² = ? × v²

Medium4. Calculate the wind pressure in pascals for a 40 m/s wind, using ρ = 1.2 kg/m³.

Hint: 0.5 × 1.2 × 40 × 40.

Medium5. Construction ran from January 1887 to March 1889. About how many months is that?

Hint: Two full years is 24 months; then count January to March.

Challenge6. Why does an open lattice tower feel much less wind force than a solid tower of the same outline?

Hint: In F = q · Cd · A, which number does a lattice shrink?

Challenge7. Why is the tower widest at the bottom?

Hint: At each height, the structure below must resist the wind on everything above.
