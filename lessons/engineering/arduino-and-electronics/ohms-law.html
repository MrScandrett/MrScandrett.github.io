02 / Name what you saw

Push, Flow, and Squeeze

In the lab you changed two things and watched a third. Those three quantities are the whole story of a circuit: the push, the flow, and what slows the flow down.

V
Voltage (Volts, V) Voltage is electrical pressure — the push that drives electrons through a circuit. A 9V battery has more push than a 1.5V AA cell. Without voltage, nothing moves. Named after Alessandro Volta.
I
Current (Amperes, A) Current is the flow of electric charge — how much charge passes a point each second. Think of it like the volume of water flowing through a pipe. The symbol is I, and small circuits often use milliamps (mA).
R
Resistance (Ohms, Ω) Resistance is opposition to current flow. A resistor acts like a narrow section of pipe: higher resistance means less current for the same voltage. Measured in ohms (Ω), named after Georg Ohm.
The Water Analogy Voltage is water pressure. Current is how fast the water flows. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is. A wider pipe (lower resistance) lets more water flow for the same pressure. A higher pressure (more voltage) pushes more water through the same pipe. The analogy breaks down eventually (charges are not used up, and wires are not empty pipes), but it makes the relationships easy to feel.

03 / The rule

What Is Ohm's Law?

In 1827, German schoolteacher and physicist Georg Simon Ohm published the same experiment you just ran. He changed the push on a wire, measured the flow, and found the two rose together in a straight line, as long as the temperature stayed steady.

Other scientists did not believe him at first. Careful measurements won the argument, and today the unit of resistance carries his name.

Your data table showed the key idea: for one resistor, V ÷ I never changes. Call that constant R, and you have the law.

Solve for Voltage V = I × R Voltage equals current times resistance
Solve for Current I = V ÷ R Current equals voltage divided by resistance
Solve for Resistance R = V ÷ I Resistance equals voltage divided by current
One Equation, Three Unknowns You only need to know two of the three values to find the third. If you know the voltage across a resistor and its resistance, you can predict its current before you build the circuit. Real parts and supplies vary, so measure to check your prediction.

04 / Use it

Solve It

Every problem follows the same three moves: list what you know, convert to base units (V, A, Ω), then pick the form of the equation that solves for the unknown. Try each example on paper before you open the solution.

Ex 1 LED with a current-limiting resistor

Situation: You have a 5V power supply and want to limit current through an LED to 20 mA (0.020 A). The LED drops about 2V across itself. How large a resistor do you need?

Show solution

The voltage available for the resistor = 5V − 2V = 3V

Use R = V ÷ I

R = 3V ÷ 0.020A = 150 Ω

Answer: Use a 150 Ω resistor (or the next standard value up — 180 Ω — to keep the current safely below 20 mA).

Ex 2 Finding current in a simple circuit

Situation: A 9V battery is connected to a 470 Ω resistor. How much current flows?

Show solution

Use I = V ÷ R

I = 9V ÷ 470Ω = 0.0191 A ≈ 19.1 mA

Answer: About 19 milliamps flow through the resistor.

Ex 3 Measuring an unknown resistance

Situation: You apply 12V to an unknown component and measure 40 mA (0.040 A) of current with a multimeter. What is its resistance?

Show solution

Use R = V ÷ I

R = 12V ÷ 0.040A = 300 Ω

Answer: The component has a resistance of 300 Ω.

Ex 4 What voltage does a resistor need?

Situation: What voltage across a 2,200 Ω resistor would produce 5 mA (0.005 A)?

Show solution

Use V = I × R

V = 0.005A × 2200Ω = 11V

Answer: The resistor needs 11 V across it to carry 5 mA in this ideal model.

    05 / Why it gets hot

    Power: The Fourth Quantity

    Remember the resistor glowing in the lab at 12 V and 100 Ω? Ohm's Law deals with V, I, and R, but a fourth quantity goes hand in hand with it: Power (P), measured in watts (W). Power is the rate at which a component converts electrical energy into heat, light, or motion.

    Power from V and I P = V × I Watts = Volts × Amps
    Power from I and R P = I² × R Watts = (Amps)² × Ohms
    Power from V and R P = V² ÷ R Watts = (Volts)² ÷ Ohms

    This matters because every component has a power rating. If it has to get rid of more power than its power rating allows, the component overheats and can fail — sometimes dramatically. A resistor rated for ¼ W will burn if you run ½ W through it.

    Ex 5 Choosing a resistor by power rating

    Situation: The 150 Ω LED resistor from Example 1 carries 20 mA at 3V. How much power does it dissipate? Is a standard ¼W resistor safe?

    Show solution

    Use P = V × I

    P = 3V × 0.020A = 0.06 W

    Answer: 0.06 W — well under the ¼W (0.25W) rating. A standard resistor is fine. As a rule, keep components at or below 50% of their rated power for long-term reliability.

    Why Resistors Get Hot A resistor converts electrical energy into heat. Calculate its power before building, and choose a suitable wattage rating with margin. Disconnect power and let parts cool before touching a circuit that may have overheated.

    06 / More than one part

    Ohm's Law in Series and Parallel Circuits

    Real circuits have more than one component. The way components are connected — in series (one after another) or in parallel (side by side) — changes how resistance, voltage, and current behave.

    Series Circuits

    In a series circuit, components share the same current. The total resistance is the sum of all individual resistances. Voltage is shared — each component gets a portion of the total.

    Series Three resistors in series: 100 Ω + 220 Ω + 330 Ω, powered by 12V

    Total resistance: R_total = 100 + 220 + 330 = 650 Ω

    Current (same everywhere): I = 12V ÷ 650Ω = 18.5 mA

    Voltage across 100 Ω: V = 0.0185A × 100Ω = 1.85V

    Voltage across 220 Ω: V = 0.0185A × 220Ω = 4.07V

    Voltage across 330 Ω: V = 0.0185A × 330Ω = 6.11V

    Check: 1.85 + 4.07 + 6.11 ≈ 12V ✓ — the voltages always add up to the supply.

    Parallel Circuits

    In a parallel circuit, components share the same voltage. Each branch carries its own current. Total resistance goes down as you add more parallel branches — more paths for current to flow.

    Parallel Two resistors in parallel: 300 Ω ∥ 600 Ω, powered by 6V

    Total resistance: 1/R_total = 1/300 + 1/600 = 3/600 → R_total = 200 Ω

    Total current from supply: I = 6V ÷ 200Ω = 30 mA

    Current through 300 Ω branch: I = 6V ÷ 300Ω = 20 mA

    Current through 600 Ω branch: I = 6V ÷ 600Ω = 10 mA

    Check: 20 mA + 10 mA = 30 mA ✓ — branch currents always add up to the total.

    Property Series Parallel
    Current Same through all components Splits between branches
    Voltage Splits across components Same across all branches
    Total Resistance Adds up (always increases) Always less than smallest resistor
    One branch opens Entire circuit breaks Other branches keep working
    Common use Voltage dividers, LED current limiting House wiring, battery banks, power rails

    07 / Out in the world

    Ohm's Law in the Real World

    Every electronic device you own uses Ohm's Law — often thousands of times in a single circuit. Here are some places you will see it in action.

    LED current limiting An LED's current rises steeply with voltage. A series resistor limits that current; check the LED's own rating and subtract its forward voltage before calculating resistance.
    Voltage dividers Two resistors in series split a voltage proportionally. This is how sensors like potentiometers and thermistors produce a variable analog voltage that a microcontroller can read.
    Fuse sizing Fuses protect wiring from excessive current. Estimate normal and fault current, then choose a fuse for the wire, source, and expected startup behavior; Ohm's Law is one part of that analysis.
    Speaker impedance Speakers are rated by nominal impedance (often 4Ω or 8Ω), which changes with frequency. The simple DC resistor model is only a rough starting point for amplifier loading.
    Battery life estimation A 1000 mAh battery at a steady 50 mA suggests about 20 hours in an ideal estimate. Real capacity and current vary with load, voltage, temperature, and cutoff voltage.
    USB power budgets USB power has voltage and current limits that depend on the port and negotiation. An electronic device can change its current draw while it operates; a single fixed resistance cannot describe it.

    From Ideal Math to a Real Circuit

    Ohm's Law is exact for an ideal resistor at a fixed temperature. Real hardware adds tolerance, wire resistance, supply limits, meter loading, and components whose resistance changes. Good engineering uses the equation as a model, then checks the model with measurements.

    Source resistanceA battery's terminal voltage can sag under load because every real source has internal resistance.
    TemperatureResistors change slightly with temperature; lamps and thermistors change enough that one resistance value may not describe them.
    Meter loadingA voltmeter has a large but finite input resistance. On a high-resistance divider, the meter becomes another parallel branch and changes the reading.
    Supply current limitA bench supply may reduce its output voltage when a circuit requests more current than its set limit.

    Loaded Voltage Divider

    For two series resistors, the unloaded midpoint is Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). If a sensor, microcontroller input, or meter is connected to the midpoint, its input resistance sits in parallel with R2. Replace R2 in the formula with R2 ∥ Rload. This is why a divider can show the correct voltage with no load and collapse when it powers something.

    Design rule: voltage dividers are excellent for creating measurement signals and reference ratios. They are usually poor power supplies because the load changes the ratio. Use a regulator or converter when the output must deliver meaningful current.

    Bench Lab: Predict, Measure, Explain

    1. Choose a safe low-voltage source and two resistors. Measure each resistor with power off and record its actual value.
    2. Calculate total resistance, expected current, each voltage drop, and resistor power before connecting power.
    3. Build the series circuit. Measure supply voltage and both resistor drops with the voltmeter in parallel.
    4. Insert the ammeter in series only after moving its lead to the correct current jack and selecting a safe range.
    5. Compare prediction and measurement using percent error: |measured − predicted| ÷ predicted × 100%.
    6. Explain the difference using resistor tolerance, supply accuracy, contact resistance, and meter resolution.
    Never place an ammeter directly across a supply. In current mode a meter has very low resistance, so a parallel connection behaves like a short circuit. De-energize the circuit before moving leads or changing meter modes.

    Common Mistakes

    Forgetting to convert milliamps Ohm's Law requires consistent units. If your current is in milliamps (mA), divide by 1000 to get amps before plugging into the formula. 20 mA = 0.020 A.
    Confusing voltage across a component with supply voltage In a series circuit, each component has its own voltage drop — not the full supply voltage. Always calculate the voltage across the specific component you care about.
    Ignoring power ratings Calculating resistance with Ohm's Law is only half the job. Always check whether the power dissipated (P = I² × R) exceeds the component's wattage rating.
    Treating LEDs like resistors An LED is not a fixed resistance. It has a forward voltage drop that must be subtracted from the supply before calculating the current-limiting resistor value.
    Adding parallel resistances like series ones Two 100 Ω resistors in series give 200 Ω. In parallel, they give 50 Ω. Always use the reciprocal formula (1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2) for parallel combinations.
    Using one fixed resistance for every component V = IR describes a resistor at a given operating point. LEDs, lamps, speakers, and other parts have behavior that changes with voltage, temperature, frequency, or time.
