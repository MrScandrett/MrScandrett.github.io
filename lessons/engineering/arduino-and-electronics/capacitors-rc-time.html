Engineering · Components in Time

Capacitors & RC Time

A resistor reacts instantly. A capacitor takes time. It stores charge, and filling it through a resistor is never instant. That delay is how circuits keep time, smooth out shaky power, and ignore a bouncing button. To see it, you need a tool that draws voltage over time: an oscilloscope.

  • τ = RCThe time constant. Ohms × farads = seconds.
  • 63% · 5τOne τ gets you 63% of the way. Five τ is basically full.

The big idea

Two plates and a gap

Push current into a capacitor and electrons crowd onto one plate while the same number leave the other. The more charge piles up, the higher the voltage across the gap, and the harder it pushes back against the current filling it.

That's why charging slows down as it goes: at first the capacitor is empty and current rushes in. As it fills, the capacitor's voltage gets closer to the supply's, so there's less voltage left across the resistor, so less current flows. It approaches full but, in theory, never quite gets there.

Capacitance is measured in farads (F). One farad is huge. Real parts are usually microfarads (µF, millionths), nanofarads (nF), or picofarads (pF).

Water picture: a capacitor is a stretchy balloon in a water pipe. Water can't pass through it, but it can stretch and fill. The resistor is a narrow pipe that decides how fast it fills.

Meet the tool

Multimeter or oscilloscope?

A multimeter shows one number, updated a few times a second. That's perfect for a battery or a steady sensor. But PWM, a charging capacitor, a bouncing button, or a sound wave change thousands of times a second, faster than a meter can show. A scope draws voltage on the up-down axis and time on the left-right axis, like a graph that keeps redrawing.

Volts per division

The vertical scale. At 1 V/div, a trace four squares above the zero line is 4 V.

Time per division

The horizontal scale. At 2 ms/div, the full 10-square screen shows 20 ms.

Trigger

Tells the scope when to start drawing, such as “the moment the voltage rises past 2.5 V,” so a repeating signal holds still on the screen.

Classroom options

A small handheld scope costs about the same as a good meter. No scope? The Arduino Serial Plotter is a very slow scope you already own (see the code below).

Identify them

Reading a capacitor

Ceramic (disc or chip)

Small, cheap, not polarized. Usually 10 pF to 1 µF. Marked with a three-digit code, read like a resistor: two digits, then the number of zeros, in picofarads.

Electrolytic

A can shape, 1 µF and up. Polarized: the stripe marks the negative leg, and the longer leg is positive. The value and maximum voltage are printed on the side.

Film

Boxy, stable, not polarized. Common in audio and timing circuits where the value must stay put.

Supercapacitor

Whole farads in a coin-sized part. Low voltage (often 2.7 V) but it can run a small clock or keep memory alive for days.

Ratings matter: every capacitor has a maximum voltage. Choose one rated well above your circuit's voltage. For a 5 V project, 10 V or 16 V parts are typical. An electrolytic installed backward or over its rating can bulge, leak, or burst. Big capacitors can also hold a charge after the power is off, so discharge them through a resistor before handling, never with a screwdriver.

Sim 2 · Filter a bouncing button

An RC filter that ignores bounces

When you press a button, its metal contacts bounce for a few milliseconds before settling. An Arduino is fast enough to count each bounce as a separate press. In Inputs you fixed that in code. Here's the hardware fix: an RC low-pass filter. The capacitor can't change voltage instantly, so short bounces get smoothed away. Your goal: exactly 1 press counted, with less than 10 ms of delay.

The pins on an Arduino UNO have a built-in Schmitt trigger: the input switches to HIGH above about 3 V and only switches back to LOW below about 1.5 V. That gap between the two thresholds is what lets a slow, smooth RC curve register as one clean press.

Spot them in the wild

Five jobs capacitors do

Decoupling

A 100 nF ceramic right next to every chip's power pin. When the chip suddenly gulps current, the tiny capacitor supplies it instantly so the voltage doesn't dip. Look at any Arduino: they're everywhere.

Smoothing

Big electrolytics after a rectifier fill the gaps between the bumps of rectified AC, turning it into nearly steady DC. Motors on a robot need one too.

Timing

The famous 555 timer chip flashes an LED at a rate set by one R and one C. Blink rates, tone pitches, and delays all come from τ = RC.

Filtering

RC filters remove noise from sensors, debounce buttons, and in audio, act as bass and treble controls.

Energy bursts

A camera flash charges a capacitor slowly from a battery, then dumps it all into the bulb in a millisecond.

Real-bench lab

Turn your Arduino into a slow scope

Wire 5 V → 10 kΩ → A0, and A0 → a 100 µF capacitor → GND (stripe to GND). Run the sketch, open Tools → Serial Plotter, and press reset. You'll see the same curve as Sim 1, with τ = 1 s.

const int CAP_PIN = A0;
unsigned long start;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  start = millis();
}

void loop() {
  float volts = analogRead(CAP_PIN) * (5.0 / 1023.0);
  Serial.print("time_s:");
  Serial.print((millis() - start) / 1000.0);
  Serial.print(" volts:");
  Serial.println(volts);
  delay(20);
}

Check your τ: find the time where the curve crosses 3.16 V (63% of 5 V). It won't be exactly 1.00 s. Electrolytics are often ±20%, so anywhere from 0.8 s to 1.2 s is a healthy part. To discharge it again, unplug the 5 V wire and connect it to GND.
