Engineering · Capstone Project

Electronics Capstone: Design, Build, Prove

You've learned the pieces: Ohm's Law, dividers, capacitors, transistors, schematics, the breadboard, the meter, the soldering iron, and the Arduino. Now put them together the way engineers do. Start from a real problem, write down what “working” means, predict the numbers before you build, and prove your device works with measurements.

How the capstone works

Seven phases, one working device

Plan on two to three weeks, and keep an engineering notebook as you go. Work alone or in pairs. Each phase ends with a checkpoint: show your teacher your notebook before moving on. Your notebook below saves automatically in this browser on this device. Print it or download it to hand it in, and to keep a copy.

1 · Define

Problem, user, measurable requirements, constraints.

2 · Design

Block diagram, schematic, parts list, calculations.

3 · Power

Add up every current and pick a supply that lasts.

4 · Prototype

Breadboard one block at a time; predict, then measure.

5 · Make it real

Solder or perfboard it, check continuity, add an enclosure.

6 · Test & iterate

Test every requirement, log failures and fixes.

7 · Present

Demo it, defend one decision with numbers, reflect.

Pick a problem worth solving

Phase 3 tool

Power budget calculator

This is Kirchhoff's current law in practice: every part draws from the same supply, so the currents add up. Your power budget is that sum. List each part, look up its current in the datasheet or measure it with your meter, and see whether your supply can keep up and how long a battery lasts.

Battery life

Typical currents: Arduino UNO board itself about 45 mA; red LED with 220 Ω about 15 mA; SG90 servo 100–250 mA moving, up to 650 mA stalled; small DC motor 100–400 mA; piezo buzzer about 5 mA; HC-SR04 ultrasonic about 15 mA; most analog sensors under 5 mA. Battery capacities shrink when you draw a lot of current, so use only about 80% of the rated mAh.

Phase 4 tool

Predict, then measure

Before you power anything, write down what the meter should read at each test point. Then measure. A difference within 10% is normal: parts have tolerances. A bigger gap means you have something to explain, and explaining it is where most of the learning happens.

Where this fitsThe final lesson of the electronics curriculum. Students can start the skills check's Before column at the beginning of the module and return to it here.
Timeline (about 12 periods)Define + brief: 1. Design + calculations: 2. Power budget: 1. Prototype: 3. Make it permanent: 2. Test & iterate: 2. Present: 1.
CheckpointsDon't let a team pick up a soldering iron until Phase 2 (a schematic you've checked) and Phase 4 (a working breadboard with measurements) are signed off. Most failed capstones skip straight to building.
MaterialsStandard kit plus brief-specific parts: soil moisture sensor, TMP36 or DHT11, small DC fan, logic-level MOSFET and 1N4001 diode, sound sensor module, perfboard, and 4×AA holders.
EvidenceNotebooks are stored per browser. Collect the downloaded .txt or the printout. The predicted-vs-measured table and the iteration log are the strongest evidence of understanding; weight them in conversation during the presentation.
SafetyBattery and USB power only. Solder in a ventilated area, with eye protection. Any brief that switches a motor must include the flyback diode from Outputs.
