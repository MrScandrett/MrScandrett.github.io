Multimeter Lab

Electricity is invisible. A multimeter lets you see it. With one tool you can check a battery, find a broken wire, confirm a resistor’s value, and measure how much current an LED draws. To get a true answer, connect it the right way for each question.

The big idea

The meter becomes part of your circuit

When you turn the dial, you change what the meter is inside. That one fact explains every connection rule, and every way students destroy meters.

Why amps mode is dangerous: an ammeter is nearly a wire. Touch it across a battery and you have built a short circuit, with the meter as the short. The meter’s fuse blows to save it. The most common way this happens: you measure current, then forget to move the red lead back before your next voltage check.

Read the display

What the numbers are telling you

A meter never lies, but it is very literal. Most “broken meter” complaints are one of these six readings.

OL

Over limit

The value is too big to show. In ohms it means an open path: a broken wire, an unplugged part, or an LED blocking the meter’s small test voltage. In volts it means you need a higher range.

-5.98

A minus sign

The probes are swapped. Black is at the higher voltage. Nothing is wrong. On a DC circuit, the sign tells you which way the voltage points.

18.2 mA

Prefixes

An auto-ranging meter picks the unit for you. Read the small letters: m is ÷1,000, k is ×1,000, M is ×1,000,000. 18.2 mA = 0.0182 A. 9.93 kΩ = 9,930 Ω.

218.6 Ω

Not exactly 220?

Good. Real parts have a tolerance. A gold band means ±5 %, so 209 to 231 Ω is a healthy “220 Ω” resistor. Your measurement is more accurate than the color code.

0.27 V

Ghost voltage

A probe touching nothing can show a small, wandering number. That is static and stray pickup on a very high-resistance input. Trust readings only when both probes are firmly on metal.

0.000 A

Zero amps in a working circuit

Check three things. Is the meter actually in series? Is the red lead in the right current jack? Is the fuse blown? A blown fuse reads zero forever.

Troubleshoot like a technician

When it doesn’t work, measure, don’t rebuild

Pulling everything out and starting over throws away your evidence. Technicians follow a fixed order, from the quickest checks to the slowest. Try it on the mystery circuit above.

  1. Check the supply first.

    Red on +, black on −. A dead battery or an unplugged USB cable accounts for an embarrassing number of “broken” projects. It takes five seconds.

  2. Walk the voltage along the loop.

    Keep black on ground and move red, point by point, from + toward −. The voltage should step down only across parts that do work, such as resistors and LEDs. Across wires, it should stay the same.

  3. Look for where the voltage piles up.

    In a series loop, a break gets nearly all the supply voltage across it, because almost no current flows to drop it anywhere else. A good wire or joint reads about 0 V. If one reads volts, you have found the break. (With an LED in the loop, expect a volt or two less than the full supply. The meter’s own tiny current still lets the LED keep about 1.5 V.)

  4. Power off, then confirm with ohms or continuity.

    Check the suspect wire, joint, or breadboard row for a beep. Check the resistor value. Use diode test to check the LED’s direction.

  5. Change one thing, then measure again.

    If the measurement doesn’t change the way you predicted, your theory was wrong. That is useful information too.

Schematics label these spots as test points. See how to read them in Electronic Schematics, and practice spotting bad joints with continuity in Soldering.

Safety

Classroom meter rules

Low voltage only

In this class we measure batteries, USB, and Arduino circuits, never wall outlets or anything plugged into one. Wall power can injure or kill. Measuring it safely needs training and a properly rated meter.

Know the CAT rating

A meter’s measurement category (CAT II, III, IV) and voltage rating say where it is safe to use. A cheap meter with no CAT rating belongs on low-voltage hobby circuits only.

Inspect, then hold properly

Don’t use cracked leads or exposed metal. Keep your fingers behind the finger guards. Connect black first and remove it last.

Dial off the circuit

Lift the probes before turning the dial. After any current measurement, move the red lead back to VΩmA right away.
