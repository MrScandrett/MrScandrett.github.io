Lesson 03 · 70 minutes · Environment kit

Build rooms like construction sets.

Instead of modeling one enormous level, create a small kit of measured pieces that snap together. The result is faster to revise, lighter to reuse, and flexible enough to make several spaces.

02 / BLOCK

Design in gray

Prove scale and flow before materials.

  1. Set Blender units to Metric.Make a 4 m grid cell and a 1.8 m player reference.
  2. Create floor, wall, doorway, and corner blocks.Type dimensions numerically; do not eyeball matching pieces.
  3. Put origins on useful snap points.For wall pieces, a bottom corner makes grid placement easy.
  4. Duplicate a test room.Build at least two room shapes from the same source pieces.
  5. Check the player route.Door width, ceiling height, furniture clearance, and camera space must support play.
Blockout rule: If the gray room is not fun or legible, textures will not rescue it. Fix navigation, sightlines, landmarks, and proportions first.

04 / DRESS

Variation without clutter

Add a trim layer and landmark layer.

Keep structure reusable. Add separate baseboard, beam, pipe, panel, window, or damage modules for variation. Then place one landmark—a color block, large prop, sign shape, or lighting contrast—to help players remember where they are.

You want some walls to have pipes and others to stay plain. What is the most reusable approach?

05 / ENGINE

Assembly test

Build a playable room in Godot.

  1. Export kit pieces with consistent settings.Use one GLB containing named pieces or separate GLBs with the same coordinate rules.
  2. Instance pieces; do not duplicate source data.Reusable scenes keep later changes manageable.
  3. Snap transforms numerically.Translate by the module size and rotate in exact 90° steps where appropriate.
  4. Add simple collision.Use planes or boxes for walls and floors; walk every seam.
  5. Re-export one changed wall.Confirm the update flows through without rebuilding the entire room manually.
