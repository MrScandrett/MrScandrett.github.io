Big Idea

Element, compound, or mixture?

Everything in this lab is about how elements join to become compounds. A compound is not just atoms sitting near each other: the atoms are chemically bonded in a fixed ratio, and the result behaves nothing like its ingredients. Sodium is a metal that fizzes in water and chlorine is a poisonous green gas, yet bonded together as NaCl they are table salt.

Element

One kind of atom

O₂, Cl₂, N₂, Na, diamond (C). Two atoms can be bonded and still be an element, as long as they are the same element.

Compound

Different elements, bonded, fixed ratio

H₂O is always 2 hydrogen : 1 oxygen. The chemical formula tells you which elements and how many of each. Separating them takes a chemical reaction.

Mixture

Mixed, not bonded

Salt water, air, trail mix. A mixture has no fixed ratio and can be separated physically (evaporate the water, the salt is still there).

Ionic compounds

Metal + nonmetal → ions in a lattice

Electrons transfer, making a cation and an anion. Ionic compounds have no separate molecules, so their formulas give the ion ratio: NaCl means 1 Na⁺ for every 1 Cl⁻.

Molecular compounds

Nonmetal + nonmetal → shared electrons

Atoms share electrons in covalent bonds and stop at a molecule. In a molecular compound, CO₂ really is one C bonded to two O atoms.

Big Idea

Bonding can lower the energy of a system.

Many main-group atoms form bonds with 8 electrons in their outer shell: a useful pattern called the octet rule, with exceptions. Hydrogen is the special case: its first shell is full with 2 electrons. Metals tend to give electrons away more easily. Nonmetals tend to pull electrons toward themselves.

Shells

Valence Electrons

The electrons in the outermost shell control most chemical bonding. If that shell is nearly empty or nearly full, the atom is more likely to react.

Stability

Octet Rule

Main-group atoms often become more stable when their outer shell reaches 8 electrons. Hydrogen follows a duet rule and becomes stable with 2.

Pull

Electronegativity

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts electrons in a bond. A large difference often leads to ionic transfer. A smaller difference usually leads to sharing.

Outcome

Ionic vs Covalent

Metal + nonmetal often becomes ionic. Two nonmetals usually become covalent. Those different structures help explain why substances have different physical properties.

Properties

Why salt is a crystal but water is a liquid

Bonding behavior does not stop once the atoms connect. The way particles arrange in bulk changes melting point, state of matter, brittleness, and conductivity.

Ionic Example

Salt forms a crystal lattice.

In sodium chloride, positive and negative ions lock into a repeating three-dimensional pattern. That rigid ionic lattice makes salt a brittle crystal with a high melting point.

Covalent Example

Water is made of separate H₂O molecules.

Inside each water molecule, electrons are shared covalently. Between molecules, hydrogen bonding pulls them together strongly enough for a liquid, but not into the same rigid kind of ionic crystal lattice that salt forms.

Covalent Network Solid

Diamond is a continuous covalent network (and quartz, SiO₂, is a network compound).

Unlike H₂O or Cl₂, carbon in diamond never stops bonding to form separate molecules — every atom links covalently to four others, all the way through the crystal. That continuous network, not ionic attraction, is what makes diamond the hardest natural material.

Model limits: shell rings and electron transfers are teaching diagrams. Bonding depends on the energy of the whole system. Read more: OpenStax: covalent bonding and electronegativity.

Rule of thumb: if atoms become charged ions and build a lattice, expect crystal-like behavior. If atoms share electrons and stop at a molecule, expect lower melting points and states like liquids or gases. If atoms share electrons and never stop, expect an exceptionally hard, high-melting network solid like diamond.

Q1 Potassium (EN 0.82) and fluorine (EN 3.98) have an electronegativity gap of 3.16 — the largest in this lab. Why is that bond ionic and not just “very polar covalent”?

The gap size is a clue, but the deciding factor is what kind of atoms are involved. Potassium is a metal with a lone, loosely held valence electron; fluorine is a nonmetal one electron short of a full octet. In solid KF, the resulting ions are stabilized by attractions throughout an ionic lattice. Metal/nonmetal categories help predict this outcome, but they are not an absolute rule for every compound.

Q2 Hydrogen and fluorine also have a large electronegativity gap (1.78), yet HF is covalent, not ionic. What's different from the K + F case?

Both atoms in H–F are nonmetals. In HF, the atoms share electron density very unevenly in a polar covalent bond. HF is real proof that “large EN gap” and “ionic” aren't the same thing — element categories and EN differences are useful clues, not universal rules. Ionic and covalent bonding describe ends of a continuum.

Q3 Table salt (NaCl) melts at 801°C. Chlorine gas (Cl₂) boils at −34°C. Both contain chlorine — why such a huge difference?

NaCl is an ionic lattice: every Na⁺ ion is held in place by strong electrostatic attraction to several Cl⁻ ions around it, all the way through the crystal. Melting requires enough energy to disrupt the ordered lattice of strongly attracting ions. Cl₂ is a covalent molecule — the atoms inside each molecule are bonded tightly, but separate Cl₂ molecules only attract each other weakly. Melting or boiling Cl₂ just has to separate those weak between-molecule attractions, not break any actual bonds.

Q4 Diamond and table salt are both described as forming a “lattice,” and both are hard, high-melting solids. Are they the same kind of bonding?

No. Salt's lattice is ionic — charged Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions attracting each other electrostatically. Diamond's “lattice” is one continuous covalent network — every carbon atom is actually covalently bonded to four neighbors, with no charged ions involved at all. They look similar from the outside (hard, rigid, high-melting) because both avoid ever breaking into small independent molecules, but the bonding underneath is different.

Q5 Nitrogen gas (N₂) is so unreactive it makes up most of the air we breathe, even though nitrogen atoms are nonmetals that clearly do bond. What makes N₂ different?

The two nitrogen atoms in N₂ share three pairs of electrons — a strong triple bond. Reactions that break this bond usually face a large activation-energy barrier, so nitrogen gas reacts slowly under ordinary conditions.

Vocabulary

Bonding & compounds glossary

Blue words anywhere in this lesson can be hovered or tapped for a definition and pronunciation.

Element
One kind of atom only (O₂, Na, C).
Compound
Two or more different elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio (H₂O, NaCl).
Mixture
Substances mixed but not bonded; separable physically (salt water, air).
Molecule
A separate group of covalently bonded atoms (one H₂O, one O₂).
Chemical formula
Symbols + subscripts that show which elements and how many of each.
Subscript
Small number after a symbol counting that atom; none means one.
Chemical bond
Attraction holding atoms or ions together through outer electrons.
Valence electron
An outer-shell electron; the ones used in bonding.
Octet rule
Atoms tend to bond until they have 8 outer electrons (H: 2).
Ion
An atom or group with a charge from gaining or losing electrons.
Cation
Positive ion (Na⁺, Mg²⁺), usually from a metal.
Anion
Negative ion (Cl⁻, O²⁻), usually from a nonmetal.
Polyatomic ion
A charged, covalently bonded group acting as one ion (CO₃²⁻, OH⁻).
Ionic bond
Attraction between oppositely charged ions after electron transfer.
Ionic compound
Compound of ions in a lattice; brittle, high melting, conducts when melted or dissolved.
Covalent bond
Two atoms sharing a pair of electrons.
Molecular compound
Compound made of separate covalent molecules; often a liquid or gas.
Polar covalent bond
Electrons shared unequally, making slightly charged ends (H–Cl).
Nonpolar covalent bond
Electrons shared evenly (Cl₂, N₂).
Electronegativity
How hard an atom pulls on shared electrons.
Crystal lattice
A repeating 3D pattern of particles, like the ions in salt.
Network solid
One giant covalently bonded structure (diamond, quartz).
Lewis dot structure
Diagram of valence electrons as dots and bonds as lines.
Lone pair
An unshared electron pair on one atom.
Double / triple bond
Two or three shared electron pairs between the same atoms.
Hydrogen bond
Strong attraction between molecules via H on N, O, or F.
Molecular geometry
The 3D shape of a molecule (linear, bent, tetrahedral…).
