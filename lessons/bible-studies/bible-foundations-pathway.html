The mental map

Keep the whole story in view

The Bible contains many books, genres, voices, and covenants. These nine anchors are not a replacement for that richness; they are a simple map for remembering where each lesson fits.

  1. 01CreationGod creates
  2. 02FallSin fractures
  3. 03CovenantGod promises
  4. 04ExodusGod rescues
  5. 05KingdomGod reigns
  6. 06Exile & returnHope remains
  7. 07JesusThe King comes
  8. 08ChurchThe good news goes
  9. 09New creationGod dwells with us

The movement: God’s good creation is fractured by sin. God answers with covenant promise and rescue, forms a kingdom people, and preserves hope through exile.

The center: Jesus announces God’s kingdom, fulfills Scripture’s hopes, dies, and rises. The Spirit sends the Church with the good news toward every nation.

The destination: The story moves toward new creation—evil judged, all things made new, and God dwelling with His people.

The Bible through timeThe story, the text, and the believing scholars who carried it

Follow the biblical story (dates before AD 100 are approximate and some are debated among believers), how the books were gathered and copied, and the Christian scholars and translators who studied and translated them. For more on this, see How the Bible Reached Us.

  1. Abraham. God calls Abraham and promises blessing for all nations (Genesis 12). Dates are approximate.
  2. The Exodus. God rescues Israel from Egypt (Exodus). Believers date this c. 1446 BC or c. 1260 BC; the lesson on Exodus names both.
  3. David. Israel’s shepherd-king; the promise of an everlasting throne (2 Samuel 7).
  4. Solomon’s temple. God’s presence dwells among His people (1 Kings 6–8).
  5. Samaria falls. The northern kingdom ends under Assyria (2 Kings 17).
  6. Jerusalem falls. Babylon destroys the temple; exile begins (2 Kings 25; Lamentations).
  7. Cyrus lets the exiles go home. Return and rebuilding begin (Ezra 1).
  8. Jesus is born. The Word becomes flesh (Matthew 1–2; Luke 2; John 1:14).
  9. Death and resurrection of Jesus. The center of the story (Luke 24). Many scholars date this AD 30, others AD 33.
  10. Earliest New Testament letters begin. Paul and others write to young churches; Galatians or 1 Thessalonians come first, depending on the scholar.
  11. Temple destroyed. Rome destroys the temple, as Jesus foretold (Luke 21:5–6).
  12. Revelation written. The last book of the New Testament closes the story with new creation.
  13. Irenaeus defends the four Gospels. Bishop of Lyon; in Against Heresies he argues the Church has four Gospels, no more or fewer.
  14. Origen’s Hexapla. Origen of Alexandria sets the Hebrew Old Testament and Greek versions side by side in six columns, a pioneering work of textual study.
  15. Eusebius writes Church History. Records which books the early churches received as Scripture.
  16. Athanasius lists the 27 New Testament books. His Easter letter names the same 27 books Christians read today.
  17. Augustine’s Confessions. Bishop of Hippo; his sermons and commentaries shaped Western biblical interpretation for a thousand years.
  18. Jerome finishes the Latin Vulgate. Jerome learned Hebrew to translate the Old Testament from the original language.
  19. Bede dies. English monk and scholar; commentator on Scripture, and according to tradition, translator of John’s Gospel into Old English as he died.
  20. Aquinas writes the Summa. Thomas Aquinas builds a vast, Scripture-saturated theology.
  21. Wycliffe Bible. John Wycliffe and his followers produce the first complete English Bible, copied by hand.
  22. Gutenberg prints the Bible. Movable type makes Scripture affordable.
  23. Erasmus publishes the Greek New Testament. The first printed Greek New Testament; translators now work from the original language.
  24. Luther’s German New Testament. Martin Luther puts Scripture in the language of ordinary people.
  25. Tyndale’s English New Testament. William Tyndale translates from Greek; he gave his life for the work in 1536.
  26. Calvin’s Institutes. John Calvin, a careful biblical commentator, publishes his first systematic guide to Christian teaching.
  27. King James Version. Some fifty scholars working in companies produce the Bible English-speaking Christians would read for centuries.
  28. Matthew Henry’s Exposition. A devotional commentary on the whole Bible, still read.
  29. Tischendorf finds Codex Sinaiticus. Constantin von Tischendorf brings the 4th-century Greek Bible from St Catherine’s Monastery to the world’s attention.
  30. Westcott and Hort’s Greek New Testament. B. F. Westcott and F. J. A. Hort, Cambridge scholars, shape modern Greek text editions.
  31. F. F. Bruce: The New Testament Documents: Are They Reliable?. A widely read case that the New Testament is well attested.
  32. Bruce Metzger: The Text of the New Testament. The standard handbook on how the New Testament text was copied and recovered.

Design commitments

How these lessons should teach

Scripture leads

The interaction serves careful reading; it does not replace the biblical text or invent a new conclusion.

Context matters

Historical background clarifies the text while remaining distinct from what Scripture itself explicitly says.

Christ-centered, not forced

Whole-Bible connections should follow the text's real canonical relationships rather than attach symbolism arbitrarily.

Formation is not a score

Faith, obedience, and wisdom are not gamified as points. Students observe, reason, discuss, practice, and reflect.

Traditions are named fairly

Where faithful Christian interpretations differ, the lesson identifies the major views without disguising differences.

Wonder and truth belong together

Maps, timelines, models, and simulations should deepen attention, accuracy, humility, and worship.
