Game Design & Development · Lesson 1

What Makes a Game?

Game Design MDA Framework Mechanics Dynamics Aesthetics

Before you write a single line of code, great games start with a clear design. Learn the four ingredients every game needs, the MDA framework designers use to think about play, and build your own game concept — no code required.

The 4 Ingredients of Every Game

Game designer Bernard Suits defined a game as "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles." Every real game has four things:

🎯 Goals

A clear win or objective — reach the flag, score 10 points, survive the longest. Without a goal, there's no direction.

📋 Rules

Constraints that limit what you can do. Rules create the challenge — you can't just walk past the enemy, you have to jump over it.

📊 Feedback

Information that shows the player how they're doing — a score, a health bar, sound effects, screen shake. Feedback creates satisfaction.

🖐️ Voluntary Play

Players choose to play. This is what separates games from forced tasks. A truly fun game makes you want to keep playing.

Think About It: Take any game you know — Minecraft, tag, chess, or Mario. Can you name the goal, at least one rule, one form of feedback, and why people choose to play it?

The MDA Framework

Game researchers Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, and Robert Zubek created MDA in 2004 — the most widely used framework for understanding and designing games. It says every game can be analyzed at three levels:

⚙️ Mechanics The rules and actions written in code or on paper. "Press space to jump." These are what the designer controls directly.
→
🌀 Dynamics What actually happens when players interact with the mechanics. Jumping over enemies creates tension and timing puzzles — emergent behavior.
→
✨ Aesthetics The emotional response in the player — fun, excitement, challenge, curiosity, mastery. This is the real goal of game design.

Designers work top-down (decide the feeling first, design mechanics to produce it). Players experience games bottom-up (feel the mechanics, experience the dynamics, have the emotional response).

Super Mario Example: Mechanic — run and jump with momentum physics. Dynamic — timing jumps across gaps creates near-misses. Aesthetic — the thrill of skillful control and overcoming obstacles.

MDA Flow

Designers think M→D→A: choose a mechanic, predict what behavior emerges, aim for a target emotion. Players experience games in reverse: they feel the aesthetic first, before they understand the rules.

8 Aesthetic Types (LeBlanc)

  • Sensation — sight, sound, feel
  • Fantasy — make-believe worlds
  • Narrative — story and drama
  • Challenge — obstacle courses
  • Fellowship — co-op, social play
  • Discovery — exploration
  • Expression — self-customization
  • Submission — relaxing "flow state"

Game Jam Tip

Write your GDD before you code. Even a half-page document with goal, mechanic, and one obstacle will save you hours of aimless building during a jam week.

Key Vocabulary

Mechanic
A specific rule or action available to the player (jump, shoot, drag, combine).
Dynamic
Emergent behavior that arises when players interact with the mechanics.
Aesthetic
The emotional response the game creates in the player.
GDD
Game Design Document — a written plan describing a game's concept, mechanics, and goals.
Emergent Gameplay
Unplanned, complex behaviors that arise from simple rules — like Minecraft's infinite possibilities from basic block placement.
Core Loop
The repeating cycle of actions that defines moment-to-moment play: shoot enemy → collect loot → upgrade weapon → shoot enemy.
Scope
The size and complexity of a project. Good game jam design starts small and well-scoped.
