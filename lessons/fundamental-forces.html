Classical Mechanics · Forces & Interactions

Everything Pushes and Pulls — But How?

There are only four fundamental forces in the universe. Every push, pull, friction, magnetism, chemical reaction, and nuclear explosion you have ever witnessed comes from just four interactions. Understanding them reveals the structure of reality.

Idea 1 · The Four Fundamental Forces

All Forces in the Universe Reduce to Four

🌍
Gravity
Range: Infinite · Carrier: Graviton (hypothetical)
Weakest of the four forces — but it has infinite range and acts between all mass-energy. Dominates at large scales because it only attracts (no repulsion), so it accumulates. Responsible for orbits, tides, black holes, and the large-scale structure of the universe.
⚡
Electromagnetic
Range: Infinite · Carrier: Photon
10³⁶ times stronger than gravity. Acts between charges: attracts opposites, repels likes. Responsible for all chemistry, all materials science, all biology, all electronics, all light. Nearly everything you interact with daily is electromagnetic.
☢️
Weak Nuclear
Range: 10⁻¹⁸ m · Carriers: W±, Z bosons
Responsible for radioactive beta decay — converting neutrons into protons (or vice versa) with neutrino emission. Makes the Sun shine: nuclear fusion requires protons to change into neutrons via the weak force. Also responsible for neutrino interactions.
⚛️
Strong Nuclear
Range: 10⁻¹⁵ m · Carrier: Gluon
The strongest force — holds quarks together inside protons and neutrons, and holds protons and neutrons together inside nuclei. Overcomes the enormous electromagnetic repulsion between positive protons. Nuclear energy and atomic bombs release this energy.
Unification

At very high energies (early universe), the electromagnetic and weak forces merge into the "electroweak" force. Physicists hope to eventually unify all four into a single theory — the "Theory of Everything." So far, only three have been unified (Standard Model).

Idea 2 · Friction — An Emergent, Not Fundamental, Force

Friction Is Electromagnetic at Heart

Friction is not a fundamental force — it emerges from electromagnetic interactions between surface molecules. When two surfaces press together, their atoms' electron clouds interact, creating the macroscopic resistance we call friction.

Static friction (f_s ≤ μ_s · N): holds object in place. Can vary from 0 up to its maximum.

Kinetic friction (f_k = μ_k · N): constant force opposing sliding. Always less than max static.

Amontons' Laws (empirical)

1. Friction is proportional to normal force (N). 2. Friction is independent of contact area. 3. Kinetic friction is independent of sliding speed (approximately). These "laws" are approximations — at atomic scales, friction is extremely complex.

Types of Friction in Practice

  • Static: prevents initial motion — always ≥ kinetic friction
  • Kinetic/sliding: while surfaces slide against each other
  • Rolling: much smaller than sliding (why wheels exist)
  • Fluid/viscous: drag through liquids or gases — grows with speed
  • Air resistance: proportional to v² for fast objects
Triboelectric Effect

Friction can also transfer charge between surfaces — that's how a balloon rubbed against hair becomes statically charged (triboelectric effect). Friction is deeply electromagnetic.

Idea 3 · Molecular Forces

The Lennard-Jones Potential: Attraction and Repulsion at Atomic Scale

Between any two atoms or molecules, two competing effects create a characteristic force profile — the Lennard-Jones potential:

  • At long range: weak attraction (van der Waals / London dispersion forces) — electrons in one atom temporarily create dipoles that attract the other atom.
  • At short range: strong repulsion — electron clouds of both atoms overlap, causing enormous Pauli exclusion repulsion.

The balance between these creates an equilibrium distance — where force = 0. This is the bond length in molecules, and explains why solids have definite crystal structures.

Why Solids Are Solid

Compress a solid: atoms repel → you feel hardness. Stretch a solid: atoms attract → you feel tension. The equilibrium is extremely sharp — even small displacements create large restoring forces. This is why materials have definite elastic moduli (Young's modulus).

From Molecular Forces to Thermodynamics

Temperature is the average kinetic energy of atoms vibrating about their equilibrium positions. Heat a solid enough and atoms gain enough energy to escape the potential well — the material melts. Heat more and they escape molecular attraction entirely — it evaporates.

Idea 4 · Pseudo Forces — Forces That Aren't Really There

Centrifugal, Coriolis, and Other Fictitious Forces

Newton's Laws only work in inertial reference frames — frames that aren't accelerating. In a rotating or accelerating frame, we experience apparent forces that don't really exist — they're artifacts of the frame's acceleration.

Centrifugal Force

Standing inside a rotating space station, you feel pushed outward — "centrifugal force." But from an inertial frame (outside looking in), there is no outward force. You're simply moving in a circle, and your inertia wants to continue straight — the wall pushes you inward.

Coriolis Force

On a rotating Earth, moving objects appear to curve — right in the Northern Hemisphere, left in the Southern. This "Coriolis force" makes hurricanes spin, deflects long-range missiles, and creates ocean gyres. It's not a real force — just the appearance of curved motion from a rotating frame.

Einstein's Equivalence Principle

Einstein noticed something profound: gravity and accelerating frames are locally indistinguishable. You can't tell (locally) whether you're in a rocket accelerating at 9.8 m/s² or sitting on Earth's surface. This equivalence led Einstein to general relativity — gravity as curved spacetime.

Idea 5 · Nuclear Forces

The Force That Powers Stars and Splits Atoms

The strong nuclear force is nature's most powerful — roughly 100 times stronger than electromagnetism at nuclear distances. It must be: it has to overcome the enormous electromagnetic repulsion between protons packed into a tiny nucleus.

The strong force is charge-blind — it acts equally on protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons). It's mediated by the exchange of particles called gluons between quarks, which effectively creates a "residual" force between nucleons.

Binding Energy and E = mc²

A nucleus weighs less than the sum of its individual protons and neutrons. This "mass defect" is converted to binding energy via E = mc². The binding energy per nucleon peaks at Iron-56 — the most stable nucleus.

  • Fission: heavy nuclei (uranium, plutonium) split → binding energy released
  • Fusion: light nuclei (hydrogen) combine → binding energy released
  • Both release energy because products have higher binding energy per nucleon
Why the Sun Shines

The Sun fuses 620 million tons of hydrogen per second, converting it to helium. The mass defect — about 0.7% of the hydrogen mass — becomes the photons and neutrinos that light and warm our planet. The same process in a thermonuclear weapon happens in microseconds.
