3. Headset microphones and wireless receivers

Wireless does not remove the signal chain—it replaces one cable with a radio link. The microphone still creates an audio signal, the transmitter sends it by radio frequency (RF), and the receiver converts it back to audio for the mixer.

Place the headset

  • Position the capsule beside the corner of the mouth—about 2–3 cm away—not directly in the breath stream.
  • Use a foam windscreen and secure the cable without pulling it tight across the skin.
  • Most theatre headsets are omni for consistent tone; directional models can improve gain before feedback but are more placement-sensitive.

Build a reliable RF link

  • Match the transmitter and receiver to the same compatible frequency; give every active system its own coordinated channel.
  • Keep receiver antennas in clear line of sight and away from metal racks, network gear, and LED power supplies when possible.
  • Set bodypack input gain for the performer’s loudest voice without clipping, then set the receiver output for a healthy mixer signal.
Diagnose by meter: No RF? Check power, frequency, antennas, distance, and obstruction. RF but no audio? Check mute, headset connector, capsule, and transmitter input. Audio clips? Lower transmitter input gain before lowering the mixer fader.

4. Production placement challenge

Choose a production brief, then adjust the pattern, angles, and distance to satisfy it.

5. Explain your engineering choice

  1. Name the pattern and describe where its strongest rejection occurs.
  2. Identify the wanted source, the loudest unwanted source, and the path between them.
  3. Predict what happens if the performer doubles their distance from the microphone.
  4. Sketch a safe monitor position and justify it using the polar plot—not “because it sounds better.”

Core model

Cardioid: strongest in front and rejects the rear. A common live-vocal choice.

Omni: equal in every direction. Natural sound, little directional rejection.

Figure-eight: front and rear pickup with deep side nulls—useful for two speakers or mid-side recording.

Shotgun: a narrow forward lobe with smaller rear/side lobes. Directional, but not a telescope.

Sound pressure ∝ 1 / distance
Sound intensity ∝ 1 / distance²

In a free field, doubling distance halves sound pressure (and quarters intensity), so a microphone’s direct-sound level falls by about 6 dB.
