How to read the table

The periodic table is arranged by atomic number. That means every step to the right adds one proton, and the layout reveals repeating patterns in chemical behavior.

Periods

Rows are called periods. As you move across a row, atomic number increases and properties shift in a regular way.

Groups

Columns are groups. Elements in the same group often have similar outer electron patterns, so they react in related ways.

Families

Labels like alkali metals, halogens, and noble gases help chemists predict reactivity, bonding, and other shared traits.

Patterns

Metals mostly appear on the left, nonmetals cluster on the right, and the staircase region between them holds the metalloids.

History and evolution of the periodic table

Dmitri Mendeleev is usually called the founder of the periodic table because in 1869 he arranged known elements by repeating properties, left gaps for missing elements, and made predictions that were later confirmed. This lesson focuses on how elements are organized; the companion Atoms lesson focuses on protons, neutrons, electrons, and why atomic number gives each element its identity.

Before chemistry

People first sorted matter by experience

Early cultures knew metals, salts, pigments, stone, charcoal, sulfur, glass, medicines, and poisons long before they knew atoms. The first element systems were attempts to explain change: burning, melting, rusting, dissolving, healing, and decay.

Greek four elements

Earth, water, air, and fire

The familiar four-element model is Greek, especially associated with Empedocles and later Aristotle. It treated visible behaviors as combinations of basic qualities such as hot, cold, wet, and dry, not as modern chemical elements.

EarthWaterAirFire
Ancient China

Wood, fire, earth, metal, and water

Ancient Chinese thought often used Wu Xing, a five-part cycle of phases or agents. Wood, fire, earth, metal, and water described change in nature, medicine, seasons, music, politics, and cosmology. In this lesson, treat it as a different historical framework for explaining matter and change.

WoodFireEarthMetalWater
Egypt to alchemy

Alchemy grew from craft, mystery, and metalwork

Alchemy became popular because it joined practical workshop knowledge with spiritual symbolism: dyeing, glassmaking, medicine, metallurgy, and the dream of transforming matter. The word reaches English through Arabic al-kimiya; older writers connected khemia with Egypt, sometimes called Khem, the black land of the Nile.

Philosopher's stone

The myth of perfect transformation

The philosopher's stone was imagined as a hidden substance that could turn base metals such as lead into gold and sometimes produce an elixir of life. The myth mattered because it kept generations experimenting with furnaces, acids, minerals, distillation, and purification.

Modern chemistry

Elements became testable substances

By the 1600s and 1700s, chemists increasingly demanded measurement and repeatable tests. Boyle challenged old element lists; Lavoisier defined elements as substances not decomposed by chemical methods; Dalton connected elements to atoms; Mendeleev organized them into a predictive table.

Ancient to 1700sElements as basic substances

Carbon, sulfur, copper, silver, tin, gold, mercury, lead, and iron were known long before modern chemistry, but there was no reliable atomic explanation yet.

1789Lavoisier lists elements

Antoine Lavoisier helped replace alchemy with chemistry by defining elements as substances that could not be decomposed by chemical methods.

1829Dobereiner triads

Johann Dobereiner noticed groups of three elements with related properties, an early clue that element behavior followed a pattern.

1864Newlands octaves

John Newlands arranged elements by atomic mass and noticed that properties repeated roughly every eighth element.

1869Mendeleev predicts gaps

Mendeleev organized elements by mass and chemical behavior, then boldly left empty spaces for elements not yet discovered.

1875-1886Predictions confirmed

Gallium, scandium, and germanium matched Mendeleev predictions, making the table a powerful scientific model instead of a memorization chart.

1913Moseley orders by protons

Henry Moseley showed that atomic number, not atomic mass, is the true organizing principle. Each step adds one proton.

1940s-todayModern synthetic elements

Seaborg reorganized the actinides, and laboratories extended the table to superheavy elements such as nihonium, moscovium, tennessine, and oganesson.
