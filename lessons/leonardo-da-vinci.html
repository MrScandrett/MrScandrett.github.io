Leonardo da Vinci

Renaissance Polymath Flying Machines Anatomy Codex STEAM Pioneer

Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) was the original STEAM mind. Painter, sculptor, architect, musician, mathematician, engineer, inventor, anatomist, geologist, botanist, and writer — he pursued every field with equal obsession, filling over 13,000 pages of notebooks with mirror-script observations, inventions, and diagrams that were five centuries ahead of their time. His flying machines anticipated aviation. His anatomical drawings weren't surpassed for 400 years. His art redefined what painting could say about light, emotion, and the human form.

Who Was Leonardo da Vinci?

Born April 15, 1452, in Anchiano, a small hamlet near Vinci in Tuscany, Italy. He was the illegitimate son of a Florentine notary, Ser Piero da Vinci, and a peasant woman named Caterina. Because he was illegitimate, he could not attend a university or enter the guilds for law or medicine — which may be why he became an artist rather than a scholar, and why he questioned every received authority rather than deferring to it.

At 14 he apprenticed in Florence under the painter Andrea del Verrocchio. By 20 he had surpassed his master. He worked in Florence under Lorenzo de' Medici, then moved to Milan in 1482 to serve Ludovico Sforza — pitching himself not as a painter but as a military engineer, hydraulics expert, and architect. He spent 17 years in Milan, during which he painted The Last Supper, began the notebooks in earnest, and dissected at least 30 human corpses at the Hospital of Santa Maria Nuova.

His notebooks — the Codex Atlanticus, Codex Leicester, Codex Arundel, and others — contain designs for a helicopter, a solar power concentrator, an armored vehicle, a calculator, a robot knight, plate tectonics observations, and the first correct description of arteriosclerosis. He wrote in mirror script (right to left) with his left hand, possibly to prevent ink smearing or to keep his ideas private. They were not widely studied until centuries after his death — meaning his ideas had almost zero direct influence on the science that followed.

In 1516 he accepted an invitation from King Francis I of France to live at the Château du Clos Lucé near Amboise. He was 64, and his right hand had been paralysed by a stroke, ending his painting. He died on May 2, 1519, reportedly in the arms of Francis I. He left no direct heirs; his notebooks passed to his pupil Francesco Melzi, and eventually scattered across Europe.

The Notebooks: 13,000 Pages of the Future

Leonardo filled over 13,000 pages across dozens of notebooks — the largest surviving record of a single mind at work in history.

He wrote in mirror script: right to left, with his left hand, so text can only be read by holding it to a mirror. Theories: left-handed smear avoidance, privacy, or habit formed in childhood.

The notebooks were never published in his lifetime; most were not studied until the 19th and 20th centuries — meaning his designs for helicopters, solar concentrators, and armoured vehicles had no direct influence on later inventors.

Major Codices

Codex Atlanticus (1478–1519) — 1,119 pages; machines, weapons, musical instruments, optics, mathematics — largest of his notebooks.

Codex Leicester (1508–1510) — 72 pages; geology, water flow, astronomy, fossils; bought by Bill Gates in 1994 for $30.8 million.

Codex on the Flight of Birds (1505) — 18 pages; systematic study of how birds fly, with design notes for a flying machine.

Windsor Anatomical Folios — 200+ anatomical drawings; described circulation, the heart as a pump, and arteriosclerosis.

Plate tectonics, 400 years early: Leonardo described the idea that continents drift and mountains were once sea floors — 400 years before Alfred Wegener's 1912 theory. He found marine fossils in Alpine rocks and reasoned correctly that the land had risen from under an ancient sea.
The fetus in the womb: His anatomical drawing of the fetus in the womb (c. 1511) remained the most accurate depiction for over 400 years. He described the heart as 'an instrument of marvellous workmanship' and correctly identified it as a pump — not the seat of the soul, as contemporaries believed.
Fluid dynamics: Leonardo's mirror-script notes on the Codex Leicester describe water flowing in spirals and eddies — observations he used to design canal systems and that were later independently replicated by 20th-century fluid dynamics researchers.

Leonardo's STEAM Contributions

🔬
Science

Described plate tectonics, studied geology, astronomy, optics, and fluid dynamics. Correct on the shape of the Earth's geology 400 years before modern geology confirmed it.

🔧
Technology

Designed a robot knight (1495), solar power concentrator, printing press improvements, and a mechanical calculator — all from notebooks never published in his lifetime.

✈️
Engineering

Designed flying machines, hang gliders, hydraulic canals, bridges, military fortifications, and the world's first armoured vehicle prototype. Many designs are structurally sound and have since been built.

🎨
Art

Invented sfumato (gradual tonal blending), aerial perspective, and the use of light and shadow (chiaroscuro) as compositional structure. The Mona Lisa and The Last Supper remain two of the most studied paintings in history.

📐
Mathematics

Illustrated Luca Pacioli's De Divina Proportione (1509) — a study of the golden ratio. Used geometry and proportion systematically in both his art and engineering designs.

🫀
Anatomy

Dissected 30+ human corpses. Produced over 200 anatomical drawings describing muscles, tendons, the heart, fetal development, and the vascular system — unmatched for 400 years.

Leonardo's Legacy Today

Aerospace engineering — His Codex on the Flight of Birds described wing camber, centre of gravity, and the physics of gliding; the Wright Brothers referenced similar principles 400 years later.

Medical imaging — His anatomical cross-section technique (slicing the body into layers) is the direct conceptual precursor to MRI and CT scan imaging.

Colour theory — His description of how shadows contain colour (not just darkness) and how objects reflect colour onto adjacent surfaces is foundational to colour theory in photography, film, and graphic design.

Design thinking — His practice of rapid sketching, iterating, and annotating concepts is recognised as the origin of modern engineering design methodology.

The Vitruvian Man is the most reproduced artwork in history and the universal symbol of the relationship between art, science, and the human body — it appears on the Italian one-euro coin.

The golden ratio (φ = 1.618...) appears throughout his paintings as a compositional tool — the Mona Lisa's face fits within a golden rectangle.

φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.618

The golden ratio: the proportion Leonardo embedded in the Vitruvian Man, the Mona Lisa, and dozens of architectural drawings.

Practice Problems

Proportion, geometry, and the physics of flight.

Easy1. In which country was Leonardo da Vinci born?

Hint: Leonardo was born in Anchiano, near the town of Vinci, in the region of Tuscany — in present-day Italy.

Easy2. What unique writing style did Leonardo use in his notebooks?

Hint: Leonardo wrote right to left with his left hand. The text can only be read normally by holding it up to a mirror — which is why it's called this.

Medium3. The golden ratio φ = (1 + √5) / 2. Using √5 ≈ 2.236, calculate φ to 3 decimal places.

Hint: φ = (1 + 2.236) / 2 = 3.236 / 2 = 1.618. This ratio appears throughout nature, architecture, and Leonardo's compositions.

Medium4. Leonardo's Vitruvian Man states that arm span equals height. If a person's arm span is 172 cm, what is their predicted height in cm according to this rule?

Hint: According to the Vitruvian Man, height = arm span. If the arm span is 172 cm, the predicted height is also 172 cm.

Challenge5. Why were Leonardo's inventions largely unknown to scientists who came after him?

Hint: After Leonardo died, his pupil Francesco Melzi inherited the notebooks. After Melzi's death they were dispersed across Europe. Most were not systematically studied until the 1800s and 1900s — meaning his helicopter was published in 1797, his anatomy in 1898.
