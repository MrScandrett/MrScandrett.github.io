Experiment 2 / Additive color

Build light from darkness.

Predict the overlap of red and green light. Turn blue off to test it. Then bring all three channels to full strength.

RGB

A screen is a light source

Center mixture: #FFFFFF

An idealized RGB screen model: sliders are 8-bit sRGB channel codes, not measured lamp power. The circles combine separate channels; this is not a paint simulation.

Why doesn’t paint mix this way?

Light sources add light. Pigments absorb some wavelengths and scatter others. Cyan, magenta, and yellow are ideal subtractive primaries; printing also uses black. Real paint mixtures depend on the pigments, illumination, and amounts. An RGB wheel cannot predict a paint recipe.

Experiment 3 / Hue, saturation & value

Give colors a relationship.

Rotate a hue and its partners follow. These are geometric recipes on an RGB hue wheel, not rules for beauty or promises about emotion.

RGB hue24°

Hue is an angle, saturation is distance from gray in this model, and value is the largest RGB channel as a percentage. Equal HSV value does not mean equal perceived brightness. At zero saturation, hue has no visible effect.

Keep one palette, change one slider, and compare.

Try a fair comparison

Choose complementary. Keep saturation and value fixed, and move hue from 0° to 60°. Red pairs with cyan; yellow pairs with blue. Notice how their apparent brightness differs. This digital wheel differs from the traditional red–yellow–blue artist’s wheel.

For monochromatic palettes, the three swatches use one hue with 100%, 65%, and 35% of your selected HSV value. Other schemes keep saturation and value identical so you can isolate hue relationships.

Experiment 4 / Evidence for a design decision

Beautiful. But can you read it?

Different hues can still have low luminance contrast. Change the actual text and background colors, then compare the measurement with your reading experience.

Behind the experiments

This lab keeps a fixed neutral viewing surface so site themes do not change the experiments. These demonstrations model screen color, not spectra or calibrated physical measurements. Screens, room lighting, and color vision affect what you see. The numerical RGB values and contrast calculations let you separate a repeatable model from a personal observation.
