Antoni Gaudí

La Sagrada Família Hanging Chain Models Catenary Arches Physics as Architecture

Antoni Gaudí (1852–1926) designed buildings that look as if they grew instead of being built. Behind the curves was physics. Gaudí hung strings loaded with little bags of lead shot, let gravity pull them into shape, and then turned the shape upside down. A hanging chain is pulled only in tension; flipped over, the same curve is pushed only in compression, which is exactly what stone is good at. That trick lets his arches stand without the flying buttresses of older cathedrals.

Who Was Antoni Gaudí?

Antoni Gaudí i Cornet was born on June 25, 1852, near Reus in Catalonia, the son of a coppersmith. He grew up watching his father hammer flat sheets of copper into curved pots and boilers, and he later said that seeing shapes in space came from that workshop. He was often ill as a child and spent long hours outdoors looking closely at plants, rocks and animals. Nature, he said, was his great teacher: "Nothing is invented, for it's written in nature first."

He studied architecture in Barcelona and graduated in 1878. The school's director is said to have remarked at the ceremony that they had given the title "either to a madman or to a genius; time will tell." That same year Gaudí met the industrialist Eusebi Güell, who became his lifelong patron and commissioned a palace, a park, and a church for his workers' village, the Colònia Güell.

In 1883, at 31, Gaudí took over a new church in Barcelona: the Expiatory Temple of the Holy Family, La Sagrada Família. He reshaped the whole design. A devout Catholic, he planned it as a Bible in stone, with three great façades telling the Nativity, the Passion, and the Glory of Christ, and eighteen towers for the twelve apostles, the four Gospel writers, Mary, and Jesus. Asked why it was taking so long, he is said to have answered, "My client is not in a hurry."

Meanwhile he built some of the most original houses in Europe: Casa Batlló, with its bone-like columns, and Casa Milà ("La Pedrera"), whose stone front ripples like a cliff. For about ten years he tested the structure of the Colònia Güell church with an upside-down model of hanging strings. From 1914 he worked on nothing but the Sagrada Família, living simply and pouring his money into it. On June 7, 1926, he was struck by a tram on his way to church. Dressed so plainly that people took him for a beggar, he was taken to a hospital for the poor, and he died three days later, on June 10, aged 73. He is buried in the crypt of the church he never saw finished.

A Life Built in Stone

  1. 1852 Born June 25 near Reus, Catalonia, the son of a coppersmith.
  2. 1878 Qualifies as an architect in Barcelona and meets his future patron, Eusebi Güell.
  3. 1883 Takes charge of La Sagrada Família, at age 31, and begins redesigning it.
  4. 1886–88 Builds Palau Güell, a townhouse with parabolic arches.
  5. 1898 Starts the hanging-string model for the Colònia Güell church, about ten years of testing.
  6. 1900–14 Park Güell: viaducts of leaning columns and trencadís mosaic.
  7. 1904–06 Remodels Casa Batlló.
  8. 1906–12 Builds Casa Milà, "La Pedrera," with its attic of catenary arches.
  9. 1914 Stops all other work to give himself entirely to the Sagrada Família.
  10. 1926 Struck by a tram on June 7; dies June 10, aged 73, and is buried in the church's crypt.
  11. 1984 UNESCO names his works a World Heritage Site (extended in 2005).
  12. 2010 Pope Benedict XVI consecrates the Sagrada Família as a basilica.

The Physics: Flip the Chain

Tension and compression. A chain can only be pulled. It can't push and it can't stiffly bend, so when it hangs, every link settles where the pulls on it balance. The curve it makes, the catenary, carries its load entirely in tension. Now turn that curve upside down. Every pull becomes a push of the same size, along the same line. The flipped shape carries its load entirely in compression, with no bending at all.

Why that matters for stone. Stone and brick are very strong when squeezed but crack easily when pulled or bent. A semicircular Roman or Gothic arch isn't quite the right shape, so the force wants to push outward at the sides; medieval builders braced their cathedrals with flying buttresses to hold the walls in. Gaudí's arches follow the force line itself, so they need no outside props.

Loads change the shape. A real building isn't an even chain. Towers, roofs and floors press on some points more than others. So Gaudí hung little bags of lead shot from his strings, each sized to the weight of the stone it stood for, and the strings bent into the exact shape of that particular building. A three-dimensional web of strings let him design a whole church at once. It was a computer made of gravity, eighty years before engineers had software to do the same calculation.

y = a·cosh(x/a)
The shape of a chain hanging under its own weight
a sets how tight or deep the curve is
Flip it (y → −y) and you have the ideal arch

Robert Hooke wrote the rule in 1676: "As hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch." The same curve shapes the Gateway Arch in St. Louis and the cables of the Brooklyn Bridge (see Emily Roebling).

Is a catenary the same as a parabola?
Close, but no. A chain carrying only its own weight makes a catenary. A cable carrying a load spread evenly along the ground below it (like a bridge deck) makes a parabola. Galileo thought hanging chains were parabolas; it took Huygens, Leibniz and the Bernoullis in 1691 to find the true curve. Gaudí's weighted strings made neither: they found the exact shape for his loads.
How did he see the building from an upside-down model?
He photographed the hanging model, flipped the photograph, and painted over it to imagine walls and windows. He also drew on mirrors placed under the model.

Hanging Chain Lab

A real chain, simulated link by link. Let it settle, hang weights on it the way Gaudí did, then flip it into an arch. The red line is a semicircular arch of the same span, so you can see where an old-style arch strays from the force line.

Find the shape with gravity

Try this: Bring the pins close together until the chain hangs deep, then flip it. That tall, pointed arch is the shape of Gaudí's attic at Casa Milà. Next, add one heavy weight: the arch develops a kink right where a tower would stand. Every load in a building changes its ideal shape.
Make it real: Hang a light chain (or a string with paper clips) from two pushpins on a board. Trace its curve, flip the tracing, and stack sugar cubes or dominoes along it. No glue needed: the arch stands because every block is only squeezed.

Gaudí's Key Contributions

🪡
Hanging-Chain Design

Used weighted string models to compute arch shapes with gravity, a physical form-finding method engineers still study.

⛪
La Sagrada Família

Spent 43 years designing a basilica that tells the Gospel in stone, still being built from his models a century later.

🌳
Leaning, Branching Columns

Tilted and branched columns to follow the force, removing the need for flying buttresses.

🌊
Geometry from Nature

Built with ruled surfaces such as hyperboloids and helicoids, curved forms that can be made from straight lines.

🧩
Trencadís Mosaic

Covered curves with broken tile, turning waste into colour and solving how to tile any surface.

🏛️
Craft as Engineering

Designed down to doorknobs, ironwork and furniture, treating every scale as one design problem.

Gaudí's Legacy Today

Seven of Gaudí's works are UNESCO World Heritage sites, and the Sagrada Família draws millions of visitors a year while construction goes on. When the Spanish Civil War burned his workshop in 1936, many of his plaster models were smashed; builders have pieced the fragments back together ever since. Today they use 3D scanning, parametric CAD and CNC stone cutting to turn his geometry into finished stone. The central Tower of Jesus Christ is designed to reach 172.5 m, making it the tallest church in the world, and Gaudí deliberately kept it just below the height of Montjuïc, the hill nearby, because he believed a human work should not surpass God's.

His idea of letting physics find the shape, called form-finding, came back in the 20th century in Frei Otto's hanging-net roofs and Heinz Isler's ice-and-fabric shell models, and it lives on in computer tools that simulate chains and cloth. When you change one number in a parametric model and the whole form updates (see Tinkercad Codeblocks), you're doing on a screen what Gaudí did with string.

Practice Problems

Think about catenaries, compression, and Gaudí's life.

Easy1. A hanging chain carries its load in which kind of force?

Hint: Can you push something with a chain?

Easy2. When Gaudí flipped a hanging shape upside down, what kind of force did the arch carry?

Hint: Flipping turns every pull into a push of the same size.

Medium3. Why is compression good news for a stone arch?

Hint: Think of stacking blocks versus hanging from a stone rod.

Medium4. What did Gaudí hang on his strings to stand for the weight of towers and roofs?

Hint: Each one was sized to the weight of the stone it represented.

Medium5. Gaudí took over the Sagrada Família in 1883 and died in 1926. For how many years did he work on it?

Hint: 1926 − 1883.

Challenge6. In the Chain Lab, you hang one heavy weight in the middle of the chain. What happens to the arch when you flip it?

Hint: Try it. A point load pulls the chain into a V near the weight.

Challenge7. Medieval cathedrals with semicircular or pointed arches needed flying buttresses. Why didn't Gaudí's Sagrada Família?

Hint: Buttresses push back on sideways thrust. What if the structure has no sideways thrust to push back on?
