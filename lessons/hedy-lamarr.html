Hedy Lamarr

Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum Hollywood Star Self-Taught Inventor

Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) was a Hollywood film star of the 1930s and 40s who, in her off-screen hours, co-invented a radio technique called frequency hopping meant to keep Allied torpedoes from being jammed in World War II. Overlooked for decades, that 1942 patent turned out to be the direct ancestor of the spread-spectrum technology behind Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and GPS.

Who Was Hedy Lamarr?

Born Hedwig Eva Maria Kiesler on November 9, 1914 in Vienna, Austria, Lamarr became a film actress in Europe in the early 1930s. She gained early notoriety for her role in the controversial 1933 Czech film Ecstasy. Around this time she was briefly married to Friedrich Mandl, an Austrian arms manufacturer whose business dealings reportedly exposed her to conversations about torpedo guidance and radio-controlled weapons technology — knowledge she would later put to unexpected use.

Lamarr fled that marriage and moved to the United States around 1937, signing with the film studio MGM and taking the stage name Hedy Lamarr. She became a major Hollywood star through the late 1930s and 1940s, appearing in films including Algiers (1938) and Samson and Delilah (1949).

Alongside her film career, Lamarr pursued inventing as a hobby, eventually working with composer and avant-garde inventor George Antheil. Motivated by the sinking of ships carrying refugee children and a desire to help the Allied war effort, the pair developed and patented, in August 1942, a "Secret Communication System" (U.S. Patent 2,292,387) — a frequency-hopping technique meant to make radio-guided torpedoes resistant to jamming. The U.S. Navy did not adopt the technology at the time, and the patent expired before it ever saw military use.

Lamarr received little public recognition for the invention during most of her lifetime. She was jointly honored, along with Antheil, with the Electronic Frontier Foundation's Pioneer Award in 1997, and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2014. She died on January 19, 2000, at age 85.

What Frequency Hopping Actually Does

A radio-guided torpedo that transmits on one fixed frequency is easy for an enemy to find and jam — just flood that one frequency with noise and the control signal is drowned out. Lamarr and Antheil's idea was to make the frequency itself a moving target: instead of staying on a single channel, the transmitter rapidly switches between many different frequencies in a pattern known only to the transmitter and receiver, who stay synchronized as they hop together.

Because an eavesdropper doesn't know the pattern, they can't predict which frequency the signal will use next, making the transmission far harder to jam or intercept. Antheil, a composer who had experimented with synchronizing multiple player pianos for an avant-garde orchestral piece, suggested a mechanism inspired by the player piano's punched paper roll to keep transmitter and receiver hopping in lockstep.

Freq 1 → Freq 4 → Freq 2 → Freq 5 → …
The signal switches frequencies many times per second
following a pattern shared only by sender and receiver
so an outside listener hears only scattered, meaningless fragments

This is the direct conceptual ancestor of the spread-spectrum technology used in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth today.

From a Wartime Patent to Everyday Devices

The Navy didn't use Lamarr and Antheil's system during World War II, and the patent expired before it was ever applied militarily. Decades later, though, frequency-hopping and related spread-spectrum techniques were independently rediscovered and adopted — first in military communications, and eventually as the foundation for civilian wireless standards, including Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and the spread-spectrum techniques used in GPS and CDMA cellular systems.

Did Lamarr's exact invention end up inside modern Wi-Fi and Bluetooth?
Not directly — the specific 1942 patent expired unused, and modern systems were engineered independently with digital electronics that didn't exist in the 1940s. But the core idea, hopping between frequencies in a synchronized pattern to resist jamming and interception, is the same principle that spread-spectrum wireless technology relies on today.
Why didn't the Navy adopt it during the war?
Historians point to several factors, including the mechanical complexity of synchronizing the player-piano-style mechanism reliably in wartime hardware, and reports that Navy officials doubted a Hollywood actress could have contributed meaningful engineering. The patent sat largely unused until long after it expired.

Lamarr's Legacy Today

For most of her lifetime, Lamarr's invention went publicly unrecognized — a fact many historians tie to assumptions of the era that a glamorous film star could not also be taken seriously as an engineer. She herself reportedly said, "Any girl can be glamorous. All you have to do is stand still and look stupid," a wry comment on how little credit her intellectual work received compared to her looks.

Her story has since become a widely cited case study in overlooked women inventors, and a reminder that meaningful scientific contributions can come from outside traditional academic or engineering careers. The technical line from her 1942 patent to the spread-spectrum principles used in Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and GPS runs directly to devices nearly everyone now carries every day.

Practice Problems

Think about frequency hopping, invention, and Lamarr's life.

Easy1. In what year were Lamarr and Antheil granted their frequency-hopping patent?

Hint: This was during World War II, several years after she moved to the United States.

Easy2. What problem was the frequency-hopping system originally designed to solve?

Hint: Lamarr and Antheil wanted to help the Allied war effort by protecting radio-controlled weapons.

Medium3. How many years passed between the 1942 frequency-hopping patent and the 1997 EFF Pioneer Award?

Hint: Subtract 1942 from 1997.

Medium4. Which modern wireless technologies trace their conceptual roots back to Lamarr and Antheil's frequency-hopping idea?

Hint: Both rely on spread-spectrum principles descended from frequency hopping.

Challenge5. What mechanism inspired Antheil's approach to keeping transmitter and receiver synchronized while hopping frequencies?

Hint: Antheil had experimented with synchronizing multiple player pianos in an avant-garde musical piece.

Medium6. If a frequency-hopping transmitter switches to a new frequency 50 times per second, how many frequency switches occur in 4 seconds?

Hint: Multiply 50 switches per second by 4 seconds.

Easy7. What name did Hedwig Eva Maria Kiesler take when she signed with MGM in 1937?

Hint: This is the stage name she is remembered by today.

Challenge8. Why do historians say Lamarr's invention went largely unrecognized for decades?

Hint: Her own wry comment about glamour versus intellect points to this bias.
