Arnaut Daniel: The Sestina Workshop

Before rap verses, Wordle, or algorithmic music, a 12th-century troubadour invented poetry's greatest mathematical puzzle: a 39-line poem built on six spiraling words with zero rhymes.

Grades 6–8: Trace the 6-word path & draft Stanza 1 Grades 9–12: Shift parts of speech & master the spiral algorithm

Guiding Question: When the exact same word returns six times in six different stanzas, how can you change its meaning so the reader hears something completely new?

Who Was Arnaut Daniel?

The Troubadour

Active around 1180–1200 in southern France. Troubadours were the singer-songwriters of the Middle Ages, performing intricate, lyric love poetry with lutes and viols.

The Vernacular

He wrote in Occitan (the living language spoken across southern France) rather than church Latin. Writing in everyday speech allowed poets to invent new musical forms.

Dante’s Tribute

In Purgatorio XXVI, Dante meets Arnaut on Mount Purgatory and calls him “the best smith of the mother tongue”—letting Arnaut speak in his native Occitan inside an Italian masterpiece.

The Original Poem: Arnaut's famous sestina begins Lo ferm voler qu'el cor m'intra (“The firm desire that enters my heart”). He chained six words across 39 lines: intra (enters), ongla (nail), arma (soul), verga (rod), oncle (uncle), and cambra (chamber).

The Sestina Algorithm: How the Spiral Works

A sestina does not use traditional AABB or ABAB rhyming. Instead, it relies on repetition under strict permutation:

Rule 1 · Six by Six

Six stanzas of six lines each = 36 lines. Every line must end with one of your six designated powerhouse words.

Rule 2 · The Retro-Spiral

The words don't repeat randomly. The bottom word of Stanza 1 jumps to the top of Stanza 2, followed by line 1, then line 5, line 2, line 4, line 3 (order: 6, 1, 5, 2, 4, 3).

Rule 3 · The Envoi (Mic Drop)

A three-line final stanza (the envoi or tornada) that packs all six words into three short lines (two per line) to deliver the emotional conclusion.

      The Word-Shifter Trick (Grades 9–12): To prevent your poem from feeling repetitive, change your word's meaning or grammatical role! For example, “light” can be sunlight (noun), a pale color (adjective), or catching fire (verb). “Stone” can be a rock by the river or a cold, unfeeling heart.

      Classroom Reflection & Discussion

      Grades 6–8 Prompt

      Write just Stanza 1 (six lines). Then advance to Stanza 2: which line became the hardest to set up? How does the constraint force your brain to think of ideas you wouldn't have planned?

      Grades 9–12 Prompt

      Why do poets impose severe rules like the sestina or sonnet on themselves? How is a poetic constraint similar to game rules (like chess or soccer)—where limitations actually generate creativity?
