Horror in literature

A strange sound, a locked room, a warning ignored. Read how writers make fear—and what their stories ask us to notice.

Start with a teacher-chosen passage. Move toward the full Gothic stories as students are ready. Read the Bible passages as Scripture in context, with a different purpose from fictional horror.

Choose a starting point

These are teaching recommendations, not fixed reading levels. Preview each selection and let students choose a quieter alternative when a scene is too intense.

K–5 · Mystery before fright

Tell a short, softened version of Sleepy Hollow or read a carefully selected scene aloud. Ask what the rider seems to be, and what evidence the ending leaves open.

Use atmosphere, prediction, and kindness. Skip violent endings and disturbing full texts.

Grades 6–8 · Suspense and choice

Read The Monkey's Paw or a selected Poe passage with guided pauses. Track what a character knows before a dangerous decision.

Preview grief, death, and violence; offer another text or an excerpt.

Grades 9–12 · Moral and Gothic questions

Read the complete works in pairs or independently. Compare unreliable narrators, social pressure, religious language, and the cost of refusing compassion.

The Lottery, Frankenstein, and The Yellow Wallpaper need particular care.

A three-pass reading ritual

Use one scene, paragraph, or image at a time. Begin with the words on the page before moving to a moral judgment.

  1. Notice. Name one exact detail: a sound, object, repeated word, or change in setting.
  2. Trace. Find what the reader knows that a character does not—or what the narrator may be hiding.
  3. Discuss. Ask what action the fear leads to. Support an answer with the passage, and allow more than one defensible reading.

The story shelf

Each link opens the original text from a publisher, literary institution, or Project Gutenberg. Start with the question beside the title; the page does not give away the ending.

The Legend of Sleepy Hollow

Washington Irving · 1820 · teacher-selected passages K–5; full story 6–12

A village tells a ghost story; a nervous schoolmaster rides home at night. Is the fear in the road, the legend, or the rider's imagination?

Look for: the details Irving gives before the nighttime ride. Which ones prepare us to expect a ghost?

Content: pursuit and an unexplained disappearance.

Read at Project Gutenberg

The Monkey's Paw

W. W. Jacobs · 1902 · grades 6–12

A family receives three wishes, along with a warning. Suspense grows from what they want and what a wish might cost.

Look for: the first warning about the paw. Why do the characters keep it anyway?

Content: death, grief, and an ominous knock at the door.

Read at Project Gutenberg

The Tell-Tale Heart

Edgar Allan Poe · 1843 · selected passages 6–8; full story 9–12

The narrator insists on being reasonable while describing a terrible deed. The voice itself becomes the source of fear.

Look for: the opening claim about the narrator's senses. What does it make you trust or doubt?

Content: murder, concealment, and a distressed narrator.

Read at the Poe Museum

The Fall of the House of Usher

Edgar Allan Poe · 1839 · grades 9–12

A visitor enters a decaying house where family, building, and fear seem to echo one another.

Look for: the first description of the house. Which words describe a building, and which seem to describe a person?

Content: illness, death, premature burial, and intense dread.

Read at Project Gutenberg

Young Goodman Brown

Nathaniel Hawthorne · 1835 · grades 9–12

A man walks into the forest and returns unable to see his neighbors as he once did. The story uses Christian words and symbols, but its events remain deliberately uncertain.

Look for: the name Faith. When does it refer to a person, and when might it carry a second meaning?

Content: satanic imagery, distrust, and spiritual despair.

Read in Mosses from an Old Manse

Frankenstein

Mary Shelley · 1818 · selected chapters 8; full novel 9–12

Victor creates a living being and recoils from him. The novel asks what a maker owes the life he has made.

Look for: Victor's actions immediately after the creature opens its eyes. What responsibility does he accept or refuse?

Content: death, violence, abandonment, and sustained grief. The creature is not named Frankenstein.

Read the 1818 text at Project Gutenberg

The Yellow Wallpaper

Charlotte Perkins Gilman · 1892 · grades 9–12

A woman records what happens when her concerns are dismissed and she is prevented from writing. The room grows frightening as her isolation deepens.

Look for: what the narrator says about John early on. Which details show how little say she has?

Content: confinement, postpartum mental distress, and psychological deterioration. Discuss care without treating illness as a moral failure.

Read at Project Gutenberg

The Lottery

Shirley Jackson · 1948 · grades 9–12

An ordinary community follows a yearly ritual without asking whether it is right. The calm, familiar details make the moral shock stronger.

Look for: how the villagers speak about the old black box. Where does habit stand in for a reason?

Content: communal killing, including children taking part. Preview before assigning.

Read at The New Yorker

When the Bible is frightening

The Bible includes visions, judgments, death, and encounters with powers people cannot control. These passages can be frightening, but they are not written as entertainment. Read the whole scene and ask what the passage says about God, human choices, judgment, lament, or hope. Literary details matter; genre and context matter too.

  • Daniel 5The writing on the wall · grades 6–12

    A feast stops when a hand writes a message the king cannot understand. Read through Daniel's interpretation: what has the king ignored?

    Read Daniel 5 in the class Bible
  • Ezekiel 37:1–14The valley of dry bones · selected reading K–5; full passage 6–12

    The opening image is eerie, but the vision moves toward breath and restoration. What changes from the first scene to the last?

    Read Ezekiel 37 in the class Bible
  • 1 Samuel 28:3–25Saul and the medium of Endor · grades 9–12

    A desperate king seeks a forbidden consultation after hearing no answer. Track Saul's fear and the consequences named in the scene.

    Read 1 Samuel 28 in the class Bible
  • Revelation 6:1–8The four horsemen · grades 9–12

    Four seals release images of conquest, war, scarcity, and death. What does symbolic vision show that a plain report might not?

    Read Revelation 6 in the class Bible
  • Revelation 9:1–12The locust vision · grades 9–12

    Unsettling, composite creatures fill the vision. Notice the repeated limits on what they may do before interpreting any single image.

    Read Revelation 9 in the class Bible
A helpful boundary

Hawthorne may use Christian language for a fictional ambiguity; Revelation uses apocalyptic imagery inside Scripture. Similar images do not make the texts the same kind of writing. Avoid making a Bible vision into a prediction game or claiming one contested interpretation is certain.

For the teacher

One class period: fear and evidence

5 minutes: name the difference between surprise, suspense, and dread. 10 minutes: read one chosen scene aloud, stopping before its turning point. 10 minutes: students mark a sound, object, or repeated phrase. 15 minutes: discuss what the character knows, wants, and fears, with a line of evidence. 10 minutes: read the ending and revise one prediction. For a Bible passage, also locate its speaker, genre, and surrounding chapter.

Christian discussion without forcing a single answer

Ask what a story shows about responsibility, truth, neighbor-love, pride, or hope. Distinguish a character's claim from the author's claim, and a fictional religious image from Christian teaching. Students may disagree about an ambiguous ending while still reading carefully.

Care and choice

Give content notes before reading. Invite students to step away from a scene or choose a less intense text without explaining personal experiences. Do not ask students to disclose grief, mental illness, trauma, or spiritual fears to the class. Discuss the treatment of the narrator in The Yellow Wallpaper with compassion and historical context.
