Paradise Lost

John Milton’s epic of rebellion, temptation, and the first choice

Milton set out to write the great English epic, and he chose the biggest story available: why humans fall, and what that means. He wrote most of it blind, dictating to helpers, and he published it in 1667. Nearly four hundred years later, readers still argue over its most magnetic character, Satan.

Grades 6–8 Track: Character Voice, The Fall & Visual Contrast Grades 9–12 Track: The Charismatic Antihero, Free Will & Milton's Politics Epic conventions Blank verse Hero or villain?
Big Idea

Milton states his goal in the first book: to “justify the ways of God to men.” The poem works by making both sides speak, so readers must judge for themselves what free choice really costs.

Quick Facts

AuthorJohn Milton, born London, 1608; died 1674
Published1667 in ten books; revised 1674 into twelve
FormBlank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter, about 10,500 lines
ComposedAfter Milton went totally blind (c. 1652), by dictation
SubjectThe rebellion of Satan, the temptation of Adam and Eve, and the loss of Eden

Why Milton Matters Today: The Architect of the Charismatic Antihero

Why do modern movies, comics, and video games give their most compelling speeches to villains? John Milton pioneered that psychological trick in 1667.

From Darth Vader to Magneto

Earlier poets usually drew Satan as a grotesque monster: Dante’s is a silent, three-faced giant frozen in ice. Milton gave Satan towering grandeur, sorrowful eyes, passionate leadership, and speeches that make readers want to cheer. Long before Thanos, Loki, or Killmonger, Milton proved that the most dangerous villains are the ones who believe they are freedom fighters.

The "Psychological Trap" for the Reader

Milton puts you inside Satan's camp for the first two full books. You hear his stirring rallying cries before you ever see Heaven or Eden! Milton deliberately tests your ability to spot persuasive manipulation versus genuine truth.

Composed in the Dark

Milton was completely blind when he composed all 10,500 lines. He composed at night, held dozens of lines in his head, and dictated them in the morning to whichever daughter, nephew or friend came to write them down. That is why the poem is built for the ear—it was spoken aloud before it was ever inked.

Student Jargon Buster: Words to Know Before You Read

Demystify Milton’s epic language with these essential literary terms.

Blank Verse
noun · English poetic meter

Unrhymed lines of ten syllables following an iambic rhythm (unstressed / stressed). Milton famously called rhyming a "troublesome and modern bondage" and chose blank verse because it flows with the natural force and cadence of elevated speech.

Pandemonium
pan-duh-MOH-nee-um · noun

A word literally coined by Milton! Built from Greek pan ("all") + daimonion ("demon"). In the poem, it is the golden capital city and palace built by the fallen angels in Hell. Today it means wild uproar or chaos.

In Medias Res
in MAY-dee-ahs RACE · Latin phrase

"Into the middle of things." Milton doesn’t begin with the war in Heaven; he drops us directly onto the fiery lake after the war was already lost. The backstory is told later by the angel Raphael.

Epic Simile
noun · literary device

An elaborate, multi-line comparison introduced by "as" or "like" that paints an entire mini-landscape. For instance, Milton compares Satan’s shield to the magnified face of the moon seen through Galileo’s telescope.

Soliloquy
suh-LIL-uh-kwee · noun

A speech delivered by a character when they are completely alone, exposing their private doubts and torment to the audience. When Satan lands on Mount Niphates in Book IV, his heroic mask drops and he admits his misery.

Invocation
in-voh-KAY-shun · noun

The opening prayer of an epic, calling on a Muse for help. Homer and Virgil call on the Greek Muses; Milton calls on the “Heav’nly Muse” and the Holy Spirit, and in Book III he prays for inner light because he is blind.

Caesura & Enjambment
si-ZHOOR-uh · en-JAM-ment · nouns

A caesura is a pause inside a line (“Which way I flie is Hell; my self am Hell”). Enjambment is a sentence running on past the end of a line without stopping. Milton uses both to make blank verse sound like a voice thinking, not a song.

Providence
PRAH-vi-duhns · noun

Divine protective care, direction, and foresight over human destiny. The poem ends with Adam and Eve walking out into the world with "Providence thir guide."

Lines Worth Knowing

Milton’s text is in the public domain, and the spelling below is close to his original. Read each line aloud.

Of Mans First Disobedience, and the Fruit Of that Forbidden Tree, whose mortal tast Brought Death into the World, and all our woe,Book I, the opening lines. The subject is stated at once.

Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heav’n.Satan, Book I. The line most often quoted from the poem.

The World was all before them, where to choose Thir place of rest, and Providence thir guide: They hand in hand with wandring steps and slow, Through Eden took thir solitarie way.Book XII, the closing lines. Adam and Eve leave, but not without hope.

Milton’s source

The temptation and the Fall take up one short chapter of the Bible: Genesis 3, twenty-four verses. Milton turns it into 10,565 lines. Read the chapter first, then list three things the poem adds that Genesis never says (a war in Heaven, a council in Hell, Adam’s reason for eating). Ask what each addition is for.

Milton’s Universe: A Map of the Poem

Milton builds a whole cosmos. Heaven is at the top, Hell at the bottom, and between them is Chaos, a storm of raw matter. Our entire universe of stars hangs from Heaven on a golden chain. Press a place on the map, or a name below, to read what happens there.

Books III, V–VI

Heaven

“Nine dayes they fell”

God’s home of light, ringed by a wall of crystal and towers of opal. The war in Heaven is fought here for three days; on the third the Son drives the rebel angels to the edge, and they tumble out through Chaos for nine days before Hell closes over them.

Book III

The stairs to Heaven

“The Stairs were such as whereon Jacob saw / Angels ascending and descending”

When Satan reaches the outside of the World he sees a golden stairway let down from Heaven’s gate, the ladder from Jacob’s dream (Genesis 28). Heaven and Earth are still connected; Satan stands at the bottom and cannot climb it.

Books II, III, VIII

The World

“This pendant world, in bigness as a Starr / Of smallest Magnitude close by the Moon”

“The World” means our whole universe: Earth, Sun, planets and stars, wrapped in shells. Seen from Chaos it is a speck hanging from Heaven on a golden chain. In Book VIII Adam asks whether the Sun or the Earth sits at the centre, and Raphael leaves both answers open. Milton had met Galileo.

Books IV, IX

Eden

“At one slight bound high overleap’d all bound”

A walled garden on a mountaintop, at the centre of the World. Satan doesn’t use the gate: he jumps the wall in one leap, and Milton compares him to a wolf leaping into a sheepfold. Everything in the poem’s cosmos points toward this one small place and the one choice made there.

Book II

Chaos

“The Womb of nature and perhaps her Grave”

Not empty space but raw matter at war with itself: hot, cold, wet and dry all fighting, ruled by the old powers Chaos and Night. Everything God makes is made out of it. Satan’s wings fail in it, and he drops thousands of fathoms before a blast of air throws him back up.

Books II–III

Satan’s flight

“Into this wilde Abyss the warie fiend / Stood on the brink of Hell and look’d a while”

The heroic journey of the poem, and it belongs to the villain. Satan alone volunteers to cross Chaos, asks Chaos for directions, and climbs to the outer shell of the World. Epic heroes travel to the underworld; Milton’s anti-hero travels out of it.

Book II

Hell Gate

“And thrice threefold the Gates; three folds were Brass, / Three Iron, three of Adamantine Rock”

Two figures guard the nine-layered gates: Sin, who was born out of Satan’s head when he first imagined rebellion, and Death, their son. Sin holds the key. She unlocks the gate for her father, and once it is open she has no power to shut it again.

Books I–II

Hell

“No light, but rather darkness visible”

A burning lake and a dry plain, three times as far from Heaven as the centre of the World is from its outer pole. The fallen angels build Pandemonium here and hold their council. Hell is a place in the poem, but Satan later learns it is also a state of mind he carries with him.

Book X

Sin and Death’s bridge

“a Bridge / Of length prodigious”

After the Fall, Sin and Death sense the change and pave Satan’s route into a highway from Hell Gate to the World. The one hard journey of a single rebel becomes an easy road for everyone after. That is Milton’s picture of how sin spreads.

Think with the map

Compare this with Dante’s Hell, a funnel inside the Earth. Milton puts Hell outside our universe entirely, as far as possible from God. Why might a poet writing after Galileo want a bigger, stranger cosmos than Dante’s?

The Story in Five Movements

Twelve books can feel large. Follow the story in five groups.

What happens

Satan and his followers wake on a burning lake. They build Pandemonium and hold a council on what to do.

Key voices

Moloch wants war, Belial wants ease, Mammon wants wealth, and Beelzebub proposes to corrupt God’s newest creation, humanity.

Turning point

Satan volunteers to fly through Chaos to Earth. At Hell’s gate he meets Sin and Death.

What to notice

The devils argue like a parliament, which reflects Milton’s own experience in Puritan politics. Satan is charismatic and persuasive, and we are in his company for two full books.

What happens

God foresees the Fall. The Son offers to sacrifice himself for humanity. Satan reaches Eden and sees Adam and Eve for the first time.

Key idea

God says humans were made “sufficient to have stood, though free to fall.”

Satan’s state

He is envious, and in a soliloquy he admits that God had done him no wrong.

What to notice

In Book IV Satan says, “Which way I flie is Hell; my self am Hell.” The line changes the picture: Hell is a state of mind he carries wherever he goes.

What happens

The angel Raphael visits Adam and warns him. He tells of Satan’s rebellion, the war in Heaven, and the creation of the world.

Adam’s questions

Adam asks about the stars and about his own first day, and Raphael tells him not to explore beyond what he needs to know.

The warning

Raphael reminds Adam that obedience must be freely chosen.

What to notice

These books are a poem inside the poem: Heaven’s side of the story, told in retrospect. Notice that we hear the war twice, once from Satan’s point of view and once from Raphael’s.

What happens

Satan, in the serpent, persuades Eve to eat. Adam eats too, knowing what he does, so he will not be separated from her.

Consequences

Sin and Death build a bridge from Hell to Earth. Adam and Eve blame each other, then despair.

Turning point

Eve proposes they seek forgiveness together.

What to notice

Milton takes time with Adam’s choice. He is not tricked. He chooses love over obedience, and Milton’s readers have argued for centuries whether that is noble or tragic.

What happens

The angel Michael shows Adam a vision of human history, from Cain’s murder to the Flood to a future redemption. Then he sends them out of Eden.

Tone

The poem moves from horror to a hard hope.

Ending

Adam and Eve leave hand in hand, free to choose their place.

What to notice

After eleven books of grand scenery, the last image is two people walking away, small in a big world. The final lines are quiet and open.

Who’s Who in the Poem

Keep track of the speakers. Notice that several characters exist mainly to be compared with Satan.

Satan

Once the archangel Lucifer. Leads the rebellion, flies to Eden, tempts Eve. Gets the poem’s grandest speeches.

Beelzebub

Satan’s second in command. At the council he proposes the plan to ruin humanity, but the idea was Satan’s first.

Moloch, Belial, Mammon

The other voices at the council in Hell: open war, quiet ease, and building wealth in Hell. Three ways of refusing to repent.

Sin and Death

Satan’s daughter and the son she bore him. They guard Hell Gate, then build the bridge to Earth. An allegory inside the epic.

Abdiel

The one angel who stands up in Satan’s camp and refuses to rebel. He walks out alone: “faithful found / Among the faithless, faithful only hee” (Book V).

The Son

Offers to die for humanity in Book III, wins the war in Heaven, creates the World, and judges Adam and Eve with mercy.

Raphael

The “sociable” angel who has lunch with Adam and Eve and tells them about the war in Heaven and the Creation (Books V–VIII).

Uriel, Gabriel, Ithuriel

Uriel guards the Sun and is fooled by Satan’s disguise. Gabriel commands Eden’s guard; Ithuriel’s spear touches the toad at Eve’s ear and Satan springs up in his true shape.

Michael

The warrior archangel. In Books XI–XII he shows Adam the history to come, then leads the pair out of Eden.

Adam

Curious, thoughtful, and deeply in love. He eats the fruit knowing what it means, so that he will not lose Eve.

Eve

Asks to work alone in the garden, is persuaded by the serpent, and later is the first to ask for forgiveness and make peace.

Abdiel vs. Satan

Satan says he is the brave rebel against a tyrant. Abdiel stands alone against a crowd of thousands and says no. Which of them takes the greater risk? Which one is really thinking for himself?

Interactive Rhetoric Lab: Charisma vs. Reality

Satan's speeches sound magnificent when first heard. But Milton plants subtle traps inside the rhetoric. Select a famous speech below to inspect how the charm works—and what the poem reveals beneath it.

“Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heav’n.” (Book I)

The Rhetorical Charm (Why It Hooks You)

It sounds like the ultimate battle cry of personal sovereignty and liberty: choosing hardship and independence over humble servitude to a supreme ruler.

The Reality Check (What Milton Uncovers)

Look at what he rules over: a barren lake of fire and millions of suffering followers whom he led to disaster. His "freedom" is an eternal prison of pride.

Satan’s Shrinking Shapes

Track what Satan turns into as the poem goes on. Step through the books and watch the direction he travels: from blazing archangel down to a snake on its belly.

✨

Book I · Fallen archangel

“his form had yet not lost / All her Original brightness”

Still enormous and splendid. Milton compares him to the Sun glimpsed through morning mist or in an eclipse: dimmed, but still the Sun. This is the Satan readers admire.

😇

Book III · Disguised cherub

“And now a stripling Cherube he appeers”

To get past Uriel, the angel who guards the Sun, he disguises himself as a young angel and asks for directions. Uriel is fooled, because, Milton says, no man or angel can see hypocrisy. His first move in our world is a costume.

🐦

Book IV · Cormorant

“Sat like a Cormorant”

He perches on the Tree of Life in the middle of Eden, disguised as a greedy sea bird, “devising Death” on the very tree of life. He looks, but he can’t enjoy what he sees.

🐸

Book IV · Toad

“Squat like a Toad, close at the eare of Eve”

The angels find him crouched by the sleeping Eve, whispering a bad dream into her ear. One touch of Ithuriel’s spear and he jumps up in his own shape, caught.

🐍

Book IX · Serpent

“foul descent! that I who erst contended / With Gods to sit the highest, am now constraind / Into a Beast”

He chooses the snake himself, and he knows exactly how far he has fallen. He says so out loud, then goes ahead anyway.

🐍

Book X · Punished

“A monstrous Serpent on his Belly prone”

Back in Pandemonium he gives a victory speech and waits for applause. Instead he hears a vast hiss: he and every one of his followers have been turned into snakes. The great orator ends without a voice.

Talk about it

In Book I many readers cheer for Satan. By Book X he is a hissing snake. Is Milton changing Satan, or slowly showing us what he was all along? Use two of his shapes as evidence.

Big Questions the Poem Raises

These are the debates that keep the poem alive in classrooms.

Is Satan the hero?

In the early 1790s William Blake wrote that Milton was “of the Devil’s party without knowing it.” Others say Milton deliberately shows how attractive rebellion sounds, so readers learn to spot it.

Who is the real rebel?

Satan rebels against God; Abdiel rebels against Satan’s whole army, alone. If courage means standing against the crowd, which one is braver?

Free will

God gives Adam and Eve real freedom, so they can fall. Does freedom that includes the risk of disaster still deserve to be called good?

Adam’s choice

Adam eats knowingly, for love of Eve. Is this the poem’s tragic center or its greatest fault?

Politics

Milton served the English republic and later saw the monarchy return. Many readers see Satan’s rebellion, and its failure, as a comment on revolutions.

Blindness and vision

Milton asks for inner light because he cannot see. Watch how the poem keeps returning to sight, darkness, and light.

Epic scale

The poem covers Heaven, Hell, Earth, and all of time, yet it turns on a single choice by two people.

Milton’s Life in Brief

  1. 1608: Born in London to a prosperous family and educated at Christ’s College, Cambridge.
  2. 1638–39: Travels in Italy, where he meets Galileo, then under house arrest.
  3. 1642: The English Civil War begins. Milton turns to political writing.
  4. 1649: After the king’s execution, joins the government as Secretary for Foreign Tongues.
  5. c. 1652: Loses his sight entirely.
  6. 1660: The monarchy returns, and Milton is briefly arrested.
  7. 1667: Publishes Paradise Lost in ten books.
  8. 1674: Publishes a revised twelve-book edition and dies in November.

Try It Yourself & Tiered Classroom Inquiry

Take a position on Milton’s characters, then defend it with textual evidence.

Grades 6–8 Track: Character Motives, Sound & Modern Villains

  • The Antihero Profile: Compare Milton’s Satan to a modern movie or comic antagonist (e.g. Darth Vader, Magneto, Loki, or Killmonger). What makes a villain who truly believes they are fighting for justice more interesting than an ordinary monster?
  • Speaking in Blank Verse: Try reciting Satan’s famous line aloud: “Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heav’n.” Tap your foot to the 10 beats (da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM). Why did Milton compose without rhyming?
  • The Ending Walk: Adam and Eve are expelled from Eden, but Milton writes that they leave “hand in hand with wandring steps and slow.” Does the poem end in total defeat, or quiet courage?

Grades 9–12 Track: The Great Satan Debate & Political Allegory

  • Hero, Tragic Rebel, or Villain? (Sentence Starters):
    • If arguing Hero / Tragic Rebel: "Satan demonstrates epic courage when he declares [insert Book I quote], refusing to submit to an omnipotent monarch even after total battlefield defeat."
    • If arguing Master Manipulator / Villain: "While Satan adopts the rhetoric of freedom in Books I–II, his internal soliloquy on Mount Niphates in Book IV reveals that his rebellion is driven by pure pride, admitting that [insert quote]."
  • Milton’s Politics: Milton was a passionate republican who served Oliver Cromwell’s Commonwealth and defended the trial and execution of King Charles I. When the monarchy returned in 1660, Milton was arrested and narrowly avoided execution. Is Satan’s rebellion a reflection of England’s failed revolution, or a warning against demagogues who promise freedom while establishing their own tyranny?
  • Blake's Famous Accusation: In The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790–93), poet William Blake claimed Milton was “of the Devil’s party without knowing it.” Do you agree? Why did Milton make Satan’s voice so much more dynamic and poetry-rich than the speeches of God the Father?

Milton in the Type of the 1600s

Milton went blind and dictated Paradise Lost to helpers. It was printed in 1667 in the heavy, ink-rich types of Restoration London. Here are lines he wrote, set in a face from that time (with the old spelling kept where it adds flavor).

That to the highth of this great Argument I may assert Eternal Providence, And justifie the wayes of God to men. John Milton, Paradise Lost, Book 1, lines 24–26

Milton’s stated aim: to explain why a good God allows suffering and evil. Argument here means the subject of the poem.

It was the Winter wilde, While the Heav’n-born-childe, All meanly wrapt in the rude manger lies; John Milton, On the Morning of Christ’s Nativity, stanza 1 (1629)

Milton wrote this Christmas poem at twenty-one. Meanly wrapt means humbly wrapped.

They also serve who only stand and wait. John Milton, Sonnet 19, “When I consider how my light is spent” (c. 1652–55)

Written after he lost his sight, as he asks how a blind man can still serve God. His answer is one of the best-known lines in English.
