Language & Literature · Poetry Compendium

Dylan Thomas

Six poems, read first and then annotated

Thomas wrote poems meant to be heard: thick with sound, crowded with images of body and nature, and driven by one large idea, that the force which makes things grow is the same force that ends them. This page is a quick reading compendium. For each poem, read the full text first, aloud and without stopping. Then use the annotations to see how the words, sounds, and form are working.

The force that through the green fuse drives the flower

Written 1933 · Collected in 18 Poems (1934) · Five stanzas

Step 1 · Read the full poem at poets.org ↗Read it aloud once, start to finish, before the annotations.

Before you read

One of Thomas’s earliest famous poems, written when he was 19. It sets a pattern in each stanza: a force in nature does something, the same force does it to the speaker’s body, and the speaker says he is unable to tell anyone.

Step 2 · Annotations

“green fuse”
A plant stem and a fuse at the same time. Growth is presented as a slow-burning charge, and “green” already means young and unripe.
“drives my green age”
The verb “drives” keeps returning. The force that pushes the flower up is the one that pushes the speaker through youth.
“is my destroyer”
The turn of the first stanza. The short line is left hanging, so the creative force and the destroyer are one and the same.
“dumb”
Mute, and also foolish. Each stanza’s last two lines begin with the speaker saying he cannot say what he knows.
“crooked rose”
A rose bent by weather or time. The word “crooked” comes back in the last stanza with “crooked worm,” closing a loop.
“wintry fever”
A paradox: fever is heat and wintry is cold. Life’s heat and death’s cold are shown as one illness.
“mouthing streams”
Streams that seem to speak or gulp. “Mouth” starts a motif that returns in “the same mouth sucks.”
“turns mine to wax”
Blood becomes wax, pale, still, and candle-like. The force that dries the streams does the same to him.
“shroud sail”
A shroud is a burial cloth, but “shrouds” are also the ropes that support a ship’s mast. Wind fills the sail and death carries him forward.
“hangman’s lime”
Quicklime, used on the bodies of executed prisoners. The speaker’s own clay supplies the executioner’s material.
“lips of time leech”
Time drains life like a parasite. “Leech” was also an old word for a doctor, and both senses hover.
“crooked worm”
The final couplet drops the metaphors. The worm at the “sheet” (a bedsheet and a winding-sheet) is the same force as the fuse in stanza one.
Listen for

Hard, driving sounds: drives, dries, drips, dumb. Heavy stresses and a run of short, sharp verbs. Notice how the rhythm of the three-line opening gives way to a slower two-line refrain in each stanza.

Form

Four five-line stanzas, each with a nature image, a body image, and a refrain, then a closing two-line stanza that is only the refrain. Rhyme is slant and irregular.

And death shall have no dominion

Written 1933 · Collected in Twenty-five Poems (1936) · Three stanzas

Step 1 · Read the full poem at poets.org ↗Read it aloud once, start to finish, before the annotations.

Before you read

The title echoes a line from the Bible, Romans 6:9, which says that death has no more dominion over Christ. Thomas uses the phrase for something wider: the dead do not vanish, they become part of wind, moon, stars, and sea.

Step 2 · Annotations

“And death shall have no dominion”
The refrain opens and closes the poem’s stanzas. “Shall” is a promise, not a description, so the poem reads like a vow.
“the man in the wind and the west moon”
The dead are absorbed into nature. Identity is not kept but joined to something larger.
“stars at elbow and foot”
A physical, almost comic image of the dead merged with the cosmos. Notice how everyday the body parts are.
“Though lovers be lost love shall not”
A compact paradox: individuals are lost, but love is not. The line turns on the repeated word “lost.”
“Though they go mad they shall be sane”
A series of reversals, each one promising the opposite of what the world expects: madness to sanity, sinking to rising.
“Twisting on racks”
Torture images: racks, wheels. They show the worst that bodies can suffer, in order to promise they will not break.
“Heads of the characters hammer through daisies”
An image that can be read more than one way: the dead pushing up as flowers, or the dead still striking out. Thomas leaves it open.
“Break in the sun till the sun breaks down”
The promise stretches past the life of the sun itself. Note the repetition of “break” in a poem about what will not break.
Listen for

Thomas’s repeated “shall” sets a chant-like beat. The lines lengthen and shorten around the refrain, and the vowels of “sea,” “rise,” “again” carry the sense of rising.

Form

Three stanzas of nine lines, each framed by the refrain. Rhyme is loose and mostly slant. The stanzas build in the same order of promise, then an exception.

Fern Hill

Written 1945 · Collected in Deaths and Entrances (1946) · Six stanzas

Step 1 · Read the full poem at poets.org ↗Read it aloud once, start to finish, before the annotations.

Before you read

The farm of Thomas’s aunt, Ann Jones, in Carmarthenshire, where he spent childhood summers. The poem is told by an adult remembering, but it speaks in the child’s eager voice. Time is the main character.

Step 2 · Annotations

“young and easy”
The opening state: carefree, at home in the world. “Easy” also carries the sense of being at ease with Time, who has not yet turned.
“green and golden”
Two colors of childhood, fresh and sunlit. “Golden” also suggests ripeness, and ripeness is a step toward harvest.
“happy as the grass was green”
A simile in which the two halves are almost the same thing. Happiness and green are treated as one state.
“Time let me hail and climb”
Time is personified as a generous host. The child believes he is in charge, but Time is the one letting him.
“in the mercy of his means”
“Mercy” shows that the child is dependent, though he does not know it. The phrase gives Time both kindness and power.
“Nothing I cared, in the lamb white days”
The child’s lack of care. Compound description (“lamb white”) captures innocence in a single color and animal.
“Time held me green and dying”
The poem’s turning point and its central paradox. To be “green” is to be alive and young, and “dying” shows that Time was always counting.
“sang in my chains like the sea”
The ending: chained by Time, but singing like the sea, which is also bound by tides. Sadness and joy in the same line.
Listen for

Long, flowing lines and lots of vowels: “apple boughs,” “dingle,” “mercy.” The rhythm rolls, and the ending slows it down. Notice how many words are colors or light.

Form

Six stanzas of nine lines, each following the same pattern of line lengths. The consistent shape keeps the poem steady while its meaning turns from delight to loss.

A Refusal to Mourn the Death, by Fire, of a Child in London

Written 1944 · Collected in Deaths and Entrances (1946) · Four stanzas

Step 1 · Read the full poem at poets.org ↗Read it aloud once, start to finish, before the annotations.

Before you read

Written during the Second World War after German air raids on London. An elegy is a poem of mourning, and this one is titled a refusal. It says it will not mourn in the usual way, and the poem itself is a form of mourning.

Step 2 · Annotations

“Never until the mankind making”
The refusal begins with a condition. He will not mourn until the world itself ends, so the poem sets the child’s death at the scale of all creation.
One long first sentence
The first stanza is a single sentence that delays its main verb. The slow build forces the reader to wait for the refusal to arrive.
“Zion of the water bead”
Zion is the holy hill of Jerusalem. Thomas makes a drop of water a sacred place, so nature itself is a temple.
“synagogue of the ear of corn”
A companion image: a house of worship in a single grain of corn. Ordinary natural things carry religious weight.
“the majesty and burning of the child’s death”
“Burning” refers literally to the fire of the raid. “Majesty” dignifies the death, which is why crude grief would cheapen it.
“London’s daughter”
The dead child is named for the city. She joins the first dead, and the river Thames carries on.
“After the first death, there is no other.”
The closing line. It plays on the Christian idea of a “second death” after the first: for Thomas, once a person has died, nothing further can harm them.
Listen for

Very long, winding sentences, then a very short last line. Notice how the syntax makes you hold your breath, and how the ending releases it.

Form

Four stanzas that move from a single long sentence to plain statement. Rhyme is present but stretched, so it is felt more than heard.

Do not go gentle into that good night

Written 1947 · Published 1951, collected in In Country Sleep (1952) · Villanelle

Step 1 · Read the full poem at poets.org ↗Read it aloud once, start to finish, before the annotations.

Before you read

Written for Thomas’s father, David John Thomas, a schoolteacher, who was ill and losing his sight. He died in December 1952. Where “The force that through the green fuse” accepts a shared fate, this poem refuses to accept it.

Step 2 · Annotations

“that good night”
A gentle euphemism for death. The word “good” is soft, and the poem answers it by asking the listener not to be soft.
“Rage, rage against the dying of the light”
The second refrain. The doubled “rage” stammers with feeling, and “light” stands for life, sight, and the day.
“Wise men”, “Good men”, “Wild men”, “Grave men”
Four types, one per stanza. Each stanza gives a reason that particular kind of person has not finished living.
“their words had forked no lightning”
The wise feel their words did not strike hard enough. “Forked lightning” is a vivid image for words with power.
“Grave men, near death, who see with blinding sight”
A paradox: those going blind see most clearly. It has personal force, as his father was losing his sight.
“Blind eyes could blaze like meteors and be gay”
Even blind eyes can flash. “Gay” is used in its older sense of joyful.
“you, my father, there on the sad height”
The turn where the speaker addresses one person. The “sad height” is a high place of grief, and the father is at the edge.
“Curse, bless, me now with your fierce tears”
Opposites offered together. He wants a reaction of any kind, even anger, because it means his father is still fighting.
Listen for

Two refrains alternate, like a drumbeat that keeps coming back. Notice how the rhyme sounds (night, light, right, flight, sight) are repeated across the poem, so the poem itself seems to refuse to end.

Form

A villanelle: nineteen lines in five tercets and a final quatrain, with only two rhyme sounds. Line 1 returns as lines 6, 12, and 18, and line 3 returns as lines 9, 15, and 19.

In my craft or sullen art

Written 1945 · Collected in Deaths and Entrances (1946)

Step 1 · Read the full poem at poets.org ↗Read it aloud once, start to finish, before the annotations.

Before you read

A poem about why the poet writes at all. It takes place at night, when lovers lie awake, and it says his audience is not the famous or the paying, but ordinary people with private griefs.

Step 2 · Annotations

“sullen art”
“Sullen” means quiet and gloomy, and also something worked at stubbornly. He calls his craft a serious, unshowy labor.
“only the moon rages”
The moon “rages” at night while everyone else rests. Compare this to the “rage” in “Do not go gentle.”
“Not for ambition or bread”
The poem defines itself by what it is not. He lists the usual reasons to write, and refuses them.
“the strut and trade of charms”
The display and sale of showy talent. “Strut” mocks public performance, and “trade” mocks commerce.
“common wages”
The only pay he wants is to speak for “their most secret heart,” the private feelings of ordinary people.
“the towering dead”
The great poets of the past, remembered with “nightingales and psalms.” He says he is not writing for them either.
“spindrift pages”
Spindrift is spray blown off waves. The image makes his pages fragile and light, thrown up by the sea.
“Who pay no praise or wages”
The lovers he writes for will never pay him, and will not even notice his craft. The poem accepts an audience that never applauds.
Listen for

Short lines and steady rhyme create a hush, like something spoken quietly at night. Listen for the repeated “-ages” and “-art” sounds that tie the stanzas together.

Form

Two stanzas of short lines with an intricate, repeating rhyme pattern. The form is quiet and compact, which fits the poem’s claim that it is modest.

Motifs Across the Poems

Thomas returns to a small set of images. Follow them from poem to poem.

Green

“Green fuse,” “green age,” “green and golden,” and “green and dying.” Green means young and alive, and Thomas keeps showing that it is also the color of what is about to change.

Time as a force

Time drives, leeches, and holds. In “Fern Hill” it is a gentle host, and in “The force that through the green fuse” a parasite.

The body as landscape

Blood is a stream, bones are picked clean, veins have mouths. Thomas sees the body as part of the natural world.

Refusal and defiance

“Shall have no dominion,” “do not go gentle,” and “a refusal to mourn” all say no to death’s power, though they do it in different ways.

Light and sight

“The dying of the light,” “blinding sight,” and the moon that “rages.” Light stands for life, vision, and speech.

Nature as sacred

A water bead is Zion, an ear of corn is a synagogue. Thomas reads religion in ordinary things.

Reading Toolkit

Terms that appear in the annotations.

Alliteration and assonance

Repeated consonant sounds (drives, dries, drips) and repeated vowel sounds. Thomas uses both to make lines feel physical.

Slant rhyme

Near-rhyme, where the sounds are close but not identical. It gives a sense of echo without a neat chime.

Refrain

A line or phrase repeated at intervals. It can work like a chorus, a vow, or an obsession.

Villanelle

A 19-line form with two refrains and two rhyme sounds. The repetition suits poems about things that will not let go.

Paradox

A statement that seems to contradict itself but holds a truth: “green and dying,” “curse, bless.”

Personification

Giving human traits to a force or thing. Time has lips and a hand, and the moon rages.

Elegy

A poem of mourning. Thomas’s “Refusal to Mourn” is an elegy that says it will not be one.

Syllabic verse

A form organized by counting syllables, not stresses. “Fern Hill” repeats a set pattern of syllable counts in each stanza.

Thomas’s Life in Brief

  1. 1914: Born in Swansea, Wales. His father teaches English at the local grammar school.
  2. 1931–1934: Leaves school at 16 to work as a newspaper reporter, and fills notebooks with poems, many later published.
  3. 1934: 18 Poems appears and brings him quick attention in London.
  4. 1937: Marries Caitlin Macnamara. The family later settles in Wales.
  5. 1940s: Writes film scripts and radio pieces during and after World War II. Deaths and Entrances appears in 1946.
  6. 1949–1953: Lives in the boathouse at Laugharne and tours the United States, reading his work to packed halls.
  7. 1953: Dies in New York at 39. Under Milk Wood is broadcast the following year.

Go Further

Optional next steps for a longer study.

Hear him

Recordings of Thomas reading his own poems exist and are worth hearing after you have read the text. Notice where his voice slows and where it rushes.

Compare pairs

Read “The force that through the green fuse” beside “Do not go gentle.” One accepts a shared fate, and the other resists it. Note where the vocabulary overlaps.
