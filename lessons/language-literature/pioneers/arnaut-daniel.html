Arnaut Daniel: The Sestina Workshop

Before rap verses, Wordle, or algorithmic music, a 12th-century troubadour invented poetry's greatest mathematical puzzle: a 39-line poem built on six spiraling words with zero rhymes.

Grades 6–8: Trace the 6-word path & draft Stanza 1 Grades 9–12: Shift parts of speech & master the spiral algorithm

Guiding Question: When the exact same word returns six times in six different stanzas, how can you change its meaning so the reader hears something completely new?

Who Was Arnaut Daniel?

The Troubadour

Active around 1180–1200 in southern France. Troubadours were the singer-songwriters of the Middle Ages, performing intricate, lyric love poetry with lutes and viols.

The Vernacular

He wrote in Occitan (the living language spoken across southern France) rather than church Latin. Writing in everyday speech allowed poets to invent new musical forms.

Dante’s Tribute

In Purgatorio XXVI, Dante meets Arnaut on Mount Purgatory and calls him “the best smith of the mother tongue”—letting Arnaut speak in his native Occitan inside an Italian masterpiece.

The Original Poem: Arnaut's famous sestina begins Lo ferm voler qu'el cor m'intra (“The firm desire that enters my heart”). He chained six words across 39 lines: intra (enters), ongla (nail), arma (soul), verga (rod), oncle (uncle), and cambra (chamber). Its first stanza, in his own Occitan, is below.
Lo ferm voler qu'el cor m'intra no·m pot jes becs escoissendre ni ongla de lausengier, qui pert per mal dir s'arma; e car non l'aus batr' ab ram ni ab verga, sivals a frau, lai on non aurai oncle, jauzirai joi, en vergier o dins cambra. Arnaut Daniel, Lo ferm voler qu'el cor m'intra, stanza 1 (spelling varies by manuscript and edition)

“The firm desire that enters my heart / no beak can tear out, nor the nail / of the slanderer, who loses his soul by speaking ill; / and since I dare not beat him with branch or rod, / at least in secret, where I shall have no uncle, / I shall enjoy joy, in orchard or in chamber.” (Literal English by the lesson author.) The six red-underlined words are the ones the sestina spirals.

His Surviving Songs

About eighteen songs survive under Arnaut's name, each one found in the great medieval songbooks called chansonniers. Most are love songs (cansos) built from rare words, odd rhymes and clashing sounds. He called this hard, dense style trobar ric (“rich composing”); his admirers said he made the language itself sound like metal being hammered. The titles below are each song's first line (its incipit), because medieval songs had no other title. Spellings differ from one manuscript and edition to the next.

SestinaInvented c. 119039 lines

Lo ferm voler qu'el cor m'intra

“The firm desire that enters my heart”

The poem that started a form. Six end words rotate through six stanzas and return in a three-line closing tornada. Dante and Petrarch copied the idea, and poets from Sir Philip Sidney to Elizabeth Bishop have used it ever since. Explore it in the navigator below.

CansoBirdsong

Doutz brais e critz

“Sweet cries and calls”

Opens with the sounds of birds, then turns the song into a contest between nature's music and a lover's longing. The title line alone is a sound-puzzle: brais, critz, lais, cantars, voutas are all different kinds of noise.

CansoColour

Er vei vermeills, vertz, blaus, blancs, gruocs

“Now I see red, green, blue, white, yellow”

Five colour words in one line paint the orchards and meadows before the poet turns to love. Good for asking: what colour words would you use for your own spring?

CansoWind

L'aur'amara

“The bitter breeze”

One of his hardest songs, a showpiece of trobar clus (“closed” style) in which short, clipped lines make the bitter wind strip the leaves from the branches. Famous for words that seem to crackle when read aloud.

CansoCraft

Chansson do·ill mot son plan e prim

“A song whose words are smooth and fine”

A poem about poem-making: he sets out to file and polish his words like a smith, as though the song were a thing built by hand. It is one of only two songs whose melody survives, so musicians can sing it today.

CansoHeights and depths

Autet e bas entrels prims fuoills

“High and low among the first leaves”

Another nature opening, this time set “high and low” among the first leaves. Look for how his songs love opposite pairs.

CansoAutumn

Can chai la fuoilla

“When the leaf falls”

The seasonal opposite of the spring songs: the leaf falls, yet love goes on. Its melody survives too, which makes it a rare chance to hear him. Compare its opening with Er vei vermeills and notice how the sounds change from bright to dry.

CansoMelody

Ab gai so cundet e leri

“With a gay, graceful, light tune”

A song of lightness and ease that nevertheless hides its craft in tight, clipped rhymes. Like all his work, it was meant to be sung, then admired for how it was made.

CansoSpring

Lanquan vei fueill' e flor e frug

“When I see leaf and flower and fruit”

A song that opens in the growing season. Many of his songs begin in a landscape and then fold the weather into the heart.

CansoParadox

Sols sui qui sai lo sobrafan qu'sofre

“Only I know the excess of grief I suffer”

A song of private pain, in which the poet claims that only he knows how much he suffers.

CansoParadox

Anc ieu non l’aic, mas elha m’a

“I never had her, yet she has me”

A riddle in the first line: love without possession. Ask students what a poet gains by saying something that sounds impossible.

CansoList

Amors e jois e liocs e tems

“Love and joy and place and time”

Opens with four nouns in a row, the kind of heaped-up list he liked. Try writing your own opening with four nouns.

CansoSeason

Ar resplan la flors enversa

“Now the reversed flower shines”

Another seasonal opening with a puzzling, unusual image (a “reversed” flower), typical of his love of rare phrases.

CansoWeather

En breu brisaral temps braus

“Soon the harsh weather will break”

Winter turns toward spring and the poet’s hope grows with it. Notice the cluster of br sounds in the first line.

Try it

Choose any incipit and read it aloud twice: first quickly, then slowly, giving every consonant its full sound. Which words feel hard to say, and why might a poet want that? Write two lines of your own where the sounds fit the meaning (soft sounds for calm, hard sounds for storm).

Arnaut Speaks in Dante's Purgatorio

On the seventh terrace of Mount Purgatory, Dante asks the poet Guido Guinizzelli who stands beside him. Guido points at Arnaut as “the best smith of the mother tongue.” Arnaut then answers Dante in Occitan, one of the very few times in the Divine Comedy that a poet is allowed to speak his own language at length. (Purgatorio XXVI, lines 140–147.)

Tan m'abellis vostre cortes deman, qu'ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire. Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan; consiros vei la passada folor, e vei jausen lo joi qu'esper, denan. Ara vos prec, per aquella valor que vos guida al som de l'escalina, sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor! Dante, Purgatorio XXVI.140–147, spelling as in common modern editions

“So much your courteous question pleases me that I cannot and will not hide from you. I am Arnaut, who weeps and goes singing; sorrowful I see my past folly, and joyful I see the joy I hope for, ahead. Now I pray you, by that power which guides you to the top of the stair, remember in due time my pain!” (Literal English by the lesson author.)

Look closer: Dante lets Arnaut say ieu sui Arnaut, “I am Arnaut,” in the language of Arnaut's own songs. Why might a poet give another poet's voice back to him in his native tongue, instead of translating?

The Sestina Algorithm: How the Spiral Works

A sestina does not use traditional AABB or ABAB rhyming. Instead, it relies on repetition under strict permutation:

Rule 1 · Six by Six

Six stanzas of six lines each = 36 lines. Every line must end with one of your six designated powerhouse words.

Rule 2 · The Retro-Spiral

The words don't repeat randomly. The bottom word of Stanza 1 jumps to the top of Stanza 2, followed by line 1, then line 5, line 2, line 4, line 3 (order: 6, 1, 5, 2, 4, 3).

Rule 3 · The Envoi (Mic Drop)

A three-line final stanza (the envoi or tornada) that packs all six words into three short lines (two per line) to deliver the emotional conclusion.

      The Word-Shifter Trick (Grades 9–12): To prevent your poem from feeling repetitive, change your word's meaning or grammatical role! For example, “light” can be sunlight (noun), a pale color (adjective), or catching fire (verb). “Stone” can be a rock by the river or a cold, unfeeling heart.

      Classroom Reflection & Discussion

      Grades 6–8 Prompt

      Write just Stanza 1 (six lines). Then advance to Stanza 2: which line became the hardest to set up? How does the constraint force your brain to think of ideas you wouldn't have planned?

      Grades 9–12 Prompt

      Why do poets impose severe rules like the sestina or sonnet on themselves? How is a poetic constraint similar to game rules (like chess or soccer)—where limitations actually generate creativity?
