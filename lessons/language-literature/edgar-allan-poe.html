Language & Literature · Close reading · Grades 7–12

Edgar Allan Poe

A tapping at the door. A repeated word. A speaker whose certainty we cannot share.

Read a stanza before receiving an explanation. Hear how Poe builds a pattern, then notice where a narrator turns an ordinary event into a frightening story. Poe (1809–1849) wrote poems, criticism, mysteries, satire, and tales of psychological suspense. “The Raven” made him widely known in 1845.

1 · Read aloud first

“The Raven”: opening stanza

One voice reads the stanza aloud. A second reading lets students tap the beat and underline repeated sounds. This opening comes from the public-domain text linked below; spelling and punctuation vary among editions.

Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary, Over many a quaint and curious volume of forgotten lore— While I nodded, nearly napping, suddenly there came a tapping, As of some one gently rapping, rapping at my chamber door. “’Tis some visitor,” I muttered, “tapping at my chamber door— Only this and nothing more.”

Continue the full poem at Project Gutenberg. At the end, return to this stanza: what did the speaker know here, and what did he only suppose?

2 · Phrase-anchored notes

What the words are doing

“midnight dreary” / “weak and weary”
The paired sounds and heavy stresses establish a weary pace before the visitor appears. Read the line slowly, then quickly; hear the change in mood.
“nearly napping” / “tapping” / “rapping”
Repeated n and p sounds make the noise hard to ignore. Mark each new sound word in the stanza.
“As of some one”
This is a comparison, not a sighting. The narrator has heard a sound and proposed a cause. Keep observation and inference separate.
“Only this and nothing more”
The closing line tries to settle the event. Read onward and trace how this phrase changes as the speaker grows less certain.

3 · Form and voice

Listen for the mechanism

Internal rhyme

“Dreary” meets “weary” inside one line. Circle pairs that make the line feel musical.

Refrain

The stanza closes with a repeated shape. Compare its later endings and ask what each return now means to the speaker.

Limited knowledge

We share the speaker’s hearing, guesses, and grief. A confident voice can still misread what happens.

Reading question: On a second reading, make two columns: what the speaker observes and what he concludes. Cite words from the poem in both.

4 · Continue with prose

Choose a Poe reading

“The Purloined Letter”

Grades 7–12 · A detective story about seeing what is hidden in plain sight. Track each change in what the police and Dupin know.

Read the story

“The Tell-Tale Heart”

Grades 8–12 · A narrator insists on sanity while describing a murder and concealment. Preview violence before assigning it. Trace the gap between what he says and what he reveals.

Read the story

“The Fall of the House of Usher”

Grades 9–12 · Illness, dread, and a collapsing household. Preview the intensity. Mark where the house is physical setting and where it seems to mirror its inhabitants.

Read the story

For a gentler introduction, read only the opening stanza of “The Raven” and focus on sound. Offer a different text to students who prefer to avoid disturbing material.

One class period

  1. 5 minutes: Read the opening stanza without commentary.
  2. 10 minutes: Read it again and mark sounds and repeated phrases.
  3. 15 minutes: Build the observation/inference columns with exact words.
  4. 15 minutes: Continue the poem or a selected tale; return to one early claim the narrator made.
  5. 5 minutes: Write one sentence about how the wording changed your reading, with a quotation.

In a Christian-school discussion, treat the narrator’s grief and fear with care. Poe’s fictional voice is a subject for literary study; it is not a statement of Christian doctrine. Do not ask students to disclose personal grief.
