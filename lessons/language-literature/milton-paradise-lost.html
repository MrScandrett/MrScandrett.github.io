Paradise Lost

John Milton’s epic of rebellion, temptation, and the first choice

Milton set out to write the great English epic, and he chose the biggest story available: why humans fall, and what that means. He wrote most of it blind, dictating to helpers, and he published it in 1667. Nearly four hundred years later, readers still argue over its most magnetic character, Satan.

Epic conventionsBlank verseFree willHero or villain?
Big Idea

Milton states his goal in the first book: to “justify the ways of God to men.” The poem works by making both sides speak, so readers must judge for themselves what free choice really costs.

Quick Facts

AuthorJohn Milton, born London, 1608; died 1674
Published1667 in ten books; revised 1674 into twelve
FormBlank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter, about 10,500 lines
ComposedAfter Milton went totally blind (c. 1652), by dictation
SubjectThe rebellion of Satan, the temptation of Adam and Eve, and the loss of Eden

Lines Worth Knowing

Milton’s text is in the public domain, and the spelling below is close to his original. Read each line aloud.

Of Mans First Disobedience, and the Fruit Of that Forbidden Tree, whose mortal tast Brought Death into the World, and all our woe,Book I, the opening lines. The subject is stated at once.

Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heav’n.Satan, Book I. The line most often quoted from the poem.

The World was all before them, where to choose Thir place of rest, and Providence thir guide: They hand in hand with wandring steps and slow, Through Eden took thir solitarie way.Book XII, the closing lines. Adam and Eve leave, but not without hope.

Invocation

Epics begin by calling on a Muse. Milton asks the Holy Spirit instead, and asks for light because he is blind.

In medias res

The story begins in the middle. We meet Satan already fallen, lying in a lake of fire in Book I.

Epic simile

Long comparisons that extend across many lines. Milton compares Satan’s spear to the tallest pine cut for a ship’s mast.

Blank verse

Unrhymed lines of ten syllables. Milton said rhyme was a “troublesome and modern bondage,” so his lines run on into each other like speech.

The Story in Five Movements

Twelve books can feel large. Follow the story in five groups.

What happens

Satan and his followers wake on a burning lake. They build Pandemonium and hold a council on what to do.

Key voices

Moloch wants war, Belial wants ease, Mammon wants wealth, and Beelzebub proposes to corrupt God’s newest creation, humanity.

Turning point

Satan volunteers to fly through Chaos to Earth. At Hell’s gate he meets Sin and Death.

What to notice

The devils argue like a parliament, which reflects Milton’s own experience in Puritan politics. Satan is charismatic and persuasive, and we are in his company for two full books.

What happens

God foresees the Fall. The Son offers to sacrifice himself for humanity. Satan reaches Eden and sees Adam and Eve for the first time.

Key idea

God says humans were made “sufficient to have stood, though free to fall.”

Satan’s state

He is envious, and in a soliloquy he admits that God had done him no wrong.

What to notice

In Book IV Satan says, “Which way I flie is Hell; my self am Hell.” The line changes the picture: Hell is a state of mind he carries wherever he goes.

What happens

The angel Raphael visits Adam and warns him. He tells of Satan’s rebellion, the war in Heaven, and the creation of the world.

Adam’s questions

Adam asks about the stars and about his own first day, and Raphael tells him not to explore beyond what he needs to know.

The warning

Raphael reminds Adam that obedience must be freely chosen.

What to notice

These books are a poem inside the poem: Heaven’s side of the story, told in retrospect. Notice that we hear the war twice, once from Satan’s point of view and once from Raphael’s.

What happens

Satan, in the serpent, persuades Eve to eat. Adam eats too, knowing what he does, so he will not be separated from her.

Consequences

Sin and Death build a bridge from Hell to Earth. Adam and Eve blame each other, then despair.

Turning point

Eve proposes they seek forgiveness together.

What to notice

Milton takes time with Adam’s choice. He is not tricked. He chooses love over obedience, and Milton’s readers have argued for centuries whether that is noble or tragic.

What happens

The angel Michael shows Adam a vision of human history, from Cain’s murder to the Flood to a future redemption. Then he sends them out of Eden.

Tone

The poem moves from horror to a hard hope.

Ending

Adam and Eve leave hand in hand, free to choose their place.

What to notice

After eleven books of grand scenery, the last image is two people walking away, small in a big world. The final lines are quiet and open.

Hero or Tragedy? Read Satan’s Lines

Readers have argued for centuries about whether Satan is a hero or a tragedy. Read each line and decide which effect it has.

Big Questions the Poem Raises

These are the debates that keep the poem alive in classrooms.

Is Satan the hero?

In the early 1790s William Blake wrote that Milton was “of the Devil’s party without knowing it.” Others say Milton deliberately shows how attractive rebellion sounds, so readers learn to spot it.

Free will

God gives Adam and Eve real freedom, so they can fall. Does freedom that includes the risk of disaster still deserve to be called good?

Adam’s choice

Adam eats knowingly, for love of Eve. Is this the poem’s tragic center or its greatest fault?

Politics

Milton served the English republic and later saw the monarchy return. Many readers see Satan’s rebellion, and its failure, as a comment on revolutions.

Blindness and vision

Milton asks for inner light because he cannot see. Watch how the poem keeps returning to sight, darkness, and light.

Epic scale

The poem covers Heaven, Hell, Earth, and all of time, yet it turns on a single choice by two people.

Milton’s Life in Brief

  1. 1608: Born in London to a prosperous family and educated at Christ’s College, Cambridge.
  2. 1638–39: Travels in Italy, where he meets Galileo, then under house arrest.
  3. 1642: The English Civil War begins. Milton turns to political writing.
  4. 1649: After the king’s execution, joins the government as Secretary for Foreign Tongues.
  5. c. 1652: Loses his sight entirely.
  6. 1660: The monarchy returns, and Milton is briefly arrested.
  7. 1667: Publishes Paradise Lost in ten books.
  8. 1674: Publishes a revised twelve-book edition and dies in November.

Try It Yourself

Take a position, then defend it from the text.

Debate

Was Satan a hero, a tragic figure, or a villain? Pick one, and defend it with at least two quotations.

Rewrite

Write four lines of blank verse (unrhymed, ten syllables per line) in the voice of someone standing at a threshold.

Compare

Compare Dante’s Satan (frozen in ice, silent) with Milton’s (speaking, scheming). What does each poet gain?

Connect

Blake called Milton a poet of the “Devil’s party.” Read Blake’s lesson on this site, then explain how Blake’s own poems ask questions rather than give answers.
