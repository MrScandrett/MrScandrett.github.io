The Wonderful Wizard of Oz

L. Frank Baum’s 1900 “modernized fairy tale,” read in its own time

Many of us know Oz from a movie. This lesson goes back to the book that came first: what Baum actually wrote, what Denslow actually drew, and what the America of the 1890s was worrying about while they worked.

Our guiding question

Baum promised a fairy tale with “the heartaches and nightmares left out.” What does the book tell us about power, appearances, and where the things we want really come from?

Quick Facts

AuthorL. Frank Baum (1856–1919), born in Chittenango, New York; wrote the book while living in Chicago
IllustratorW. W. Denslow (1856–1915), whose two-color and full-color pictures are part of the book’s design
Published1900, Chicago (George M. Hill Company); Baum’s introduction is dated April 1900
Original titleThe Wonderful Wizard of Oz. “The Wizard of Oz” became common later, largely through stage and film versions
LegacyBaum wrote thirteen more Oz books; the series made Oz one of the first great American fantasy worlds

The “Green Curtain”: What Is in the Book

Almost everyone remembers the moment the little dog pulls back the curtain and reveals the Wizard. It’s one of the most famous scenes in American storytelling, but the exact image comes mostly from the movie. Here is what Baum wrote.

The Lion thought it might be as well to frighten the Wizard, so he gave a large, loud roar, which was so fierce and dreadful that Toto jumped away from him in alarm and tipped over the screen that stood in a corner. As it fell with a crash they looked that way, and the next moment all of them were filled with wonder. For they saw, standing in just the spot the screen had hidden, a little old man, with a bald head and a wrinkled face, who seemed to be as much surprised as they were.Chapter XV, “The Discovery of Oz, the Terrible.” Public-domain text.
A screen, not a curtain

In the book it’s a screen in a corner of the throne room, and it’s the Lion’s roar that makes Toto knock it over. The curtain version, with the famous “pay no attention” line, belongs to the 1939 film.

The green is in the glasses

The real “green curtain” in the book is a filter: green spectacles, locked on every visitor with a key. The Wizard later admits, “when you wear green spectacles, why of course everything you see looks green to you.”

Both are the same idea

A screen hides the maker; glasses hide the truth about the place. In both, power depends on people not looking behind or beyond what they’re shown.

“But isn’t everything here green?” asked Dorothy. “No more than in any other city,” replied Oz; “but when you wear green spectacles, why of course everything you see looks green to you… but my people have worn green glasses on their eyes so long that most of them think it really is an Emerald City.”Chapter XVI, “The Magic Art of the Great Humbug.” Public-domain text.
Why the difference matters

Adaptations change details for good reasons. Comparing the two helps you see what each version decided to emphasize: the film makes the reveal one dramatic moment; the book spreads the deception across four different forms, a throne room, a screen, and a city-wide filter.

Look closer: the four faces of Oz

Each visitor sees something different: Dorothy sees a giant Head, the Scarecrow a lovely Lady, the Tin Woodman a terrible Beast, and the Lion a Ball of Fire. Notice that each one matches what that visitor most expects or fears. Discuss: does Oz make himself into what people imagine, or do people see what they imagine in him? Then compare with the Wizard’s own explanation of how he did it, behind the screen with a papier-mâché head, a wire, and a voice.

Five Themes to Track

Use these as lenses while you read. For each, find a short quotation and say what it shows, not just what happens.

1 · The power was already there

The Scarecrow thinks fast, the Tin Woodman weeps over a hurt beetle, and the Lion protects the group. The Wizard says the Lion needs only “confidence in yourself.” What does Baum suggest about wanting something you already show?

2 · Humbug and appearances

Oz is “a humbug,” and by his own account an ordinary man. Yet his “magic” helps people. Is a helpful deception still a deception? When is it harmless, and when does it keep people dependent?

3 · Home

“There is no place like home,” Dorothy says early on, about a Kansas where everything is gray. Baum doesn’t make home glamorous. What is he claiming about loyalty and belonging?

4 · The journey is the gift

Glinda tells Dorothy the shoes could have taken her home “the very first day.” The Scarecrow answers, “But then I should not have had my wonderful brains!” Is the journey wasted, or the point?

5 · Wonder without terror?

Baum’s introduction promises no “horrible and blood-curdling incidents.” But heads are cut off, wolves are chopped, and a witch melts. Does the book keep its promise?

Having this thought in mind, the story of “The Wonderful Wizard of Oz” was written solely to please children of today. It aspires to being a modernized fairy tale, in which the wonderment and joy are retained and the heartaches and nightmares are left out.L. Frank Baum, Introduction, Chicago, April 1900. Public-domain text.

America in 1900: The World Behind Oz

Baum finished his book at the turn of the century. Here are the pressures his readers knew well.

The gray plains

The Great Plains suffered drought and farm failure in the 1890s. Baum had lived in Aberdeen, Dakota Territory, in the late 1880s, where his store failed and drought hurt local farmers. Notice how Baum describes Kansas: “the great gray prairie on every side.”

The Panic of 1893

A severe economic depression followed. Many farmers and workers faced foreclosure and unemployment. Debate over money, tariffs, and who the economy served dominated the elections of 1896 and 1900.

A new, shining city

Baum lived in Chicago, where the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition built the “White City,” a dazzling, temporary fairground of grand buildings. It is often suggested as a model for the Emerald City, though Baum never said so. How is a showpiece city both wonderful and unreal?

Machines and workers

Industry was changing work. The Tin Woodman, a man gradually replaced by tin parts after a series of accidents, can be read as a worker made into a machine. That is one reading, so test it against his chapters.

The frontier is “closed”

The 1890 census reported that there was no longer a clear frontier line. Dorothy’s road is a journey through unexplored, fantastic land, a kind of new frontier of the imagination.

Women and power

Baum’s mother-in-law, Matilda Joslyn Gage, was a leading women’s suffrage activist. In Oz, most of the powerful figures, good and wicked, are women, and a girl, not a prince, is the hero.

A complicated author

Baum was a gifted storyteller and also a man of his time. In 1890–91, as a newspaper editor in South Dakota, he wrote editorials calling for the extermination of Native Americans. Historians cite these when discussing Baum, and readers today weigh them alongside his children’s books. Reading a work in its context means examining its author’s views too, not just the parts we like.

Is Oz a Political Allegory? Debate the Evidence

In 1964 a teacher named Henry Littlefield argued that Oz is a parable of Populism, the 1890s farmers’ movement. In 1990 economist Hugh Rockoff made a detailed case that it is a monetary allegory about the gold standard versus silver. Baum himself never confirmed either reading, and scholars still disagree.

How to argue well

A matching detail is not proof. For each claim, give one detail that supports it, one that complicates it, and say whether a reader in 1900 would have noticed. Baum’s own stated purpose, in the introduction, was entertainment; consider how that affects the debate.

Your Reading Sessions

Read the chapters first, then open the notes. Interpretation notes contain spoilers and stay closed until you open them. The free text is at Project Gutenberg; any unabridged edition works.

01 · Kansas and the arrival (Chapters I–III)

Notice: How Baum describes color and people in Kansas, how Dorothy treats the Scarecrow when she meets him, and what she asks of the people she meets.

Discuss: What does Baum’s gray Kansas tell the reader about why Dorothy still wants to go back?

After reading: context (contains spoilers)

The Kansas chapter is plain and realistic, with an unglamorous farm and a worn-out aunt. That realism makes the fantasy that follows feel larger, and sets up the book’s claim that home matters even when it is plain.

02 · The companions (Chapters IV–VIII)

Notice: What each companion says he lacks, and then what he does on the road.

Discuss: Pick one companion and cite a moment where his behavior contradicts what he says about himself.

After reading: context (contains spoilers)

The three companions repeatedly display the quality they ask the Wizard for. The book builds this irony slowly so the reader sees it before they do.

03 · The road to the city (Chapters IX–XI)

Notice: The Guardian of the Gates, the key, the spectacles, and how the city is described.

Discuss: Why does Oz require glasses “night and day”? What does the Guardian say the reason is?

After reading: context (contains spoilers)

The Guardian says the brightness would blind visitors. Later chapters show another reason. Compare the first explanation with the Wizard’s own account in Chapter XVI.

04 · Four audiences and the Witch (Chapters XII–XIV)

Notice: The four forms Oz takes, the Witch’s weakness, and how Dorothy defeats her.

Discuss: Is Dorothy’s act heroic, accidental, or both? Cite the text.

After reading: context (contains spoilers)

Dorothy melts the Witch by throwing water in anger over a stolen shoe, not by a plan. Baum keeps the violence short and a little comic, in keeping with his promise of a lighter fairy tale.

05 · The humbug (Chapters XV–XVII)

Notice: What causes the screen to fall, what Oz says about himself, and how he “gives” each companion what they want.

Discuss: Is Oz a villain, a fraud, or a kind of teacher? Find two quotations that support different answers.

After reading: context (contains spoilers)

Oz admits he is a humbug but delivers gifts that work because the receivers believe in them. Baum leaves it to the reader whether this is wise leadership or comfortable deception.

06 · South to Glinda, and home (Chapters XVIII–XXIV)

Notice: The obstacles on the road south, what Glinda says about the shoes, and what each companion ends up ruling.

Discuss: Is the Scarecrow right that the journey was worth the detour? Give a reason from the book.

After reading: context (contains spoilers)

The ending hands each companion a kingdom, which suggests that what you become on a journey can matter more than the thing you set out to get. Dorothy’s own ending, return home, is quieter than the film’s.

After the Book: Bring the Evidence Together

Choose one task and support it with at least three details from different chapters. Explain a complication, not only the examples that agree.

Appearance and reality

Compare the screen, the green spectacles, and the Emerald City. How does Baum show that what you see depends on what you’re given to look through?

Who has the power?

Trace power across the book: the Wizard, the witches, the companions, and Dorothy. Who has real power, who only seems to, and what is the difference?

Book, film, or both

Choose one change from the film and argue whether it improves, weakens, or just changes the story. Cite the book.

Creative extension: Write a short scene in which a leader’s trick is exposed. Decide what hides it, a screen, spectacles, or something modern, and how the others react. Annotate two choices with comparisons to Baum.

Words and Terms

Humbug
A fraud or pretender; also nonsense or deception. The Scarecrow calls Oz a humbug, and Oz happily agrees.
Allegory
A story in which characters and events stand for larger ideas. Whether Oz is an allegory is debated.
Parable
A short story that teaches a lesson, often indirectly.
Populism
A late-19th-century American farmers’ political movement that challenged banks and railroads.
Gold standard
A monetary system in which paper money is backed by gold. Debate over adding silver was central to the 1896 election.
Fairy tale
A traditional story with magic and often a moral. Baum calls his book a “modernized” one.
Illustrator’s color coding
Denslow’s use of green, red, blue, and yellow on chapter pages to signal where the story takes place.

Baum and Denslow in Brief

  1. 1856: L. Frank Baum is born in Chittenango, New York.
  2. 1888–91: Baum lives in Aberdeen, Dakota Territory; his store fails and he edits a local newspaper.
  3. 1891: Moves to Chicago and later works as a reporter and salesman.
  4. 1897: Publishes Mother Goose in Prose, illustrated by Maxfield Parrish.
  5. 1899: Publishes Father Goose, His Book, illustrated by W. W. Denslow; it is a bestseller.
  6. 1900: The Wonderful Wizard of Oz appears.
  7. 1902: A stage musical of the book opens in Chicago.
  8. 1904: Baum publishes the first sequel, The Marvelous Land of Oz.
  9. 1919: Baum dies in Hollywood, California; he had written fourteen Oz books in all.
