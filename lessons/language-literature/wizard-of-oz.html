Read What Baum Actually Wrote

L. Frank Baum, The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (Chicago: George M. Hill Co., 1900). Compare these unabridged passages with modern adaptations.

Chapter X · The Guardian of the Gate (The Spectacles)

Then he opened the big box, and Dorothy saw that it was filled with spectacles of every size and shape. All of them had green glasses in them. The Guardian of the Gate said:

“You must put on the spectacles.”

“Why?” asked Dorothy.

“Because if you did not wear them, the brightness and glory of the Emerald City would blind you. Even those who live in the City must wear them night and day. They are all locked on, for Oz so ordered it when the City was first built, and I have the only key that will unlock them.”

He opened the spectacles and fitted them upon Dorothy’s eyes. There were two golden bands fastened to them, which passed around the back of her head, where they were locked together by a little key that was at the end of a chain the Guardian of the Gate wore around his neck. When they were on, Dorothy could not take them off had she wished, but of course she did not want to be blinded by the glare of the Emerald City, so she said nothing.

Chapter XI · The Wonderful City of Oz (The Green Tint)

Even with eyes protected by the green spectacles Dorothy and her friends were at first dazzled by the brilliancy of the wonderful City. The streets were lined with beautiful houses all built of green marble and studded everywhere with sparkling emeralds. They walked over a pavement of the same green marble, and where the blocks were joined together were rows of emeralds, set closely, and glittering in the brightness of the sun. The window panes were of green glass; even the sky above the City had a green tint, and the rays of the sun were green.

There were many people—men, women and children—walking about, and these were all dressed in green clothes and had greenish skins… In one place a man was selling green lemonade, and when the children bought it Dorothy could see that they paid for it with green pennies.

Chapter XV · The Discovery of Oz, the Terrible (The Screen)

The Lion thought it might be as well to frighten the Wizard, so he gave a large, loud roar, which was so fierce and dreadful that Toto jumped away from him in alarm and tipped over the screen that stood in a corner. As it fell with a crash they looked that way, and the next moment all of them were filled with wonder. For they saw, standing in just the spot the screen had hidden, a little old man, with a bald head and a wrinkled face, who seemed to be as much surprised as they were.

The Tin Woodman, raising his axe, rushed toward the little man, crying, “Who are you?”

“I am Oz, the Great and Terrible,” said the little man, in a trembling voice. “Don’t strike me—please don’t!—and I’ll do anything you want me to.”

“I thought Oz was a great Head,” said Dorothy.

“And I thought Oz was a lovely Lady,” said the Scarecrow.

“And I thought Oz was a terrible Beast,” said the Tin Woodman.

“And I thought Oz was a Ball of Fire,” exclaimed the Lion.

“No; you are all wrong,” said the little man meekly. “I have been making believe.”

Chapter XVI · The Magic Art of the Great Humbug (The Confession)

“Just to amuse myself, and keep the good people busy, I ordered them to build this City, and my Palace; and they did it all willingly and well. Then I thought, as the country was so green and beautiful, I would call it the Emerald City; and to make the name fit better I put green spectacles on all the people, so that everything they saw was green.”

“But isn’t everything here green?” asked Dorothy.

“No more than in any other city,” replied Oz; “but when you wear green spectacles, why of course everything you see looks green to you. My people have worn green glasses on their eyes so long that most of them think it really is an Emerald City; and it is a fine city, with plenty of jewels and precious metals, and every good thing that is needed to make one happy. I have been good to the people, and they like me; but ever since this Palace was built, I have shut myself up and would not see any of them.”

Baum’s Introduction · Chicago, April 1900

“Having this thought in mind, the story of ‘The Wonderful Wizard of Oz’ was written solely to please children of today. It aspires to being a modernized fairy tale, in which the wonderment and joy are retained and the heartaches and nightmares are left out.”

Literary Analysis

Five Themes to Track

Use these lenses while analyzing Baum’s narrative craft and character arcs.

1 · The Power Was Already There

The Scarecrow devises clever plans, the Tin Woodman weeps over a hurt beetle, and the Lion leaps across chasms to protect the group. The Wizard tells the Lion he needs only “confidence in yourself.” Baum suggests we often seek external validation for virtues we already practice.

2 · Humbug and Appearances

The Wizard admits he is a humbug, yet his placebo gifts (bran and needles for brains, a silk heart, colored dish-water for courage) work because the recipients believe in them. Is a comforting deception harmless, or does it breed intellectual dependency?

3 · Home and Reality

“There is no place like home,” Dorothy says about a Kansas where the prairie, the houses, and Aunt Em’s eyes are drained of color. Baum refuses to romanticize Kansas, asserting that love and belonging outshine fantasy splendor.

4 · The Journey Is the Gift

Glinda reveals Dorothy could have clicked her shoes home on day one. The Scarecrow protests: “Then I should not have had my wonderful brains!” The trials on the road shaped the companions into leaders.

5 · Wonder Without Terror?

Baum promised no “heartaches and nightmares,” yet wolves are decapitated, Kalidahs perish, and a witch melts. Notice how Baum treats danger with brisk, matter-of-fact directness rather than gothic dread.

Historical Backdrop

America in 1900: The World Behind Oz

The Gray Plains & Drought

In 1888–91 Baum ran a store and edited a newspaper in Dakota Territory. Severe drought wrecked local farms, inspiring his description of Kansas as a “great gray prairie.”

The Panic of 1893 & The White City

Living in Chicago, Baum witnessed both the devastating economic crash of 1893 and the dazzling, temporary “White City” of the World’s Columbian Exposition—a plaster spectacle that likely inspired the Emerald City.

Women and Power

Baum’s mother-in-law, Matilda Joslyn Gage, was a famed suffragist leader. In Oz, real political and magical power rests with women (Glinda, the Witches, Dorothy), while the male ruler is an impotent fraud behind a screen.

Scholarly Debate

Is Oz a Political Allegory?

In 1964, Henry Littlefield argued Oz is a parable on Populism; in 1990, Hugh Rockoff detailed a monetary allegory. Baum never confirmed these readings.

Curriculum Guide

Six Reading Sessions

Keep these focus questions beside your copy of the 24 chapters.

01 · Kansas & Arrival (Chapters I–III)

Notice: How Baum describes the gray prairie and Aunt Em’s faded vitality; how Dorothy treats the Scarecrow upon meeting him.

Discuss: Why does Dorothy yearn for gray Kansas when the Land of the Munchkins is vibrant and fertile?

02 · The Companions (Chapters IV–VIII)

Notice: The specific backstory of the Tin Woodman; the Kalidahs; the Poppy Field.

Discuss: Identify two moments where the Scarecrow displays intellect or the Woodman displays deep empathy.

03 · The Emerald City (Chapters IX–XI)

Notice: The Guardian of the Gate, the spectacles box, the lock and key, and the green lemonade and green pennies.

Discuss: Why did the Guardian lock the glasses on day and night? Compare his explanation with Chapter XVI.

04 · Four Audiences & The Witch (Chapters XII–XIV)

Notice: The four distinct shapes Oz assumes; how Dorothy defeats the Wicked Witch with a simple bucket of water.

Discuss: Was Dorothy’s defeat of the Witch an act of heroic military cunning or an everyday domestic reflex?

05 · The Discovery of the Humbug (Chapters XV–XVII)

Notice: What makes the screen fall; Oz’s confession of Omaha, ballooning, and spectacle-making; the placebo gifts.

Discuss: Is Oz an evil dictator, an amusing trickster, or a pragmatic leader responding to the public’s thirst for illusion?

06 · South to Glinda & Kansas (Chapters XVIII–XXIV)

Notice: The Dainty China Country; the Hammer-Heads; Glinda’s revelation about the silver shoes.

Discuss: What did each companion rule at the end, and why did they each achieve what they originally thought they lacked?

Key Vocabulary

Words and Terms

Humbug
A fraud, impostor, or pretender; a deception designed to cultivate unearned awe.
Allegory
A narrative where characters and plotlines stand systematically for larger historical, religious, or political realities.
Parable
A succinct story teaching a specific moral or political principle.
Populism
An 1890s American political movement of farmers and laborers against railroad monopolies and eastern banks.
Gold Standard
A monetary system backing currency exclusively with gold bullion, restricting currency expansion.
Free Silver
The 1890s movement advocating unlimited coinage of silver to increase money circulation and relieve farm debt.

Historical Milestones

Baum and Denslow Timeline

  • 1856: L. Frank Baum born in Chittenango, New York; W. W. Denslow born in Philadelphia.
  • 1888–91: Baum operates a novelty store and edits The Aberdeen Saturday Pioneer in South Dakota.
  • 1893: Panic of 1893 strikes; the World’s Columbian Exposition opens in Chicago.
  • 1900: The Wonderful Wizard of Oz published by George M. Hill Company in Chicago.
  • 1902: Smash hit Broadway musical adaptation opens, popularizing the shortened title The Wizard of Oz.
  • 1939: MGM releases the Judy Garland film adaptation in three-strip Technicolor.
  • 1964: Henry Littlefield publishes the “Parable on Populism” thesis in American Quarterly.
