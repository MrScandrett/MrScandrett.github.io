The Pilgrim’s Progress

John Bunyan’s dream of a journey from this world to the next

Keep your book open beside this companion. Read an episode first, collect a detail that puzzles or interests you, and then use the guide to test an interpretation against Bunyan’s words.

Our guiding question

How does Bunyan turn invisible beliefs and struggles into a visible journey—and how does the story test the difference between seeming and being?

Quick Facts

AuthorJohn Bunyan, born Elstow, England, 1628; died London, 1688
PublishedPart One in 1678; Part Two (the journey of Christian’s wife Christiana) in 1684
FormA prose allegory framed as a dream
BackgroundBunyan was a tinker (a mender of pots) who preached without a license and was jailed for about twelve years
LegacyTranslated into well over a hundred languages; it inspired later writers from Thackeray to Louisa May Alcott

Before you begin: keep the text in charge

This guide follows Part One, published in 1678. Part Two (1684) follows Christiana, her children, and their companions. Editions divide the story into different chapters, so use the scene boundaries below instead of page numbers. Abridgements may omit scenes; mark an omission rather than inventing evidence.

1 · Read

Read the assigned scenes in your book. You can split a long session across several days. Stop to paraphrase a difficult sentence aloud.

2 · Notice

Copy a short phrase, record its page or scene, and describe what actually happens before deciding what it means.

3 · Discuss and revisit

Make a claim, test it against a second detail, and revise it when the story complicates it. Open the optional notes after your own reading.

By the end, you should be able to distinguish a literal event from an allegorical meaning, explain how dialogue and setting shape an argument, and support a reading of Christian’s progress with evidence from several episodes.

Read within Bunyan’s world

This is a Protestant Christian allegory about sin, grace, faith, salvation, and perseverance. Understanding that framework is essential to interpreting it. You may discuss its claims from any religious or nonreligious standpoint; evaluate interpretations by textual evidence, not by personal belief. Modern analogies come after, and alongside, the book’s own meaning.

For a class or reading group: begin with a one-minute recap; read a short passage aloud; give everyone time to mark a detail; discuss one question below; finish with a revised claim. Partners can rotate as reader and evidence-finder.

Practice close reading with the opening

Read the opening in your edition. Then examine this excerpt from the public-domain text; older spellings and phrasing are retained.

As I walked through the wilderness of this world, I lighted on a certain place where was a Den, and I laid me down in that place to sleep: and, as I slept, I dreamed a dream.

Part One, opening of the dream narrative. The numbers in braces in this online edition are editorial section markers, not Bunyan’s chapter numbers.

“wilderness of this world”

What does the noun suggest about the narrator’s surroundings? What changes when “this world” makes that landscape more than a physical location?

“I lighted on”

Here, “lighted on” means came upon. Paraphrase the sentence, then compare the pace of your version with the original.

“I dreamed a dream”

Distinguish Bunyan, the dreaming narrator, and Christian. What does the dream frame allow the storyteller to show?

After noticing: a worked example

Observation: the narrator calls the world a wilderness. Interpretation: this frames ordinary life as a place of uncertainty or danger. Next test: find how Christian reads his own surroundings in the following paragraph. The “Den” is traditionally associated with Bunyan’s imprisonment; that historical association is different from something explicitly named in this sentence.

Your reading sessions

Scene titles reveal locations, but plot outcomes and interpretation notes stay closed until you open them. Read only as far as your group has reached. Check off a session after recording evidence; these checks are for your reading record, not a grade.

0 of 12 sessions marked read.

01 · The dream and the departure

Read in your book: Begin with the opening dream (after the Author’s Apology, if included). Read through Christian leaving his family, the conversations with Obstinate and Pliable, and Help’s rescue at the Slough of Despond. Stop before Mr. Worldly Wiseman appears.

Before reading: Predict from the title: what might “progress” measure besides distance?

While reading · notice

Track the book, the burden, and the directions Christian receives. Compare what Christian and Pliable each expect from the journey.

After reading · discuss

Why does Pliable change direction? Use his words before and after the Slough rather than his name alone.

Track in your journal: book / burden / help. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

The burden represents the conviction of sin, not just a generic worry. Help describes the Slough in terms of fears and doubts arising from that conviction. A modern connection can illuminate the scene, but does not replace this theological meaning.

02 · Advice, the gate, and the Interpreter

Read in your book: Read from Mr. Worldly Wiseman’s arrival through the Wicket Gate and the whole visit to the Interpreter’s House. Stop as Christian leaves the Interpreter to continue his journey.

Before reading: What would make advice trustworthy? Keep this question open while reading.

While reading · notice

List the route Worldly Wiseman recommends and Evangelist’s objections. At the Interpreter’s House, choose one demonstration and distinguish the picture from its explanation.

After reading · discuss

Why teach with a dusty room, a fire, or a picture instead of only a speech? Explain how one detail changes your understanding.

Track in your journal: guidance / appearances / grace. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

Worldly Wiseman directs Christian toward Legality and Morality. Bunyan is distinguishing salvation through Christ from relying on law and one’s own efforts. The Interpreter gives demonstrations whose explanations constrain, rather than eliminate, a reader’s interpretation.

03 · The cross and the climb

Read in your book: Read from the cross and sepulchre through Simple, Sloth, Presumption, Formalist, Hypocrisy, and Hill Difficulty. Continue through the lost roll and the lions to Christian’s reception at Palace Beautiful.

Before reading: Will relief end the journey, or change the kind of struggle? Make a prediction before reading.

While reading · notice

Track the burden, the clothing, and the roll. Note what happens during Christian’s rest and how that changes his route.

After reading · discuss

How does the lost roll complicate the idea that Christian is always moving forward? Support your answer with two moments.

Track in your journal: burden / roll / false entrances. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

The burden falls at the cross early in Part One; this is not the book’s midpoint. Forgiveness does not end the pilgrimage. Formalist and Hypocrisy enter over the wall, raising the question of how someone enters the way, not only how they look on it.

04 · Hospitality and preparation

Read in your book: Read the conversations and stay at Palace Beautiful, including the armoury. Stop when Christian leaves and begins descending into the Valley of Humiliation.

Before reading: What can a community offer a traveller that solitude cannot?

While reading · notice

Notice the questions Discretion, Piety, Prudence, and Charity ask. Identify a question that makes Christian reflect rather than merely report.

After reading · discuss

What do the conversations add that food, rest, and armour alone could not? Cite a question and Christian’s response.

Track in your journal: companionship / memory / equipment. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

The palace is commonly read as an image of Christian fellowship and the church. Its hospitality includes inquiry and preparation. Compare the welcome here with other invitations you encounter later.

05 · Two valleys

Read in your book: Read the battle with Apollyon in the Valley of Humiliation and the journey through the Valley of the Shadow of Death. Stop before the extended conversation with Faithful.

Before reading: How might a loud battle and a quiet passage create different kinds of suspense?

While reading · notice

Compare weapons, sounds, light, and speech in the two valleys. Find a moment when Christian misreads what he hears.

After reading · discuss

How does Bunyan make an inward struggle feel like physical danger? Choose one detail from each valley.

Track in your journal: voice / armour / fear. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

Apollyon challenges Christian’s allegiance and accuses him of failures. In the next valley, prayer and the recognition of another voice matter alongside physical weapons. Bunyan draws on biblical images; the valleys are distinct episodes, not one generic fear scene.

06 · Faithful and Talkative

Read in your book: Read Christian’s meeting with Faithful, Faithful’s account of his own journey, and the encounter with Talkative. Stop after Evangelist’s warning, before entering Vanity Fair.

Before reading: Can two travellers follow the same way but experience different trials?

While reading · notice

Compare one of Faithful’s experiences with Christian’s. Then mark where Faithful’s opinion of Talkative changes and why.

After reading · discuss

What evidence distinguishes religious speech from a changed life in this encounter? Does a name alone prove the case?

Track in your journal: speech / action / companionship. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

Talkative gives impressive religious answers. Christian and Faithful test those answers against conduct. Bunyan’s concern is the relation of profession to practice, not a rule that all talk is worthless.

07 · Vanity Fair

Read in your book: Read the description of the fair, the arrest, the trial, and its aftermath. Stop when Christian and Hopeful are travelling together after the fair.

Before reading: What kinds of things can be bought? What ought not to be for sale?

While reading · notice

Catalogue what is offered for sale. Separate the pilgrims’ actions from what the witnesses claim about them; notice the names of the judge and jurors.

After reading · discuss

How does the trial turn an institution meant to judge fairly into an instrument of persecution? Refer to a specific accusation.

Track in your journal: truth / judgement / witness. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

Faithful is executed; Hopeful joins Christian after witnessing the pilgrims’ conduct. The fair satirizes worldly values and persecution of dissenting faith. Comparisons with advertising or peer pressure need to account for the trial and violence as well as the marketplace.

08 · Profit and the easier path

Read in your book: Read the conversations with By-ends and his companions, the encounter with Demas and the silver mine, the pillar of salt, and the river of life. Continue through By-path Meadow and the capture at Doubting Castle.

Before reading: How could comfort or profit affect the way someone explains a moral choice?

While reading · notice

Compare By-ends’ reasons with the pilgrims’ replies. Track who proposes leaving the path and who hesitates.

After reading · discuss

Why does the easier path seem convincing? Explain how the setting and the dialogue work together.

Track in your journal: profit / leadership / shortcuts. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

Bunyan tests religion adopted for social or material advantage. In By-path Meadow, Christian’s confidence influences Hopeful; the guide itself can be mistaken. Distinguish the desire for rest from the choice to abandon the way.

09 · Doubting Castle

Read in your book: Read the imprisonment by Giant Despair and Diffidence, the pilgrims’ conversations, and their escape. Stop after they return to the way and leave a warning for others.

Before reading: This episode includes violence and pressure toward self-destruction. Focus discussion on the characters, the language, and the sources of help; personal disclosure is never required.

While reading · notice

Notice how the dialogue between the companions changes. Track what Christian remembers and what the travellers do after leaving.

After reading · discuss

Why name the key Promise? Explain both the concrete action of the key and its theological meaning.

Track in your journal: promise / memory / mutual help. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

The key evokes God’s promises, not simply optimistic thinking. Hopeful’s companionship and Christian’s remembrance both matter. This is an allegorical account of spiritual despair, not a medical explanation or treatment of depression.

10 · The mountains and misleading guides

Read in your book: Read the Delectable Mountains and the shepherds’ warnings; continue through Ignorance, Little-faith, the Flatterer, and Atheist. Stop as Christian and Hopeful enter the Enchanted Ground.

Before reading: What have the travellers learned about accepting directions? Predict a test of that learning.

While reading · notice

Keep a list of warnings, directions, and forgotten instructions. Compare a guide who looks helpful with one whose actions prove helpful.

After reading · discuss

Why do experienced pilgrims still need correction? Use the Flatterer episode to revise an earlier claim about progress.

Track in your journal: sight / discernment / correction. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

The shepherds offer instruction and a glimpse toward the destination. Later, the Flatterer’s appearance deceives the pilgrims. Little-faith’s story also complicates the equation of weak confidence with complete failure.

11 · Wakefulness and the grounds of hope

Read in your book: Read the Enchanted Ground, Hopeful’s account of his conversion, and the renewed conversation with Ignorance. Stop before the description of the country of Beulah.

Before reading: What makes a long conversation an action in a journey story?

While reading · notice

Compare Hopeful’s account of change with Ignorance’s reasons for confidence. Identify the questions Christian uses to test each account.

After reading · discuss

How does dialogue keep the travellers moving here? Explain what their disagreement reveals about the book’s idea of faith.

Track in your journal: wakefulness / testimony / belief. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

In Bunyan’s theology, confidence based on personal goodness differs from faith in Christ. The discussion makes that distinction explicit. Readers can explain the argument accurately while also asking how fairly the narrative treats opposing voices.

12 · The river, the city, and the last surprise

Read in your book: Read from Beulah through the river crossing and the arrival at the city. Read on through Ignorance’s outcome and the narrator waking; do not stop at the pilgrims’ welcome.

Before reading: Before reading, predict whether Bunyan will close with comfort, warning, or both.

While reading · notice

Compare Christian’s and Hopeful’s experience of the river. Then compare how Christian, Hopeful, and Ignorance approach entry to the city.

After reading · discuss

Why place Ignorance’s ending after the welcome? How does the last scene revise your understanding of the title?

Track in your journal: death / assurance / ending. Record a phrase, a page or scene, and a question.

After reading: context and interpretation (contains spoilers)

The river represents death and the city heavenly life. Hopeful supports Christian, but arrival is not simply a reward for endurance. Ignorance’s rejection closes with a warning about false assurance and returns the reader to Bunyan’s theological purpose.

Tools for a difficult passage

Untangle the sentence

Find the speaker and main verb. Replace unfamiliar words. Say the sentence in your own words, then reread the original to see what your paraphrase loses.

Read on two levels

Literal: a person does something in a place. Allegorical: that event embodies a spiritual condition or argument. Explain the bridge with a detail, not just a character’s name.

Listen to the dialogue

Who asks questions? Who changes the subject? Who gives reasons? Read a short exchange with a partner and explain how the voices differ.

Allegory
A sustained narrative in which people, places, and events carry another level of meaning.
Personification
Giving an abstract quality a human form, voice, or action; one of Bunyan’s tools for allegory.
Slough / despond
A bog or muddy hollow / loss of hope. “Slough” here rhymes with “cow.”
Wicket
A small gate, often within or beside a larger one.
Sepulchre
A tomb or burial place.
Vanity
Emptiness or worthlessness as well as pride in appearance; consider both senses at the fair.
Grace
In this theological context, God’s freely given favour, rather than something a person earns.
Justification
Being accounted righteous before God; central to the book’s distinction between faith and reliance on personal merit.
Profession / conversation
“Profession” can mean a declared belief; “conversation” can mean conduct or manner of life, not just talking.
Thou / thee / thy / ye
You (subject) / you (object) / your / plural you. Translate the pronouns before tracing an argument.

Biblical references: when your edition supplies a margin note, read the referenced passage if available. Ask what image or argument Bunyan borrows. Keep a quotation from Bunyan distinct from the biblical source he draws on.

After Part One: bring the evidence together

Return to the guiding question. Choose one task and support it with at least three details from different episodes. Explain a complication instead of only collecting examples that agree.

Is progress a straight line?

Trace a mistake, a correction, and a later test. Argue how Christian changes, and what still makes him vulnerable.

Seeming and being

Compare two persuasive speakers or welcoming places. How does the story teach a reader to judge them? Include a case in which appearances mislead.

A visible inner life

Track one motif—burden, book, roll, path, sleep, or companionship. Explain how its role changes over the journey. Cite a scene that complicates your first reading.

Creative extension: write a short allegorical scene about a difficult choice. Make a setting or object carry meaning, then annotate two craft choices with comparisons to Bunyan. A modern analogy is a new creation; explain where it differs from his spiritual journey.

Continuing with Part Two

Follow Christiana, Mercy, the children, and Great-heart with the same evidence-journal method on paper or in a downloaded copy. Compare the departure, encounters on the shared road, and the role of a guide. How do family life, care for others, and differing abilities alter the journey? Record both a repeated scene and a significant change. The 12 sessions above cover Part One only.

Self-review: Did I locate my evidence? Distinguish events from interpretations? Explain the theological meaning before a modern analogy? Consider an alternative? Revise a claim after discussion?

Bunyan’s Life in Brief

  1. 1628: Born in Elstow near Bedford, the son of a tinker who mended pots and pans.
  2. 1644–47: Serves as a soldier during the English Civil War.
  3. 1650s: Undergoes a long spiritual struggle, joins a Bedford congregation, and becomes a preacher.
  4. 1660: After the monarchy is restored, is arrested for preaching without a license and jailed for about twelve years.
  5. 1666: Publishes his spiritual autobiography, Grace Abounding.
  6. 1678: Publishes The Pilgrim’s Progress, Part One.
  7. 1684: Publishes Part Two.
  8. 1688: Dies in London and is buried in Bunhill Fields.
