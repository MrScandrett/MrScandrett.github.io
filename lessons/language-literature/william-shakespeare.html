William Shakespeare

His plays were not written to sit quietly on library shelves. They were written to be shouted, whispered, interrupted, and acted before roaring crowds in open-air wooden theaters.

The Three Pillars of Elizabethan Theater

The City

London

A booming port of about 200,000 people. Plays competed with street markets, river traffic, church bells, and bear-baiting rings for attention.

The Stage

The Globe

Built in 1599. Up to 3,000 spectators stood in the yard for a penny or sat in covered galleries under daylight with no electric lights or microphones.

The Book

The First Folio

In 1623, fellow actors John Heminge and Henry Condell compiled 36 plays. Without their effort, masterpieces like Macbeth and The Tempest would be lost.

Interactive Lab: The Heartbeat Rhythm (Iambic Pentameter)

Why does Shakespeare sound so memorable? Most of his dialogue is written in iambic pentameter: five pairs of syllables that follow a human heartbeat rhythm: da-DUM, da-DUM, da-DUM, da-DUM, da-DUM (10 syllables total).

💡 Actor's Rule: When a character breaks the regular 10-syllable heartbeat, it means their emotions are breaking down or their world is falling apart.

Three Ways a Story Can Turn

Reunion & Wit

Comedy

Disguises, mistaken identity, and chaotic love that resolves in marriage and celebration (e.g., Much Ado About Nothing, Twelfth Night).

Catastrophe & Choice

Tragedy

A noble character makes a fateful choice driven by flaw, ambition, or blinded judgment, ending in sorrow (e.g., Macbeth, Hamlet).

Crowns & War

History

Rival kings and rebellious nobles fight over who has the rightful claim to the throne of England (e.g., Henry V, Richard III).

Performance Lab: One Line, Three Deliveries

In Act 1, scene 7 of Macbeth, the general contemplates assassinating King Duncan in his own castle. How an actor delivers this single sentence alters the audience's understanding of his conscience:

“If it were done when ’tis done, then ’twere well
It were done quickly.”
Afraid: Pause after “done.” Let “quickly” come out as a terrified wish to escape judgment. Notice how the repetition of “done” feels like an echo he cannot silence.

Discussion Question: Which delivery makes Macbeth seem more dangerous: when he is shaking with terror, or when he is cold and matter-of-fact? Find a partner and recite the line both ways.

Essential Theater Jargon for Grades 6–12

Soliloquy vs. Monologue

A soliloquy is spoken by an actor alone on stage, revealing their private inner truth to the audience. A monologue is a long speech delivered to other characters in the room.

The Aside

A quick, private remark whispered directly to the audience that other characters on stage pretend not to hear. It creates a secret partnership between the actor and the crowd.

Dramatic Irony

When the audience knows a crucial secret that a character on stage does not (e.g., we know Juliet took a sleeping potion, but Romeo genuinely believes she is dead).

Verse vs. Prose

Kings and philosophers speak in dignified, rhythmic verse (iambic pentameter). Everyday workers, comedic clowns, and characters losing their minds speak in ordinary prose.

Choose Your Starting Scene

Grades 6–9 Track

Start with Action & Surprise

Read A Midsummer Night's Dream, Act 3, scene 1. Watch a group of bumbling amateur actors rehearse a play in the enchanted woods until magical mischief transforms one of them.

Look for: How does Shakespeare use stage directions and sound effects for comedy?

Grades 9–12 Track

Start with Foreshadowing & Fate

Read the Prologue to Romeo and Juliet. In fourteen lines of a sonnet, the Chorus tells the audience the ending before the play even starts.

Look for: How does knowing the ending create intense suspense rather than spoiling the story?

Exit Ticket: Pick any three lines from Shakespeare. Mark which syllables are stressed, and write one acting direction instructing an actor where to pause for breath or look at the audience.
