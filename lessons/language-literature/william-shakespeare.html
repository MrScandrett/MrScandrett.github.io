Language & Literature · Drama

William Shakespeare

His words were written to be spoken, interrupted, and acted in front of people. Start with a question: how does a line change when an actor changes the way it is said?

Big idea: A play gives us words and actions; performers and audiences help make their meaning.

Shakespeare (1564–1616) was an actor, playwright, and shareholder in a London theater company. His plays moved between public stages, royal courts, and, later, an indoor theater. We know some facts from records, but much of his private life remains uncertain.

A playwright in a working theater

Company

Shakespeare wrote for actors in the Lord Chamberlain's Men, later the King's Men. A scene had to work with bodies, voices, entrances, and a live audience.

Stage

Many plays were performed at the Globe, which opened in 1599. Imagine the audience close enough to react while an actor speaks.

Book

In 1623, fellow actors John Heminge and Henry Condell helped publish the First Folio. Some plays survive in print because of that collection.

Three ways a story can turn

Comedy

Confusion, disguise, and mistaken love often lead toward reunion. In A Midsummer Night's Dream, amateur actors rehearse a play inside the play.

Tragedy

Choices, conflict, and limited knowledge can move toward loss. In Macbeth, ambition presses against conscience.

History

Rulers fight over power and legitimacy. A history play interprets the past for an audience; it is not a neutral record.

These are useful starting categories, not rules that every scene follows.

Performance lab: one line, three choices

Read the opening line of Macbeth's Act 1, scene 7. He is thinking about killing King Duncan. Predict which delivery will make him sound most certain; then try each aloud.

“If it were done when ’tis done, then ’twere well / It were done quickly.”
Afraid: Pause after “done.” Let “quickly” come out as a wish to escape the consequences. What words suggest he is hesitating?

Discuss: Which delivery fits the rest of the speech best? Read the full scene at the Folger Shakespeare Library and cite a later line that supports or challenges your choice.

Choose a first scene

Start with conflict

Read the prologue to Romeo and Juliet. Mark what the audience learns before the characters do. How does that knowledge change the opening?

Start with performance

Read A Midsummer Night's Dream, Act 3, scene 1. Find the moment rehearsal turns into surprise. What must an actor show without explaining it?

Exit ticket: Choose one short line, write two different acting directions for it, and explain which word in the line supports each direction.
