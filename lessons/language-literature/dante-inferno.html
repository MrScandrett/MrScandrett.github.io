Dante’s Inferno

A pilgrim, a guide, and nine circles of Hell

In about 1308, an exile from Florence began a poem in which he walks through Hell, climbs Mount Purgatory, and ascends to Heaven. The Inferno is its first third. Dante wrote it in the everyday Italian of his time instead of Latin, and he filled its circles with real people, so the poem reads as both a story and a judgment on his world.

ContrapassoTerza rimaAllegoryGuide and pilgrim
Big Idea

Every punishment in the Inferno is designed to mirror the sin. The souls are not simply tortured at random. Each one becomes a permanent picture of the choice that ruled their life.

Quick Facts

AuthorDante Alighieri, born Florence, 1265; died Ravenna, 1321
WorkThe Divine Comedy: Inferno, Purgatorio, Paradiso (about 14,000 lines)
Inferno34 cantos: 1 introduction plus 33 for Hell
FormTerza rima, interlocking three-line stanzas rhyming aba, bcb, cdc
Fictional dateHoly Week of the year 1300, when Dante was 35

The Poem in Three Moves

The poem opens with a quotation you can read in two languages.

Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita mi ritrovai per una selva oscura, ché la diritta via era smarrita.Inferno, Canto I. Longfellow’s 1867 translation: “Midway upon the journey of our life / I found myself within a forest dark, / For the straightforward pathway had been lost.”

1. Lost

Dante wakes in a dark wood, halfway through life, unable to climb the sunlit hill. Three beasts block his way, and he cannot save himself.

2. Guided

The Roman poet Virgil, who stands for human reason, offers to lead him through Hell. A woman named Beatrice, sent from Heaven, had asked Virgil to help.

3. Changed

Dante learns by looking at sin honestly. He comes out at the other side of the earth and sees the stars again.

The last line

Inferno ends with “E quindi uscimmo a riveder le stelle,” roughly “and from there we came out to see the stars again.” Each of the three books ends with the word stelle (stars).

Map and Walk the Descent

Hell is a funnel, not a maze: each ring is wider and less severe near the top, narrower and worse near the bottom. Pick a stop, from the map or the list, to see its sin, punishment, art, a quotation, and who Dante meets there.

Lasciate ogne speranza, voi ch’intrate.Canto III, the inscription over the gate. Longfellow: “All hope abandon, ye who enter in!”

Where

Cantos 1–3: the dark wood, then the gate and the vestibule.

Who is here

The uncommitted, who never chose good or evil and now chase a blank banner forever.

Punishment

Stung by wasps and hornets. They lived without commitment, so they are always chasing something without meaning.

This miserable mode / Maintain the melancholy souls of those / Who lived withouten infamy or praise.Canto III, on the uncommitted. Longfellow’s translation.

What to notice

Even the shore before Hell has a punishment. Dante’s moral world has no neutral place: refusing to choose counts as a choice.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • The uncommitted angels, who sided neither with God nor with Satan’s rebellion
  • The nameless human uncommitted, who lived without praise or blame
  • “He who made through cowardice the great refusal” — traditionally read as Pope Celestine V, who resigned the papacy in 1294
  • Charon, ferryman of the dead across the river Acheron

That one is Homer, Poet sovereign; / He who comes next is Horace, the satirist; / The third is Ovid, and the last is Lucan.Canto IV, Virgil naming the four poets. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Not a sin. These souls lived before Christ or were unbaptized.

Punishment

Longing without hope. Their sighs make the air tremble.

Who

Homer, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, and Virgil himself.

What to notice

Virgil lives here, so his guiding Dante has a personal cost: he can lead Dante near Heaven but cannot enter it. Dante honors the great pagan thinkers even as his theology puts them out of Heaven.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Homer, Horace, Ovid, and Lucan — the four great poets who welcome Dante as their equal
  • Virgil, Dante’s guide, who lives here permanently
  • Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle (“the master of those who know”)
  • Julius Caesar, Saladin, and other virtuous rulers and warriors
  • Unbaptized infants

Love, that exempts no one beloved from loving, / Seized me with pleasure of this man so strongly, / That, as thou seest, it doth not yet desert me.Canto V, Francesca speaking. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Letting desire override reason.

Punishment

Blown endlessly about by a violent storm.

Who

Francesca da Rimini and her lover Paolo.

There is no greater sorrow / Than to be mindful of the happy time / In misery.Canto V, Francesca on remembering happiness. Longfellow’s translation.

What to notice

Contrapasso in action: the lovers were swept away by passion, and now a wind sweeps them away forever. Francesca speaks beautifully but blames a romance book and never her own choice, and Dante faints from pity. Look for how the poet lets the reader feel the pull of the sin.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Francesca da Rimini and Paolo Malatesta, murdered lovers who speak together
  • Minos, the judge who assigns souls to their circle by coiling his tail
  • Helen of Troy, Cleopatra, and Dido, named among history’s famous lovers
  • Achilles, Paris, and Tristan, named in the same company

Cerberus, monster cruel and uncouth, / With his three gullets like a dog is barking / Over the people that are there submerged.Canto VI. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Living for appetite.

Punishment

Lying in freezing, filthy rain, watched over by the three-headed dog Cerberus.

Who

Ciacco, a Florentine, who predicts trouble for the city.

What to notice

Indulgence turns into mud. The pleasure that once felt warm and generous becomes cold, dirty, and repetitive.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Ciacco, a Florentine glutton who predicts the city’s coming factional violence
  • Cerberus, the three-headed hound who guards and mauls the gluttons
  • The gluttonous dead in general, unnamed, lying face-down in the mire

Rolling weights forward by main force of chest… / Crying, “Why keepest?” and, “Why squanderest thou?”Canto VII. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Hoarding money or wasting it.

Punishment

Two crowds push huge weights in opposite directions, collide, and shout “Why do you hoard?” and “Why do you squander?”

Who

Popes and clergy among them.

What to notice

Both extremes fail. The souls are so wrapped up in the same obsession that their faces are unrecognizable, which is Dante’s comment on what money does to identity.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Unnamed clergy — Virgil says many were “clerks… Popes and Cardinals”
  • Plutus, the god of wealth, who guards the circle and collapses when Virgil rebukes him
  • The hoarders and the spendthrifts, so disfigured by obsession that none can be recognized

A marsh it makes, which has the name of Styx… / They smote each other not alone with hands, / But with the head and with the breast and feet.Canto VII–VIII. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Anger, and its hidden twin, sullenness.

Punishment

The angry fight each other on the surface of the river Styx while the sullen gurgle beneath the mud.

Who

Filippo Argenti, an enemy of Dante.

What to notice

Dante shows anger as both loud and silent. Look at how Dante himself reacts to Argenti, and whether the poet shares in the sin he is judging.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Filippo Argenti, a Florentine of a rival family, whom Dante watches other souls tear apart
  • Phlegyas, the furious boatman who rows Dante and Virgil across the Styx
  • The wrathful, fighting on the surface, and the sullen, submerged and gurgling beneath the mud

Behold there Farinata who has risen… / He uprose erect with breast and front / E’en as if Hell he had in great despite.Canto X. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Rejecting the teaching of the Church, here mainly denying the soul’s survival.

Punishment

Locked in fiery tombs inside the walls of the City of Dis.

Who

Farinata, a proud Florentine leader, rises from his tomb as if he despised Hell itself.

What to notice

The tomb was where they said the soul ended, and now it is where they are trapped. The city gate marks the shift from sins of weakness to sins of the will.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Farinata degli Uberti, a Florentine Ghibelline leader, defiant even in his tomb
  • Cavalcante de’ Cavalcanti, who rises to ask after his son, the poet Guido Cavalcanti
  • Pope Anastasius II, whose tomb marks the entrance (mentioned in Canto XI)
  • Epicurus and his followers, condemned here for denying the soul’s immortality

Then stretched I forth my hand a little forward, / And plucked a branchlet off from a great thorn; / And the trunk cried, “Why dost thou mangle me?”Canto XIII. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Violence against neighbors, against oneself, and against God and nature.

Punishment

A boiling river of blood, a forest of thorny trees (the suicides), and a plain of burning sand.

Who

Pier della Vigna, a suicide turned into a tree.

What to notice

Contrapasso at its most literal. Those who took their own lives gave up their bodies, so they never get them back. Their branches bleed when snapped, and Dante makes the reader feel the horror and the pity.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Pier della Vigna, a suicide and once the trusted chancellor of Emperor Frederick II
  • Capaneus, a blaspheming giant who still defies the gods on the burning sand
  • The Minotaur, guarding the entrance to this ring, and the Centaurs who patrol the river of blood
  • Lano and Jacopo da Sant’Andrea, profligates chased and torn apart by black hounds

Ye were not made to live like unto brutes, / But for pursuit of virtue and of knowledge.Canto XXVI, Ulysses to his crew. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Deceiving those who had no reason to distrust you.

Punishment

Ten separate ditches called Malebolge, including flatterers in filth, fortune-tellers with heads on backward, and hypocrites in gilded lead cloaks.

Who

Ulysses, who urged his crew to follow “virtue and knowledge” past the edge of the known world, and is punished as an evil counselor.

What to notice

Fortune-tellers tried to look into the future, so now they can only see behind them. The more elaborate the punishment, the more cleverly Dante turns a sin into an image.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Ulysses and Diomedes, sharing one flame as evil counselors
  • Guido da Montefeltro, deceived into sin by a corrupt pope
  • Pope Nicholas III, a simoniac stuffed head-down in a stone well
  • Jason and Thaïs, named among the seducers and flatterers
  • Vanni Fucci and other thieves, transformed and reborn as serpents

The Emperor of the kingdom dolorous / From his mid-breast forth issued from the ice.Canto XXXIV. Longfellow’s translation.

Sin

Betraying those who trusted you: family, country, guests, and benefactors.

Punishment

Frozen in the lake Cocytus, the deeper the betrayal, the deeper the ice.

Who

At the center, Satan chews Judas, Brutus, and Cassius.

What to notice

Hell is not fire at the bottom. It is ice, the total absence of warmth and love. Dante and Virgil climb down Satan’s body, pass the center of gravity, and flip upside down toward the surface.

Who Dante meets or names here
  • Satan (Dis, Lucifer), three-faced and chest-deep in ice at the center of the universe
  • Judas Iscariot, chewed in Satan’s central mouth for betraying Christ
  • Brutus and Cassius, chewed in the side mouths for betraying Julius Caesar
  • Count Ugolino, gnawing the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri nearby, in the ring for traitors to country
  • Friar Alberigo and Branca d’Oria, traitors to their guests, whose souls fell to Hell while their bodies still walked the earth

Match the Sin to the Punishment

Contrapasso means the punishment echoes the sin. Choose the punishment that fits.

Tools Dante Uses

Use these terms when you talk or write about the poem.

Contrapasso

“Suffer the opposite” or “suffer in kind.” The punishment mirrors or answers the sin.

Terza rima

Dante invented this rhyme scheme (aba bcb cdc). Each stanza links to the next like a chain, so the poem keeps moving forward.

Number symbolism

Three (the Trinity) shapes everything: three books, cantos in groups of 33, three-line stanzas, and a hundred cantos in total.

Allegory

Dante said the poem can be read four ways: literally, as an allegory, morally, and as a spiritual journey to God. The dark wood is both a forest and a life gone wrong.

Virgil and Beatrice

Virgil stands for human reason, which can take you far but not all the way. Beatrice, for Dante, stands for divine love and revelation.

The vernacular

Dante wrote in Tuscan Italian, not scholarly Latin, so ordinary people could read the poem. It helped make his dialect the basis of modern Italian.

Dante’s Life in Brief

  1. 1265: Born in Florence to a family of minor nobility.
  2. 1274: Sees Beatrice Portinari for the first time, when both are about nine. She becomes his lifelong symbol of love.
  3. 1290: Beatrice dies at about 24.
  4. 1295–1300: Enters politics as Florence’s factions, the Whites and Blacks, split. He serves as one of the city’s six ruling priors in 1300.
  5. 1302: Exiled from Florence and sentenced to death if he returns. He never sees the city again.
  6. c. 1308–1320: Writes the Divine Comedy in exile.
  7. 1321: Dies in Ravenna, soon after finishing Paradiso.

Try It Yourself

Dante’s Hell is a moral map. Draw your own.

Contrapasso challenge

Choose a modern habit (gossip, procrastination, doom-scrolling). Design a punishment that mirrors it, and explain the link in one sentence.

Compare the pictures

Look at Botticelli’s map and Doré’s gate. What does each artist emphasize? Which one helps you understand the poem’s structure better?

Argue it

Francesca blames a book for her fate. Is that fair? Use lines from Canto V to support your view.

Guide and pilgrim

Virgil represents reason. When have you needed reason to guide you, and when did you need something else?
