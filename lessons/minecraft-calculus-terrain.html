The calculus hiding inside every Minecraft mountain

Every time Minecraft generates a new world, it is quietly running calculus: stacking wave functions to sculpt terrain, measuring slope with a derivative, and moving water downhill by following the steepest gradient. Build all three yourself.

Big idea: a "height function" f(x, y) plus its derivative (the slope) can generate an entire landscape and decide exactly where water goes.

Terrain is a function

Minecraft never stores every mountain by hand. It evaluates a math function at every (x, z) column and turns the output into a height.

📈 f(x, y)

A function of two inputs (position east/west and north/south) that outputs one number: the ground height at that spot.

🌊 Continuous

Real terrain has no sudden jumps between neighboring columns — the height function has to change smoothly, the same way a calculus function must be continuous to have a derivative.

🎲 Deterministic randomness

The "randomness" in a Minecraft world is actually a fixed function of the seed and coordinates — plug in the same numbers and you always get the same mountain.

Think of it like a giant spreadsheet: column A is x, column B is y (called z in-game), and column C is a formula that computes height from A and B. Missions 2–4 build that formula from scratch.

📜 History Files: from graph paper to procedural worlds

1983 · Ken Perlin's noise functionWorking on visual effects for the movie Tron, Ken Perlin invented "Perlin noise" — a fast way to compute a smooth, natural-looking random function of position. It won a Technical Achievement Academy Award and now runs inside almost every procedural landscape, including Minecraft's.
2009 · Minecraft's first terrain generatorMinecraft layers several noise functions at different frequencies (an "octave" stack, Mission 2) to get both huge mountain ranges and small bumpy detail from the same underlying math.
2

Stacking waves into mountains

A single sine wave looks like ripples, not mountains. Add several waves of different frequency and height together — "octaves" — and you get a believable landscape profile.

The formula: height(x) = Σ amplitudei · sin(frequencyi · x + phasei) — this sum-of-waves idea is the same one behind Fourier series (see the related lab below). Each new octave roughly doubles the frequency and halves the amplitude, adding fine detail without erasing the big shape.

One octave = one gentle wave. Slide up to see mountains gain sharp ridges and small bumps at the same time.
3

Slope, gradient & the derivative

The steepness of a hill at any point is literally the derivative of the height function. In two dimensions, the full "which way is steepest" answer is called the gradient.

📐 Derivative

df/dx tells you the slope of a 1D curve at a point — rise over run as the run shrinks toward zero.

🧭 Gradient (∇f)

For a hill f(x, y), the gradient is a vector pointing in the direction of steepest uphill climb, with a length equal to how steep that climb is.

🔻 Steepest descent

Walking in the exact opposite direction of the gradient, −∇f, is the fastest way downhill — precisely how water finds its path in Mission 4.

Hover or tap anywhere on the terrain map below. The computer can't take a perfect symbolic derivative of noisy terrain, so it does what engineers do in practice: a finite-difference approximation, nudging x and y by a tiny step h and measuring how much height changes — ∂f/∂x ≈ (f(x+h,y) − f(x,y)) / h.

The yellow arrow is −∇f: the direction a marble (or a drop of water) would start rolling if placed at your cursor. Longer arrow = steeper slope = faster acceleration downhill.
4

Water flow & steepest descent

Minecraft's water doesn't know any physics — it just repeatedly asks "which neighboring block is lower?" and flows there. That greedy rule is steepest descent in disguise.

Real-world version: gravity accelerates real water down the true gradient continuously, but the "always move toward the locally lowest spot" rule is such a good approximation that it is also how erosion-simulation software and machine-learning training (gradient descent) both work — same math, three very different uses.

Click anywhere on the terrain to drop water. It will step toward the steepest downhill neighbor until it reaches a local minimum (a lake bed) or the edge of the map.

Goal

Students describe terrain as a function of two variables, build a height profile by summing waves, interpret slope as a derivative and gradient, and connect steepest-descent water flow to gradient-based algorithms elsewhere in computing.

Materials

  • One device per pair or group
  • Projector for whole-class discussion
  • Optional: graphing calculator or graph paper for the 1D warm-up

60-minute flow

  1. Terrain is a function — 10 min
  2. Stacking waves into mountains — 15 min
  3. Slope, gradient & the derivative — 15 min
  4. Water flow & steepest descent — 20 min

Prerequisite check

Students should already be comfortable with slope as rise-over-run and reading a function graph (see the Graphing Calculator lab) before this lesson's jump to two variables.

Where the real math is simplified

Minecraft actually uses 3D Perlin/simplex noise, not literal stacked sine waves, but the sum-of-frequencies structure (an "octave stack" or fractal Brownian motion) is mathematically the same idea and much easier to compute by hand or read as a formula.

Common misconception

Students often think the gradient arrow points downhill. Clarify: ∇f points uphill (direction of increase); water and marbles move along −∇f.

Answer aloud or on paper

  1. What are the inputs and output of a Minecraft-style terrain function?
  2. Why does adding more octaves add detail without erasing the big mountains?
  3. What does the gradient of a height function tell you, and which direction does it point?
  4. How is "water flows to the lowest neighbor" related to a derivative?

Expected ideas

Inputs x and y (position); output is height. Higher octaves are higher-frequency, lower-amplitude waves layered on top, so they add small ripples without changing the overall shape. The gradient points in the steepest uphill direction and its size is the steepness; water follows the opposite direction, −∇f, which is the calculus meaning of "downhill fastest."
