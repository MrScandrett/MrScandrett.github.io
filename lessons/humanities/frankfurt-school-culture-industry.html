The Frankfurt School

Critical Theory and Adorno's "Culture Industry"

In 1923 a group of German scholars opened the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt. They wanted to understand why modern society, with all its science and wealth, still produced so much suffering and so little freedom. Within ten years the Nazis forced them out of Germany. In exile in America, two of them, Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer, watched Hollywood, radio, and advertising up close, and wrote one of the most argued-about essays of the twentieth century: The Culture Industry.

Big Idea

Entertainment is never just entertainment. Adorno and Horkheimer argued that movies, songs, and shows made like factory products also train us how to think, want, and obey. You do not have to agree with them, but after this lesson you should be able to state their argument fairly, spot it in action, and test it against your own experience.

What Makes a Theory "Critical"?

In 1937 Horkheimer wrote an essay called "Traditional and Critical Theory." It gave the school its name. His point: a theory can study society the way a chemist studies a sample, or it can ask whether society has to be the way it is.

Traditional theory

Describe and predict

  • Treats society as a set of facts to measure.
  • The researcher tries to stand outside, neutral.
  • Good results help the system run more smoothly.
  • Question it asks: What is happening, and how much?
Critical theory

Explain and change

  • Treats "the facts" as products of history that could have been different.
  • Admits the researcher lives inside the society being studied.
  • Good results show where people are less free than they could be.
  • Question it asks: Who benefits, what is hidden, and could it be otherwise?

True story: Adorno and the "like" button

In 1938 Adorno took a job with the Princeton Radio Research Project, led by the sociologist Paul Lazarsfeld. Researchers there tested programs with a device that let listeners press one button when they liked what they heard and another when they didn't. Adorno thought this missed the point: it measured reactions without asking where the tastes came from. He later recalled being told to "measure culture" and thinking that culture might be exactly the thing that can't be measured. The two men respected each other and kept arguing for years. The Culture Industry: The Argument in Seven Ideas

The chapter is dense, angry, and often funny. Here is its argument broken into seven ideas, in plain English, with a place to spot each one today. Quotations are from John Cumming's 1972 English translation.

The Blockbuster Machine

You are a studio executive. Choose a hero, a world, and the rest, and the machine writes the film. Change everything you like. Then switch on the X-ray and look at what never changes.

    What Adorno would point out

    The highlighted words feel like choices, and you did choose them. But the beats, the order, the timing, and the ending were decided before you sat down. That is pseudo-individuality: surface variety on top of an identical frame. Notice also that the machine pushes back on you, the executive. Adorno said the system disciplines the people who make culture, not just the people who buy it.

    But consider: sonnets have fourteen lines and hymns have meters, and nobody calls them factory goods. Is a formula always a cage, or can it be a tool? What would make the difference?

    Talking Back to Adorno

    A critical theory should be open to criticism too. Here are six serious responses, from friends, students, and opponents, with how Adorno might reply.

    Walter Benjamin · 1935

    Copies can set art free

    In "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility," Benjamin argued that photos and film strip art of its aura, the feeling of a unique original. That loss could be good: art leaves the museum and reaches everyone, and audiences become sharp critics.

    Adorno might reply: Reaching everyone is not the same as freeing everyone. The question is who controls the copies and what they are built to do.

    Stuart Hall · 1973

    Audiences are not sponges

    The British cultural theorist Stuart Hall argued that viewers "decode" media. Some accept the intended message, some negotiate with it, and some read it against the grain. Fans remix, mock, and repurpose what they are given.

    Adorno might reply: Choosing among readings someone else designed is still a narrow freedom. But he partly agreed (see "Free Time" below).

    Many critics · ongoing

    Snobbery about jazz

    Adorno wrote harshly about jazz in the 1930s, mostly from the commercial dance music he heard in Germany. Critics say he missed the improvisation and genius of Black American musicians, and that his taste for difficult classical music made him look down on ordinary people.

    Adorno might reply: His target was the industry, not the audience. Still, most scholars think the jazz essays are his weakest work.

    Georg Lukács · 1962

    The "Grand Hotel Abyss"

    The Hungarian philosopher Lukács mocked the Frankfurt thinkers for living in a comfortable "Grand Hotel Abyss": a beautiful hotel on the edge of despair, where you can enjoy gloomy theory between good meals and never actually act.

    Adorno might reply: Thinking clearly about why things go wrong is a kind of action, and rushing into action without thinking had produced disasters.

    Adorno himself · 1969

    "Free Time"

    Late in life Adorno studied how Germans watched a royal wedding on TV in 1966. They enjoyed the show, but most did not believe the hype about its importance. He concluded that people are not as completely captured as the 1944 chapter suggested; their real interests still push back.

    Notice: the author revised his own theory when evidence came in. That is a model of good thinking.

    Jürgen Habermas · 1962 onward

    Don't give up on reason

    Adorno's own former assistant thought Dialectic of Enlightenment was too dark. If reason itself turns into domination, how can we criticize anything? Habermas argued that free, honest public conversation can still correct a society's errors.

    Adorno might reply: Fine, but look at who owns the channels where that conversation happens.

    Run a Culture Industry Audit

    Pick one thing you watch, play, or listen to: a show, a song, a game, a feed. Rate it honestly on Adorno's six warning signs. Then pin it and audit something very different to compare, such as a novel, a hymn, a symphony, or something a friend made.

    From Radio to the Feed

    Adorno never saw a smartphone. Where does his argument fit today, and where does it break?

    Where it fits: the algorithm

    A recommendation feed learns your taste and serves more of the same. Adorno's "something is provided for all" now happens one person at a time.

    Where it fits: you are the product

    On free platforms, your attention is sold to advertisers. That is close to his claim that the customer is the object of the industry, not its king.

    Where it breaks: anyone can publish

    A teenager with a phone can reach millions without a studio. Benjamin's hope that copies would hand production to ordinary people looks partly true.

    Where it bends: creators chase trends

    Yet many independent creators copy the same sounds, formats, and thumbnails to please the algorithm. Is a formula still a formula when no executive imposes it?

    A note on the name

    Today you will hear "critical theory" used in news and political arguments, sometimes for later academic fields that borrowed the label and sometimes as a slogan. Historians use it narrowly for the Frankfurt School tradition. Good practice: when someone uses the term, ask which thinkers and which specific claims they mean, then check those claims against the sources.

    8. 1940 Benjamin dies at Portbou, on the French–Spanish border, while fleeing the Nazis.
    9. 1941 Horkheimer and Adorno move to Los Angeles, a few miles from Hollywood.
    10. 1944 Philosophical Fragments, the first version of Dialectic of Enlightenment, circulates in a small copied edition.
    11. 1947 Dialectic of Enlightenment is published in Amsterdam, including "The Culture Industry."
    12. 1950 The Authoritarian Personality studies what makes people drawn to dictators.
    13. 1951 The Institute reopens in a new building in Frankfurt.

    15. 1963 Adorno's radio talk "Culture Industry Reconsidered" restates the argument for a new generation.
    16. 1964 Marcuse's One-Dimensional Man becomes a bestseller among American students.
    17. 1969 Adorno dies in Switzerland, aged 65.

    Discussion and Writing

    1. State it fairly

    In three sentences, explain the culture industry argument so well that Adorno would nod. Only then say where you disagree.

    2. Formula or craft?

    Find a piece of entertainment that uses a formula and is still excellent. What makes it more than its formula?

    3. Your audit

    Compare your two audits. Which scored higher? Did the scores match how much you value each one? Why or why not?

    4. Who is free?

    Adorno says choosing among products is not real freedom. What would real freedom in culture look like for you?

    5. Made for more

    Adorno and Horkheimer were not religious, yet both often pointed to the biblical ban on graven images, and Horkheimer late in life wrote of a "longing for the totally other." Compare their critique with Romans 12:2, "be not conformed to this world." Where do they agree, and where do they part ways?

    6. Make something

    Benjamin hoped audiences would become makers. Create a short piece (song, comic, scene) that deliberately breaks one beat of the Blockbuster Machine. Does it still work?

    Teacher notes and answer key
    • Core claim: Film, radio, music, and magazines are made like industrial goods: standardized, centrally controlled, and designed to sell. This produces conformity while feeling like free choice and fun.
    • Why "industry": In "Culture Industry Reconsidered" (1963), Adorno explained that he and Horkheimer dropped the term "mass culture" so no one would think this culture rises up from the people themselves. It is made for them, from above.
    • Historical context: The authors had just watched propaganda and mass spectacle help the Nazis take power. Seeing similar techniques used to sell soap and films in America alarmed them. That context explains both their insight and their gloom.
    • Common misreading: Adorno is not saying popular things are bad because they are popular, or that ordinary people are stupid. He blames the system of production. His jazz writing is where that line is hardest to defend.
    • Quiz answers: trailer sounds and sequels = standardization; rebel pop star and phone colors = pseudo-individuality; binge before Monday = amusement as extension of work; song you hated then loved = plugging; perfect burger ad = the promise never kept; "made for you" playlist = consumer as object.
