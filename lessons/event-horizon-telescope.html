How radio measurements become image evidence

u–v coverage

This is not another map of Earth or the sky. It is a standard radio-astronomy graph showing which sizes and directions of structure the selected baselines measure.

  • Each colored curve comes from one telescope pair as Earth rotates during an eight-hour observation.
  • Distance from the center represents spatial frequency. Farther out means sensitivity to finer angular detail.
  • Direction around the center represents the orientation of the measured structure.
  • Mirrored curves are the expected conjugate measurements for a real-valued sky brightness image.

Important: one long curve does not make a complete picture. Reliable reconstruction needs coverage at many distances and directions.

Observe the causal chain: station pairs create baselines → baselines sample spatial frequencies → synchronized recordings can be correlated → algorithms reconstruct an image from incomplete data.

Movement I · Listen

One station hears change over time.

ALMA records the strength and phase of 1.3 mm radio waves arriving from M87*. Its hydrogen-maser clock gives every measurement an extremely precise time.

Why is that not already an image?

An image describes how brightness varies across different positions on the sky. One station records one combined signal from its direction toward M87*. By itself, it has no separated viewpoint to compare, so it cannot determine the source’s small-scale spatial structure.

What is real at this stage The radio signal and its timestamp are real data. The orange ring is not yet available from one station; it must be reconstructed from comparisons across an array.

Movement II · Connect

Relationship creates the first spatial evidence.

Why exactly two stations?

A baseline is the separation vector between a pair of telescopes. The correlator compares how the same radio wave arrives at ALMA and APEX. Differences in timing and phase provide one measurement of the source’s spatial structure. One station has nothing separated to compare with.

Why clock synchronization matters At 230 GHz, the radio wave oscillates about 230 billion times per second. Hydrogen-maser clocks let the correlator align recordings made thousands of kilometers apart. Turn synchronization off: the map still has a geometric baseline, but its signal phases can no longer be compared reliably.
Why one baseline is still insufficient This pair measures structure at one projected length and direction at each moment. Many different source shapes could match that limited evidence, so a complete-looking ring would be poorly constrained.

Movement III · Accumulate

Earth turns the array through new viewpoints.

As Earth rotates, every station pair changes its projected orientation toward M87*. Each pair therefore traces a curve in the u–v plot instead of contributing one ordinary image pixel.

Explain the four readouts
Station
One physical observatory recording the source.
Baseline
The separation vector between two stations. With n stations, the number of pairs is n(n−1)÷2.
Longest baseline
The greatest separation in the selected array. At the same wavelength, a longer baseline can distinguish smaller angles.
Nominal resolution
An estimate of the smallest angular detail supported by wavelength ÷ baseline. It does not guarantee a complete image; coverage and clean data still matter.

Movement IV · Resolve

An image emerges from incomplete measurements.

What reconstruction means The measurements are sparse, so independent imaging teams used multiple algorithms and tests. The published ring was a result supported across those methods—not a camera exposure and not a simulation simply chosen to match a prediction.
What the orange means The EHT detects radio intensity. The familiar orange palette is a display choice used to make brightness visible; it is not the object’s visible color and not a black-body temperature map.
What the image shows Hot plasma around M87* emits radio waves. Strong gravity bends those rays, producing a bright ring around a central brightness depression called the black-hole shadow. The shadow is larger than the event horizon; the image does not show the horizon’s surface.
Why the first image mattered The 2019 M87* result resolved horizon-scale structure and found a ring size consistent with general relativity. A separate 2022 result imaged Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the center of the Milky Way.
