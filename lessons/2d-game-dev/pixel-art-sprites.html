01 / WHY

Where this comes from

Small grids were a hardware limit before they were a style.

1980s

Arcade boards and home consoles had very little memory, so graphics were built from tiny blocks. The NES drew its world from 8 × 8 pixel tiles and could show only a handful of colours per sprite. Artists learned to say “plumber” or “ghost” with a few dozen pixels — the same skill you practise today.

Grid size is a budget

16 × 16 gives you 256 pixels. Every pixel must earn its place: eyes, cap, satchel, feet.

Palette is a budget

Fifteen colours plus transparent. Fewer colours make a cohesive look and force clear shapes.

Silhouette first

If the shape alone is readable, the colours only make it better. If it isn’t, no colour will rescue it.

Hitbox ≠ sprite

The courier is drawn in 16 × 16, but the game collides with a smaller 10 × 14 box. You will see why in lesson 06.

02 / DRAW

Sprite lab

Draw your courier.

  1. Block the silhouette.Use one dark colour to rough the head, body and feet. Turn on Mirror for symmetrical shapes.
  2. Add an outline.A one-pixel dark outline (colour 1) separates the hero from any background.
  3. Light and shadow.Pick one light direction. Use a lighter shade on that side and a darker one opposite.
  4. Test at 1×.Look at the smallest preview. Remove details that turn into noise.

03 / SCALE

Theory: scaling pixel art

Grow pixels into squares, never into blur.

Games draw pixel art at a small internal size and scale the whole screen up. Two rules keep it crisp:

  • Nearest-neighbour: each source pixel becomes a solid block. Smooth (bilinear) scaling averages neighbours and blurs edges.
  • Whole-number zoom: at 2.5× some pixels become 2 screen pixels wide and some 3, so lines wobble.
