Planet Field Guide · Stop 4 · Terrestrial World

Mars: Ancient Rivers in a Frozen Desert

Mars looks like a cold desert colored by iron-oxide rust, yet orbital cameras and rovers find ancient river deltas, lakebeds, and clay minerals that could only form in standing liquid water. Understanding how Mars shifted from a wetter youth to a thin-aired freezer is one of planetary science’s central mysteries.

Driving question: Why does liquid water evaporate or freeze immediately on Mars today—even on a warm +15 °C afternoon?

Geology & Scale

Record-Breaking Martian Landforms

Olympus Mons (21.9 km)

Nearly 2.5× Everest’s height above sea level and as wide as Arizona, built by one stationary volcanic hotspot over a crust without moving plates.

Valles Marineris (4,000 km)

A tectonic rift canyon system long enough to stretch across the United States, dropping up to 7 km deep—nearly 4× deeper than the Grand Canyon.

Jezero Crater Delta

An ancient lake basin where rivers deposited fan-shaped clay and carbonate sediments—now explored by NASA’s Perseverance rover for biosignatures.

Engineering · Entry, Descent & Landing

Autonomous Entry, Descent, and Touchdown

Landing a one-ton robotic rover on Mars requires decelerating from 12,000 miles per hour to a gentle stop in under seven minutes. Because radio transmissions take several minutes to reach Earth, depending on the planets’ positions, no human can pilot the craft live. The rover relies entirely on autonomous flight software, terrain-relative navigation cameras, supersonic parachutes, and a rocket-powered sky crane.
