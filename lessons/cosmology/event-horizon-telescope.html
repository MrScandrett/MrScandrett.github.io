How radio measurements become image evidence

u–v coverage (Fourier plane)

This is not a map of Earth or the sky. Each point represents a spatial-frequency sample measured by one telescope pair—its 2D inverse Fourier transform builds the Dirty Image.

  • Each colored curve is swept by one baseline as Earth rotates through the observing window.
  • Radius from the center equals spatial frequency in gigawavelengths (Gλ); dashed rings show equivalent angular scales (100, 50, 25 μas).
  • Angle around the center shows which direction across the black hole is constrained.
  • Mirrored tracks arise from conjugate symmetry for any real sky brightness distribution.

Important: One long track gives fine resolution in only one direction—leaving parallel fringes. Filling many radii and angles removes artifacts.

Causal chain: station pairs form baselines → hydrogen-maser clocks preserve wave phase → Earth rotation sweeps u–v tracks → inverse 2D FFT + CLEAN reconstructs the black-hole shadow.

Movement I · Listen

One station hears change over time.

ALMA records the electric field of 1.3 mm (230 GHz) radio waves arriving from M87*, stamped by a hydrogen-maser atomic clock accurate to trillionths of a second.

Why is that not already an image?

Look at the Receiver Scope on the stage: one telescope produces a single voltage stream over time. Without a second separated viewpoint to measure phase differences, there is no spatial-frequency data to reconstruct an image.

Why 1.3 mm radio light? Dust and gas in galactic cores block visible light, but 1.3 mm millimeter waves pierce straight out from the hot plasma orbiting just outside the event horizon.

Movement II · Connect

Compare a short pair against a long pair.

What does one baseline actually measure?

Comparing signals between two dishes measures a single 2D sine-wave component (a fringe) of the sky. Toggle between the 2.6 km Chile pair and the ~9,500 km Chile–Hawaii pair in Dirty FFT mode to see the difference directly.

Why atomic clock sync is non-negotiable At 230 GHz, a wave cycle lasts 4.3 femtoseconds. Turn Clock Sync OFF and watch the Correlator fringe lock collapse—scrambling the Fourier phases into noise.
Why one long baseline still fails ALMA + JCMT resolves tiny scales along one diagonal, producing sharp parallel stripes—not a closed ring. You need perpendicular baselines too.

Movement III · Accumulate

Earth turns the array through new viewpoints.

As Earth rotates, every baseline projects onto a changing angle and length toward the target—sweeping colored arcs across the u–v plot.

Explain the four readouts
Stations
Selected dishes currently above the target’s 12° horizon limit.
Active pairs
Simultaneously visible baselines. With n mutual sites, up to n(n−1)÷2 pairs form.
Longest chord
Straight-line 3D Earth chord between the two farthest active stations (capped by Earth’s 12,742 km diameter).
Nominal beam
Smallest angular detail supported by λ ÷ Bproj. Sidelobes remain unless u–v angles are well filled.

Movement IV · Resolve

From Dirty FFT fringes to a CLEAN ring.

Dirty FFT vs. CLEAN deconvolution Switch the stage toggle between Dirty FFT and Cleaned. Empty gaps in the u–v plane leave cross-shaped sidelobes in the Dirty image; Högbom CLEAN iteratively subtracts the Beam PSF to recover the true ring.
What the orange means EHT detects 1.3 mm radio brightness, not visible light. Click Gray or Ice below the canvas to confirm that color is a scientific visualization choice, not a property of the black hole.
Two black holes, two challenges M87* (6.5 billion Suns) is 1,500× larger than Sagittarius A* (4.0 million Suns), so gas orbits M87* over days while Sgr A* flickers in minutes. Switching to Sgr A* at declination −29° also brings the South Pole Telescope (SPT) into view!
