How the Big Bang made the first elements

In the first second the universe was too hot for anything to hold together. As it cooled, quarks bound into protons and neutrons. Between about one second and twenty minutes the whole universe was as hot and dense as the inside of a star, and protons and neutrons fused. This is Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

It made only the lightest nuclei: about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, plus tiny traces of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7. Then the universe was too thin and cool to fuse anything heavier. That prediction, worked out by Ralph Alpher and George Gamow in the 1940s, matches what we measure in the oldest, least-processed gas clouds. It is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the Big Bang.

So where did the carbon in your body, the oxygen you breathe, and the iron in your blood come from? Not from the Big Bang. Use the table below to find out.

We really are made of stardust

Stars are element factories. Inside a star like the Sun, hydrogen fuses into helium. In stars much more massive than the Sun, fusion builds carbon, oxygen, silicon, and finally iron in the core. When such a star runs out of fuel it collapses and explodes as a supernova, scattering those elements into space, where they mix into new clouds that form new stars and planets.

Elements heavier than iron mostly form when nuclei capture neutrons, either slowly inside aging giant stars or in seconds during violent events. In 2017 astronomers saw two neutron stars merge (event GW170817) and found signs of newly made gold and platinum in the glow that followed. How much of the gold in the universe comes from mergers, and how much from unusual supernovae, is still being worked out.

How the theory was built

The Big Bang was not one person's idea. It was assembled, and argued over, across about a century.

  1. 1915Einstein's general relativity describes gravity as the curving of spacetime. Applied to the whole universe, its equations say space should expand or contract. Einstein adds a "cosmological constant" to keep the universe static.
  2. 1922–24Alexander Friedmann solves Einstein's equations for an expanding universe.
  3. 1927Georges Lemaître, a Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, derives an expanding universe independently and shows it fits the redshifts already measured. In 1931 he proposes that everything began from a "primeval atom."
  4. 1929Edwin Hubble shows that more distant galaxies recede faster. This is the observation you will explore in the next lesson.
  5. 1948Alpher, Bethe & Gamow work out how a hot early universe could make light elements, and Alpher and Herman predict a leftover glow at a few kelvin. The same year, Bondi, Gold & Hoyle propose the rival Steady State model.
  6. 1949On BBC radio, Fred Hoyle, the Steady State's champion, calls the rival idea "this big bang." The name sticks.
  7. 1964Penzias & Wilson find an unexplained hiss in a radio antenna at Bell Labs. It is the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the glow predicted in 1948. Most astronomers now favor the Big Bang over Steady State.
  8. 1980sAlan Guth and others propose inflation, a burst of extremely fast expansion in the first fraction of a second, to explain why the universe is so smooth and flat.
  9. 1989–92The COBE satellite measures the CMB's spectrum with extreme precision and finds the first tiny temperature ripples in it.
  10. 1998Two supernova teams find that expansion is speeding up, pointing to "dark energy."
  11. 2001–18WMAP and Planck map the CMB ripples in detail, giving an age of about 13.8 billion years and a recipe of roughly 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, 68% dark energy.

Notice what the Big Bang theory does not claim: it describes how the universe evolved from a very hot, dense state onward. Why there was anything to expand, and whether "before" even makes sense, are open questions. That is where science shades into philosophy and theology.

A recap of Genesis: creation by spoken word

The Bible opens with an account of creation that is very different in style from a physics paper. It is a structured, poetic narrative built around a repeating pattern.

In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.

Genesis 1:1–3, King James Version

Notice the order of events in the text:

  1. Day 1"God said, Let there be light." Light is separated from darkness. Creation begins with a spoken word.
  2. Day 2The waters are divided, forming sky above and waters below.
  3. Day 3Dry land and seas appear, then plants.
  4. Day 4Sun, Moon, and stars are set in the sky. (Light was created on Day 1, before the Sun on Day 4.)
  5. Day 5Sea creatures and birds.
  6. Day 6Land animals, then humans, made "in the image of God."
  7. Day 7God rests. The pattern of six days of work and a day of rest is the root of the Sabbath.

The word before the light

Nine times in Genesis 1 the phrase "And God said" introduces the next act of creation. The rest of the Bible keeps returning to this idea:

By the word of the LORD were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth. … For he spake, and it was done; he commanded, and it stood fast.

Psalm 33:6, 9, King James Version

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. … All things were made by him.

John 1:1, 3, King James Version

So the text itself puts a spoken word before the light. That is the observation behind your question, and the next section looks at how it lines up with what physics says about the early universe.

Ways people read Genesis 1

Thoughtful believers have read the passage in several ways. A good student can describe each fairly, whichever they hold.

Literal six days

The days are ordinary 24-hour days, and the earth is young. Science is read in light of this.

Day-age

The "days" are long periods, and the order of Genesis roughly matches the order found by science.

Framework / literary

The days are a teaching structure: days 1–3 form realms, days 4–6 fill them. The point is who made the world and why, not a timeline.

Two books

Nature and Scripture are two revelations from one Author, and each answers different questions: how versus who and why.

A historical note: Georges Lemaître, who proposed the expanding universe and the "primeval atom," was a Catholic priest. He insisted that the physics of the beginning is a scientific question and Genesis is a religious one, and that neither should be used to "prove" the other. When Pope Pius XII suggested in 1951 that the Big Bang confirmed creation, Lemaître was uncomfortable and worked to steer the Vatican away from that claim.

Was there sound before light?

This is a fascinating question, and the honest answer has three separate parts. It helps to keep them separate.

1. What the text says

In Genesis, a spoken word comes first, and light follows the word. That is a claim about God and creation, not a measurement.

2. What physics says about light

In the standard picture, the very early universe was full of light already. It was a hot, glowing fog of photons, electrons, and nuclei, so dense that light could not travel far before bumping into something. Astronomers call it a plasma. The universe only became transparent, letting light travel freely, about 380,000 years after the start. That released light is the CMB. So physics does not say "sound, then light." It says a glowing plasma came first, and light escaped later.

3. What physics says about sound

Sound is a pressure wave moving through a material. The early plasma was such a material. Gravity pulled matter into slightly denser regions, but the intense radiation pushed back, like a compressed spring. The result: ripples of compression and thinning ran through the whole universe, sound waves in the cosmic plasma. They traveled at more than half the speed of light. When the universe became transparent, the waves were frozen in place, and they are printed in the CMB as tiny hot and cold spots.

So the oldest light we can see is literally a snapshot of the first "sound." The parallel is that the light carries the sound's fingerprint. The universe's first great "note" and the first free-traveling light arrive together.

That is a striking resonance with a text where a word precedes light, and it is fine to find it moving. It is not a proof that Genesis predicted the CMB, and it would be a stretch to say so. The Hebrew of Genesis is not describing pressure waves, and the CMB does not show that sound came before light. Good thinking keeps both the wonder and the caution.

Two lessons that fill in the story

The Speed of Light (which you may have just finished) explains why the CMB is the oldest thing we can see: light from far away has been traveling for a long time, and light from 13.8 billion light-years away shows the universe as it was then. The speed of light also sets how far waves in the plasma could travel by recombination.

Universe Expansion (next) shows how Hubble and others measured the stretching of space, and how redshift turns the once-hot glow of the CMB into microwaves at just 2.7 kelvin. If you understand this lesson, you already know why those measurements matter.
