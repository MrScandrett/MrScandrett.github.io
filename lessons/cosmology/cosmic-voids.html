Cosmic voids
Where did the galaxies go?

Zoom out far enough and galaxies stop looking scattered. They line up along glowing threads and sheets, the cosmic web. Between the threads are enormous, nearly empty bubbles called cosmic voids. Voids fill most of the universe's volume. Our Sun sits inside a gas cavity, while the Milky Way lies beside a cosmic void. These are different kinds of sparse space. How does gravity build the gaps between galaxies?

01 · Investigate

Gravity makes the empty places emptier

The early universe was almost perfectly smooth. Tiny ripples, a few parts in 100,000, made some places slightly thinner than others. 02 · Read the evidence

The universe is built like foam

Picture the foam on a bubble bath. The soap film collects into thin walls and edges, and the bubbles inside are mostly air. Galaxies arrange themselves the same way:

  • Clusters are the knots where walls meet. They hold hundreds to thousands of galaxies.
  • Filaments are threads of galaxies hundreds of millions of light-years long that join the clusters.
  • Walls (sheets) are thin surfaces of galaxies stretched between filaments.
  • Voids are the bubbles. They are usually 30 to 300 million light-years across and hold very few galaxies.

Voids take up most of the space in the universe, but most galaxies live in the walls and filaments. A void is an underdensity, not a perfect vacuum. Their interiors can be much less dense than the cosmic average, but the value varies with the void, its boundary, and what is measured: galaxies or total matter.

How we found them. A photo of the sky is flat. To see the foam you need each galaxy's distance, and you get it from its redshift: the farther the galaxy, the more its light is stretched by cosmic expansion. A redshift survey measures thousands of redshifts and turns a flat sky into a 3-D map. In 1978 Stephen Gregory and Laird Thompson first noticed big empty gaps in such a map. In 1981 Robert Kirshner's team found one so large that it changed how people thought about the universe: the Boötes Void.

The void ladder

#1 · Large proposed region Debated

The KBC Void, or "Local Hole"

Diameter
~2 billion ly
Distance
Our region, if supported
Found
2013

Ryan Keenan, Amy Barger, and Lennox Cowie counted galaxies in every direction and found fewer nearby than farther out, as if we live in a giant region about 20–30% below average density. Other teams say the data don't require it. It matters because living inside a void would make space around us seem to expand a little faster, which could help explain the Hubble tension.

#2 · Supervoid Debated

The Eridanus Supervoid

Diameter
~0.5–1.8 billion ly
Distance
~3 billion ly
Found
2015

Astronomers went looking for this supervoid because of the Cold Spot. In 2015 István Szapudi's team found a giant underdense region in that direction and estimated it at up to 1.8 billion light-years across. Light crossing a void loses a tiny bit of energy, the integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect. Most researchers now think this void explains only part of the Cold Spot.

#4 · The famous one Catalogued

The Boötes Void

Diameter
~330 million ly
Distance
~700 million ly
Found
1981

Robert Kirshner's team discovered it, and it is the void that made "void" a household word. Astronomer Greg Laughlin pointed out that if the Milky Way sat at its center, we wouldn't have known other galaxies existed until the 1960s. Its few galaxies line up in a thin tube through the middle, which hints that it formed when several smaller voids merged and their walls broke down.

#5 · Next door Our neighbour

The Local Void

Diameter
150+ million ly
Distance
Starts beside us
Found
1987

Brent Tully and Rick Fisher mapped it. The Milky Way lives in the Local Sheet, a thin wall of galaxies on the void's edge. The void is still expanding, and it pushes our whole Local Group away from it at about 259 km/s, nearly a million km/h. That movement on top of normal cosmic expansion is called a peculiar velocity. The void is hard to study because it lies behind the crowded, dusty center of our own galaxy.

#6 · Everyday voids Thousands known

Typical voids

Diameter
30–300 million ly
Count
Thousands catalogued
Density
~10–20% of average

Large surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey have catalogued thousands of ordinary voids. Inside them live void galaxies, which tend to be small, blue, and gas-rich, and still forming stars. With few neighbours to collide with or steal their gas, they grow slowly, like trees in an open field.

#7 · Smallest cosmic voids Measured nearby

Minivoids

Diameter
~2–15 million ly
Where
Our Local Volume
Found
2006

Voids come in every size, and big ones contain smaller voids separated by faint walls, like bubbles in foam. Close to home, where even dim dwarf galaxies are visible, Alexander Tikhonov and Igor Karachentsev mapped about 30 minivoids only a few million light-years wide. The smallest are about as wide as the gap between the Milky Way and Andromeda.

Below the cosmic scale: other kinds of emptiness

Smaller than a minivoid, "void" stops meaning a gap between galaxies. Space stays mostly empty at every scale, though:

The Local Bubble · ~1,000 lyA cavity in the Milky Way's gas, blown out by roughly 14–20 supernovae over the last 10–20 million years. The Sun is inside the Local Bubble (details in section 04).
Interstellar space · ~4 ly gapsThe nearest star is 4.2 light-years away. Shrink the Sun to a 1 mm grain of sand and the next grain is about 30 km away.
The Solar System · ~99.9…% emptyLine up all the other planets side by side and they would just about fit in the average gap between Earth and the Moon.
Atoms · a different meaning of spaceA nucleus is roughly 100,000 times smaller across than its atom. Electrons occupy quantum states around it; this is not a miniature cosmic void.

04 · Earth's voids

Your address, written in voids

Start at Earth and compare successively larger scales. These drawings are schematic: the Local Void is beside our neighbourhood, not a container around it. Distinguish a gas cavity, a wall of galaxies, and a cosmic underdensity.

Step 1 of 11

Earth

Pushed by nothing

Voids don't actually push. Gravity only pulls. Because the void has less mass than its surroundings, the walls around it pull harder from every other direction, and anything sitting on the edge drifts away from the void. That is our situation.

In 2017 Yehuda Hoffman, Daniel Pomarède, Brent Tully, and Hélène Courtois mapped the motion of thousands of galaxies and found two things acting on us together:

  • a pull toward the dense Shapley Supercluster, and
  • an apparent push from the opposite direction, an empty region they named the Dipole Repeller.

Together they set the Milky Way moving at about 630 km/s relative to the cosmic microwave background. Empty space steers the motion of galaxies as much as full space does.

05 · Why voids matter

Laboratories made of nothing

🌑

A clean test of dark energy

Voids have so little matter that dark energy dominates inside them sooner than anywhere else. Their shapes, how fast they grow, and how they bend light test models of dark energy and of gravity itself.

❄️

Cold spots in old light

Light from the Big Bang that crosses a supervoid comes out a bit colder, because the gravitational potential of an underdensity changes as the universe expands. This late-time effect is called the integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect. Stacking many voids shows the effect.

⚖️

The Hubble tension

Nearby galaxies give an expansion rate near 73 km/s per megaparsec, while the early universe predicts about 67–68. If we live inside the KBC Void, galaxies flowing outward could explain part of that gap. The question is still open.

🌱

Galaxies in isolation

Void galaxies grow up without crowds, mergers, or hot cluster gas. Comparing them with crowded galaxies shows which features come from nature and which from the neighbourhood.

👻

Ghost particles

Neutrinos move fast enough to resist clustering on small scales, changing how matter is distributed around voids. Measuring how empty voids really are helps pin down the neutrino's tiny mass.

🔭

What's next

DESI and Euclid survey galaxies across large volumes. The planned Nancy Grace Roman survey will extend this work. Comparing void catalogues can test expansion and gravity, while also revealing how results depend on survey coverage and void definitions.

    07 · For teachers

    Teaching notes & sources

    Discussion prompts

    • Voids fill most of space, yet most galaxies are not in voids. How can both be true? (Hint: think about foam.)
    • Gravity only pulls. Why do galaxies move away from voids?
    • Why are sizes for the KBC Void and the Eridanus Supervoid given as ranges or marked "debated"? What evidence would settle them?
    • If you lived in a galaxy at the center of the Boötes Void, how would your astronomy be different?

    Hands-on extension

    Blow a cup of soapy water into foam with a straw. Sketch a slice of the foam. Label nodes (clusters), edges (filaments), films (walls), and bubbles (voids), then compare the sketch with the 2dF map above.

    Common misconceptions

    • "A void is a perfect vacuum." It isn't. It holds thin gas, dark matter, and a few galaxies, at densities that vary with the region and measurement.
    • "Voids are black holes." They are the opposite: regions with less mass and weaker gravity than average.
    • "Something in the void pushes." No. The surrounding walls pull harder than the thin interior.

    Follow the evidence

    • NASA: a galaxy inside the Local Void — a void need not be empty.
    • DES: the Eridanus supervoid and Cold Spot — detection of an underdensity does not explain the full temperature anomaly.
    • NASA: measuring voids with Roman — why surveys and definitions matter.
