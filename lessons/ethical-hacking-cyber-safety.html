What is hacking?

Hacking means creatively exploring how computers work. Permission and purpose decide whether the work is ethical.

🧩 Hacker

A person who investigates systems and solves problems with computers.

🤍 White hat

Finds weaknesses with permission, reports them, and helps fix them.

🕶️ Black hat

Breaks rules to steal, damage, spy, or enter systems without permission.

Tap each situation. Decide: is it safe and permitted, or should you stop and ask?

Choose a situation to reveal the ethical-hacker decision.
📜 History Files: where "hacker" and the hats came from
1960s · MIT Tech Model Railroad ClubLong before computers were common, "hack" meant a clever, resourceful fix to a technical problem. Members who applied that mindset to MIT's early computers were the first people called "hackers" — the word originally had nothing to do with crime.
1980s · The hats show upAs computer break-ins made headlines, the community borrowed a visual shorthand from old Western movies, where heroes wore white hats and outlaws wore black hats, to separate permission-having researchers from criminals.
1988 · The Morris WormOne of the first self-spreading programs on the early internet was released as an experiment and accidentally knocked out thousands of computers overnight — an early lesson in why permission and testing matter before you run code on someone else's system.

The Secret Code Game

A Caesar cipher shifts every letter by the same amount. Repeated patterns make this code weak—but cracking it teaches careful observation.

Real history: About 2,000 years ago, Julius Caesar really did shift every letter in his military messages so that a courier who was captured couldn't read the orders. Turn the wheel below to encode and decode messages the same way he did.

📜 History Files: 1,600 years of codemakers vs. codebreakers
~50 BCE · Caesar's shift cipherJulius Caesar shifted every letter by the same fixed amount. It was fast to use, but once you know the trick, there are only 25 possible shifts to try — a computer can try them all in a blink.
~850 CE · Al-Kindi cracks substitution ciphersThe scholar Al-Kindi, working in Baghdad, wrote the first known guide to breaking ciphers by counting how often each letter appears — the same "frequency analysis" trick in the puzzle below.
1940s · Bletchley Park and the Enigma machineNazi Germany's Enigma machine scrambled messages with rotating wheels — much stronger than Caesar's shift. Codebreakers at Bletchley Park, including Alan Turing, built some of the first computers ever to crack it. The Cryptography & The Enigma lesson lets you build and break one yourself.

🧨 Puzzle 1 · Brute-Force Breaker

A real hacking technique: if there are only a few possible answers, just make a computer try every single one. This message is shifted by an unknown amount 1–25. No wheel allowed — let the computer brute-force it.

WKLV LV D VHFUHW PLVVLRQ
Click the button to watch a computer try every shift in under a second — something that would take a person many minutes by hand.

🔢 Puzzle 2 · Binary Whisper

Computers don't store letters — they store numbers as binary (1s and 0s). Here each letter is its place in the alphabet (A=1, B=2 … Z=26) written in 5-bit binary. Decode it, or let the computer do the translating instantly.

01000 00101 01100 01100 01111
Try converting the first group by hand: 01000 in binary is 8, and the 8th letter is...?

📊 Puzzle 3 · Frequency Detective

Al-Kindi's 1,200-year-old trick: in English, "E" shows up far more than any other letter. In a shifted cipher, whichever letter appears most is probably the disguised "E" — that single clue can crack the whole shift.

Click to count every letter and highlight the most common one.

Spot the Trap

Phishing messages pretend to be trustworthy so people will click, share secrets, or act too quickly. Find the warning signs.

Message A · Text message

“FREE GAME COINS!!!”

“You won! Click gamer-prize-claim.example and enter your password in the next 5 minutes or you lose it!”

Message B · Email

“School account alert”

From: helpdesk@sch00l-support.example — “Open the attachment now or your files will be erased.” (That's a zero, not the letter O — a real school would never spell its own name wrong.)

Message C · DM from a stranger

“Is this you?”

A stranger sends a shortened link — bit.ly/x7q2 — with no explanation and asks you to sign in to see.

Decide each message, then find all 9 clues. Golden rule: do not click unexpected links from strangers. Stop, verify another way, and tell a trusted adult.
📜 History Files: where "phishing" got its name
Mid-1990s · AOL and "phreaking"Early online scammers who "fished" for passwords on America Online borrowed their spelling from "phreaking" — 1970s slang for hacking phone systems — giving us "phishing" with a ph.
Today · Look-alike domainsThe oldest trick in phishing is still the most common: a web address that looks almost right at a glance (a zero instead of an "o", an extra letter) so your eyes skim right past it — exactly what Message B is testing.

📜 History Files: the first computer password ever stolen
1961 · MIT's CTSSOne of the first computers to use passwords at all was MIT's CTSS in 1961. Within a couple of years, a researcher printed out the master password file just to get more computer time than he was allowed — one of the earliest recorded password hacks.
1988 · The Morris Worm, againPart of what made the Morris Worm (Mission 1) spread so fast was that it guessed common, weak passwords like "password" and people's own usernames — the same reason Rule "avoid common passwords" still matters today.

Print & Practice Kit

Take the missions home: a paper decoder wheel and a red-flag checklist to keep by any screen.

Cut-out Decoder Wheel

Print, cut out both circles along the dashed lines, and pin the small one on top of the big one so it can spin.

Red-Flag Checklist

  1. Unexpected prize, gift, or free item
  2. Urgent countdown or scary threat
  3. Sender name is spelled a little wrong
  4. Asks for a password or secret code
  5. Link text hides where it really goes
  6. Message comes from someone you don't know

If you check even one box: stop, don't click, and tell a trusted adult.

Goal

Students explain ethical permission, decode a simple substitution cipher, recognize phishing clues, and build a stronger pretend password.

Materials

  • One device per pair or group
  • Paper and pencils for cipher work
  • Projector for whole-class discussion

60-minute flow

  1. Hacking and permission — 10 min
  2. Secret Code Game — 20 min
  3. Spot the Trap — 15 min
  4. Digital Shield and review — 15 min

Safety boundary

Keep every activity inside this page or a teacher-approved sandbox. Do not have students test real usernames, websites, networks, or passwords.

Key language

Emphasize that “hacker” describes skills; consent, authorization, and impact determine whether an action is ethical.

Password note

Length and uniqueness matter more than clever substitutions. For real accounts, use a password manager when a parent, guardian, or school supports one, and turn on multi-factor authentication when available.

Answer aloud or on paper

  1. What must an ethical hacker have before testing a system?
  2. Name two clues that a message might be phishing.
  3. Why is a long, unique password safer?
  4. What should you do when something online feels wrong?

Expected ideas

Clear permission; unusual sender, urgency, prizes, threats, hidden links, or requests for secrets; more possible guesses; stop and tell a trusted adult.
