The big idea

A lever is a rigid bar that rotates around a fulcrum.

Your push or pull is the effort. The object you want to move is the load. Distance from each force to the fulcrum controls the turning effect, called torque.

torque = force × perpendicular distance

A longer effort arm lets a smaller force balance a larger load. The trade-off is distance: your end moves farther while the load moves less. No free energy—just a useful exchange.

Class 1
E — F — L

Fulcrum in the middle. Seesaw, scissors, claw hammer.

Class 2

Load in the middle. Wheelbarrow, bottle opener.

Class 3
F — E — L

Effort in the middle. Forearm, broom, fishing rod.

Spot the pattern

The letter in the middle names the class: F in the middle is Class 1, L is Class 2, and E is Class 3.

Make · test · improve

Three classroom-safe catapult recipes

Use soft projectiles only. Each recipe is a starting point, not a final answer—measure it, change one feature, and test again.

10–15 minutes · beginner

Craft-Stick Spoon Launcher

Materials

  • 7–10 jumbo craft sticks
  • 4–6 rubber bands
  • Plastic spoon or bottle cap
  • Pom-poms or paper balls

Build

  1. Band 6 sticks into a flat stack.
  2. Band 2 sticks together at one end.
  3. Open the pair, slide the stack between them, and band the crossing point.
  4. Attach the spoon to the top arm. Test with a pom-pom.
Investigate: Move the stick stack closer to or farther from the spoon. How does changing the fulcrum position affect range?
25–35 minutes · intermediate

Cardboard A-Frame Catapult

Materials

  • Corrugated cardboard
  • Wooden dowel or pencil
  • Craft stick or cardboard arm
  • Rubber bands, tape, bottle cap

Build

  1. Cut two matching triangular side supports and a wide base.
  2. Make aligned pivot holes and pass the dowel through them.
  3. Attach the throwing arm so it rotates freely.
  4. Add a bottle-cap cup and rubber-band power. Brace the frame.
Investigate: Add an adjustable stop that changes release angle. Graph angle versus range using three trials per angle.
35–50 minutes · design challenge

Counterweight Trebuchet

Materials

  • Cardboard or craft-stick frame
  • Skewer/dowel pivot and straw bearings
  • Long throwing arm
  • Small paper-cup counterweight
  • String sling and pom-poms

Build

  1. Build and brace an A-frame with a low, wide base.
  2. Pivot the arm about one-quarter of its length from the counterweight end.
  3. Hang a light cup from the short arm.
  4. Add a simple string sling to the long arm. Start with very little weight.
Investigate: Keep arm length constant and change counterweight mass. Where do gains begin to level off?

Safety first

Launch soft, launch low, launch away.

  • Use pom-poms, mini marshmallows, or loosely crumpled paper only.
  • Never launch toward a person, animal, window, light, or device.
  • Mark one launch direction and keep everyone behind the launcher.
  • Wear eye protection when using stretched rubber bands or skewers.
  • Adults should handle hot glue, cutting tools, or sharp ends.
  • Inspect bands and frames before every trial; stop if parts crack or fray.

Engineering cycle

Turn a build into a STEAM investigation

  1. Ask: Which one change might increase accuracy or range?
  2. Predict: State what you think will happen and why.
  3. Test: Run at least three trials with the same setup.
  4. Measure: Record distance from the launch line to first landing.
  5. Improve: Change one variable and repeat.

From the latest lesson · 7,000 years of leverage

From irrigation beams to precision machines

The lever may be humanity's oldest machine. A branch beneath a stone already contains the complete idea: a rigid arm turns around a pivot, trading motion for force.

By roughly 3000 BCE, Egyptian and Mesopotamian farmers were using the shaduf, a counterweighted Class 1 lever, to raise water into irrigation channels. Around 250 BCE, Archimedes gave this practical knowledge a mathematical foundation with the law of the lever.

The law survives every redesign

Feffortdeffort = Floaddload

A piano key, claw hammer, surgical forceps, seesaw, and trebuchet look unrelated, but all obey the same torque balance.
