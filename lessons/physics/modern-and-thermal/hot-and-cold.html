Hot & Cold

Kinetic Theory Absolute Zero Kelvin & Celsius Thermal Equilibrium Conduction

At the microscopic scale, temperature is not an invisible fluid or a separate substance—it is the average translational kinetic energy of jiggling atoms and molecules. Heat is the spontaneous transfer of that disordered kinetic energy from hotter objects to colder ones until they reach thermal equilibrium.

1. What Is Temperature? Atomic Motion & Kinetic Theory

For centuries, philosophers and early naturalists believed heat was a weightless, invisible fluid called caloric that flowed into substances when warmed. In 1798, Benjamin Thompson (Count Rumford) observed that boring brass cannons generated limitless heat through friction alone, demonstrating that heat is not conserved like a fluid, but is mechanical energy in microscopic motion.

Today, kinetic theory defines temperature (\(T\)) directly in terms of microscopic motion. For an ideal gas of particles of mass \(m\), the average translational kinetic energy \(\langle E_k \rangle\) per particle is directly proportional to absolute temperature:

\(\langle E_k \rangle = \frac{1}{2} m \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{3}{2} k_B T\)

where \(k_B \approx 1.3806 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/K}\) is the Boltzmann constant.

3. Temperature Scales & The Zeroth Law

Three common temperature scales exist today, each founded on distinct historical reference points:

  • Fahrenheit (°F): Established in 1724 by Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit using an ice-water-ammonium chloride brine as \(0^\circ\text{F}\) and human body temperature calibrated near \(96^\circ\text{F}\) (later adjusted so water freezes at \(32^\circ\text{F}\) and boils at \(212^\circ\text{F}\)).
  • Celsius (°C): Devised in 1742 by Anders Celsius based on pure water at standard atmospheric pressure: freezing at \(0^\circ\text{C}\) and boiling at \(100^\circ\text{C}\).
  • Kelvin (K): The SI base unit of thermodynamic temperature proposed by Lord Kelvin in 1848. Unlike Celsius or Fahrenheit, Kelvin has no negative values—it is an absolute scale whose zero point is the complete absence of classical kinetic energy.
Conversion Formulas:
\(T_{\text{K}} = T_{\text{C}} + 273.15\)    \(T_{\text{F}} = \frac{9}{5} T_{\text{C}} + 32\)    \(T_{\text{C}} = \frac{5}{9}(T_{\text{F}} - 32)\)

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

Formulated formally by Ralph H. Fowler in 1935, the Zeroth Law establishes the transitive property of thermal equilibrium:

"If two thermodynamic systems A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system C, then system A and system B are in thermal equilibrium with each other."

This fundamental law is why thermometers work: if a thermometer bulb reaches equilibrium with object A and reads \(22^\circ\text{C}\), and later reaches equilibrium with object B and reads \(22^\circ\text{C}\), we know without placing A and B into contact that no net heat would flow between them.

4. From Absolute Zero to Stellar Cores

The universe spans an astounding dynamic range of temperatures, from nanokelvin laboratory extremes to multimillion-degree stellar plasmas:

🧊
Bose-Einstein Condensate (~100 nK) Using laser cooling and magnetic trapping, physicists cool rubidium atoms to billionths of a degree above absolute zero, causing wavepackets to merge into a single macroscopic quantum wave.
🌌
Cosmic Microwave Background (2.725 K) The thermal remnant glow of the Big Bang permeates all of deep space, chilling intergalactic matter to \(-270.4^\circ\text{C}\), just 2.7 degrees above absolute zero.
🧲
Superconducting Magnets (4.2 K) Liquid helium cools niobium-titanium coils in MRI scanners and CERN's Large Hadron Collider, dropping electrical resistance to zero and enabling massive electric currents without heat loss.
☀️
Solar Core & Fusion Tokamaks (15M – 150M K) At 15 million Kelvin inside the Sun, protons possess sufficient kinetic energy to overcome Coulomb repulsion and fuse into helium. Experimental fusion reactors (ITER) heat plasma to 150 million Kelvin.

5. Worked Example: Thermal Equilibrium Calculation

When two substances at different temperatures are placed in an insulated container (a calorimeter), energy conservation dictates that heat lost by the hotter substance equals heat gained by the colder substance: \(Q_{\text{lost}} = Q_{\text{gained}}\).

\(m_1 c_1 (T_1 - T_f) = m_2 c_2 (T_f - T_2)\)

Sample Problem: A student pours \(200\text{ g}\) of hot water at \(80^\circ\text{C}\) into an insulated cup containing \(300\text{ g}\) of cold water at \(20^\circ\text{C}\). Both have specific heat capacity \(c = 4.184\text{ J/(g}\cdot^\circ\text{C)}\). Find the final equilibrium temperature \(T_f\).

Step 1: Set up the heat transfer equation.
Since both fluids are water, the specific heat capacity \(c\) cancels from both sides:
\(m_1 (T_1 - T_f) = m_2 (T_f - T_2)\)

Step 2: Expand and solve for \(T_f\).
\(m_1 T_1 - m_1 T_f = m_2 T_f - m_2 T_2\)
\(m_1 T_1 + m_2 T_2 = (m_1 + m_2) T_f\)
\(T_f = \frac{m_1 T_1 + m_2 T_2}{m_1 + m_2}\)

Step 3: Substitute numerical values.
\(T_f = \frac{(200\text{ g})(80^\circ\text{C}) + (300\text{ g})(20^\circ\text{C})}{200\text{ g} + 300\text{ g}} = \frac{16,000 + 6,000}{500} = \frac{22,000}{500} = 44.0^\circ\text{C}\).

Check Intuition: Because there is more cold water (\(300\text{ g}\)) than hot water (\(200\text{ g}\)), the final equilibrium temperature of \(44^\circ\text{C}\) lies closer to \(20^\circ\text{C}\) than to \(80^\circ\text{C}\), exactly matching physical conservation of energy.

6. Practice Problems

Easy1. What is the value of Absolute Zero on the Celsius scale?

Hint: \(0\text{ K} = T_{\text{C}} + 273.15 \implies T_{\text{C}} = -273.15^\circ\text{C}\).

Medium2. If the absolute temperature (\(T\)) of an ideal gas is quadrupled (\(4\times\)), what happens to the root-mean-square speed (\(v_{\text{rms}}\)) of its molecules?

Hint: \(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 k_B T}{m}}\). Taking the square root of 4 gives 2.

Medium3. Room temperature is commonly taken as \(25^\circ\text{C}\). Convert this to Kelvin (to the nearest tenth).

Hint: Add 273.15 to 25.

Hard4. Two identical blocks of aluminum (\(m_1 = m_2\)) at \(100^\circ\text{C}\) and \(20^\circ\text{C}\) are placed in thermal contact inside an insulated chamber. What is the final equilibrium temperature in °C?

Hint: Since mass and material are identical, the final temperature is the simple arithmetic mean: \((100 + 20) / 2 = 60^\circ\text{C}\).
