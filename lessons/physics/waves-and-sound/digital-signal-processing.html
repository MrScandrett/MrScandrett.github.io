Digital Signal Processing

Digital signal processing (DSP) is what happens to a sound once it becomes numbers. Your phone measures the microphone's wave tens of thousands of times a second, rounds each measurement and stores it. Then arithmetic does the editing: removing hum, shaping the tone, shrinking the file. Wi-Fi, MRI scanners and voice assistants run on the same ideas.

Big question: how can a list of numbers hold a sound, and once a sound is numbers, how do you clean up a bad recording?

Start with the maths: the Fourier Series & Transform lesson shows why every sound is a sum of sine waves and how the FFT finds them. This lesson takes that idea and asks what engineers do with it.

By the end you can…

  1. Read a spectrum to locate the parts of a sound: notes, hum and hiss.
  2. Explain how the sample rate limits which frequencies a recording can hold, and recognise aliasing.
  3. Explain how bit depth sets the noise level of a recording.
  4. Choose filters to repair a recording and predict what each one will do to the sound.

Going digital, part 1: how often? (sample rate)

An analog-to-digital converter can't store a smooth wave. It measures the wave's height at regular instants, called samples, and keeps only those numbers. The dots are the samples. The orange line is the slowest wave that passes through all of them, which is what the computer "thinks" it recorded.

Try:

Camera view1 frame per sample
Predict firstKeep the sample rate at 20. At what signal frequency will the orange line first stop matching the grey wave?

Just above 10 Hz, which is half the sample rate. That cutoff is the Nyquist frequency. You need more than two samples per cycle to capture a wave. Anything faster gets "folded" back and shows up as a lower, fake frequency, called an alias. Film cameras take about 24 frames per second, which is why wagon wheels and helicopter blades in movies can seem to spin backward.

ExplainOnce a sound has been aliased, why can't software fix it afterward?

Set the signal to 17 Hz, then to 3 Hz. The dots land in exactly the same places, so the stored numbers are identical. Nothing in the file says which wave they came from. That's why recorders filter out everything above the Nyquist frequency before sampling, using an "anti-aliasing filter". It's much easier to prevent aliasing than to undo it.

Why CDs use 44,100 samples per second: human hearing tops out near 20,000 Hz. Twice that is 40,000, plus a little extra room for the anti-aliasing filter, which gives 44.1 kHz.

3

Going digital, part 2: how precisely? (bit depth)

Each sample is stored as a whole number made of a fixed number of bits. With b bits there are 2b possible levels, so every measurement is rounded to the nearest level. This rounding is called quantization. The rounding errors (orange, bottom strip) add a rough, jittery wobble to the signal. You hear it as hiss, called quantization noise.

Predict firstPlay the test sound and slide from 16 bits down to 3. Will the pitch change?

No. Pitch depends on when the samples happen, and the sample rate hasn't changed. Rounding only changes how precisely each sample is stored. Fewer bits mean bigger rounding errors, and you hear them as hiss and a crunchy, buzzy edge. The signal-to-noise readout shows how far that noise sits below the music, in dB: each bit you add pushes the noise about 6 dB quieter.

CompareSample rate and bit depth are both "quality" settings. Which one fixes which problem?

The sample rate sets the highest frequency you can record, so too low a rate causes aliasing and lost treble. The bit depth sets the noise floor, so too few bits cause hiss and crunch. They're independent. An 8-bit game console could play high notes perfectly well, but it always sounded gritty.

Why 16 bits for CDs? 65,536 levels put the noise about 96 dB below the loudest sound, roughly the gap between a silent room and a rock concert. Studios record at 24 bits so there's room to spare while they edit.

4

Filters, part 1: the equalizer

Once a sound is numbers, a digital filter can turn individual frequencies up or down. It does this with nothing more than multiplying and adding samples. An equalizer (EQ) is a row of such filters, one per band, each set in decibels. The curve shows the total effect: above the line is louder, below is quieter. The small coloured ticks mark your test sound's harmonics.

Presets:
EQ frequency response±12 dB · log frequency
Predict firstSet the test sound to 220 Hz and choose "Old telephone". Which harmonics get quieter, and why does the sound get thinner?

The telephone curve cuts everything below about 300 Hz. Your 220 Hz fundamental lands in that dip, so it drops a lot, while the harmonics near 1 kHz get boosted. You still hear the same pitch, because your brain works out the missing fundamental from the spacing of the harmonics. But the sound loses its body. Real phone lines pass only about 300–3,400 Hz, which is why voices sound "tinny".

5

Filters, part 2: rescue a ruined recording

Someone recorded a melody next to a buzzing power adapter in a room with a noisy fan. The spectrum shows three things mixed together: the melody's harmonics (cyan), electrical hum at 60 Hz and its multiples (red), and hiss spread across the high frequencies (orange). An EQ can't remove these cleanly, so this step uses three new filter types.

High-pass

Lets frequencies above the cutoff pass. Cuts everything below it.

Low-pass

Lets frequencies below the cutoff pass. Cuts everything above it.

Notch

Deletes one narrow frequency and leaves the rest almost untouched.

Hint 1I turned on the notch filter, but the hum score barely moved. Why?

Look at the red spikes. Hum isn't a single frequency: it's 60 Hz plus harmonics at 120 and 180 Hz. The notch removes only the first one. Which filter type can remove everything below a chosen frequency?

Hint 2Where exactly should the cutoffs go?

Put each cutoff in a gap between what you want to keep and what you want to remove. The lowest melody spike is at 392 Hz and the highest hum spike is at 180 Hz, so the high-pass belongs between them. The melody stops near 2 kHz and the hiss starts above it. Filters don't cut like a cliff, they slope, so leave some space either side.

ThinkWhy can't filtering ever remove the hiss completely?

Real hiss is spread across all frequencies, including the ones the melody uses. A filter can't separate two things that share a frequency. Removing that last bit takes smarter DSP, such as the noise reduction in phone calls, which estimates the noise and subtracts it.

Check for understanding

Six quick questions

Pick an answer to see why it's right or wrong.

The converter in your pocket

Every phone has an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) behind the microphone. It measures a voltage 48,000 times a second and turns each measurement into a 16- or 24-bit number. A digital-to-analog converter (DAC) does the reverse for your headphones.

Nyquist (1928) and Shannon (1949)

Harry Nyquist and Claude Shannon showed that sampling at more than twice a signal's highest frequency loses nothing: the original wave can be rebuilt exactly. Every digital audio, video and radio system is designed around that limit.

A filter is a little recipe

Each filter in this lesson is a "biquad". It works out every new output sample from the last two inputs and the last two outputs, each multiplied by a fixed number. Changing those five numbers turns the same code into a high-pass, low-pass, notch or EQ band.

Where DSP hides in your day

MP3 and streaming audio discard sounds you can't hear. Noise-cancelling headphones produce an "anti-wave". Phones filter out background noise on calls, Wi-Fi untangles radio echoes, and MRI scanners build pictures from frequencies.
