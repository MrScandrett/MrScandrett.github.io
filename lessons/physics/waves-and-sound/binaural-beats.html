Binaural Beats: Two Ears, Two Tones

Beat frequency Interference Hearing & perception Evaluating claims

Play a slightly different tone into each ear and many listeners hear a slow wobble that is in neither headphone. This lesson compares that effect with ordinary beats you can hear from a speaker, then asks a harder question: what would it take to show that a sound changes the brain?

Before You Start: Beats You Already Know

When two notes are close in frequency, their waves drift in and out of step. In step, the crests add and the sound gets louder; out of step, they cancel and it gets quieter. That loud–soft pulse is an acoustic beat, and musicians use it to tune: as two strings get closer to the same pitch, the beats slow down and stop.

You can watch this happen in the two-wave interference lab in Physics of Music. Binaural beats ask what happens when the two tones never meet in the air at all, because each one goes to a different ear.

Binaural Beating: Sound, Perception, and Evidence

Learning goal: Calculate a beat rate, explain why stereo headphones matter, and distinguish an observation from a claim about the brain.

Binaural means “with two ears.” When a steady 220 Hz tone reaches one ear and a steady 224 Hz tone reaches the other, some listeners perceive a gentle fluctuation at about 4 beats per second. The auditory system compares the signals. Neither headphone channel contains a separate 4 Hz sound wave, and you are not hearing a 4 Hz musical pitch. People vary in how clearly they notice the effect.

Beat rate = |fright − fleft|
Example: |224 − 220| = 4 Hz. This predicts the fluctuation rate, not a brain state.

Compare three listening conditions

  • Binaural: Each ear receives one steady tone. The perceived fluctuation depends on auditory processing across the ears. Stereo separation matters; mono audio settings can mix the channels and change the experiment.
  • Acoustic beats: Both tones are mixed into each ear. Their sound waves add to form a changing amplitude envelope before auditory processing. Speakers can demonstrate this ordinary interference effect.
  • Same-tone control: Both ears receive 220 Hz. The difference is zero, so this pair predicts no beating. A control helps us compare explanations.

Classroom investigation

  1. Predict: With a 220 Hz base, what will the right tone and beat rate be for differences of 0, 4, and 8 Hz?
  2. Observe: Keep the base and volume fixed. Compare all three modes, using short, optional samples. Record the mode, frequencies, predicted rate, and what you actually notice. “No clear beat” is a valid observation.
  3. Explain: Why does mixing both tones change the mechanism? If you remove one earcup in binaural mode, that ear comparison is lost; the remaining channel is a steady tone.
  4. Evaluate: Could expectation, music, or a quiet break explain someone feeling calmer? A listening report alone cannot establish brainwave synchronization. That requires appropriate EEG measurements and controlled research.
Check the predictions

The right tones are 220, 224, and 228 Hz; predicted beat rates are 0, 4, and 8 Hz. In same-tone control mode the two channels always match, regardless of the difference slider.

Age adaptations: Younger students can compare “same” and “different” and count slow pulses with teacher guidance. Older students can calculate differences, identify variables and controls, and explain why a subjective report is different from an EEG result. Students can complete the entire investigation visually without headphones or hearing the effect.

Historical case study: the Monroe Institute

Robert Monroe and the Monroe Institute helped popularize audio programs using binaural beats, including the technology called Hemi-Sync. The Institute describes its history in terms of “hemispheric synchronization” and expanded awareness. These are the organization’s interpretations and promotional claims; its own account is a primary source for what it says, not independent proof of effectiveness.

Hearing a binaural beat is an established perceptual phenomenon. Claims that a chosen beat reliably sets the whole brain to a matching rhythm, improves learning, or produces a particular mental state require separate evidence. A 2023 systematic review of 14 EEG studies found inconsistent results on brainwave entrainment. A beat rate that overlaps an EEG frequency band does not by itself establish a corresponding brain state.

Christian classroom connection: We can study hearing as part of God’s creation while practicing honesty about evidence and discernment about claims. This activity studies sound and perception. It includes no spiritual exercises, guided meditation, or attempts to induce altered states, and makes no claims about spiritual experiences or medical treatment.

Teacher references and source evaluation
  • Ingendoh, Posny & Heine (2023), PLOS ONE: systematic review of binaural beats and brain oscillations — scientific background and limits of the evidence.
  • Monroe Institute: its account of its sound technology — teacher background for evaluating an organization’s own claims. The external site includes spiritual and altered-awareness material beyond this classroom activity; preview before sharing with students.

Discussion prompt: Which source tells us what an organization claims? Which examines evidence across studies? What further evidence would a claim about improved learning need?

Big Idea

Beat rate = |fright − fleft|. With speakers, the beat is in the air. With one tone per ear, any beat you hear is made by your auditory system comparing the two ears.
