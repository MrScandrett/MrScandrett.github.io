Coulomb's Law

In 1785, Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured the invisible push and pull between tiny charged spheres and uncovered the mathematical law governing electric charge. Like gravity, it obeys an inverse-square law—yet it can both attract and repel, and at atomic scales it is trillions of trillions of times stronger.

1. The Law & Vector Superposition

The electrostatic force between two point charges depends directly on the product of their charges and inversely on the square of the center-to-center distance between them:

F = k · q₁q₂ / r²
F = electric force (Newtons, N)
k = Coulomb's constant ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
q₁, q₂ = charges (Coulombs, C)
r = center-to-center distance (meters, m)
Opposite charges attract (+ and −) One positive and one negative charge pull inward along the line joining their centers. More electrons on one object and fewer on the other create a mutual inward pull.
Like charges repel (+/+ or −/−) Two positive or two negative charges push outward away from each other along the line joining them. Neither object can escape the pair's equal-and-opposite push.
Vector direction & Newton's Third Law: Notice the formula multiplies q₁ × q₂ together. That means a +1 μC charge and a +8 μC charge exert the exact same force magnitude on each other—just in opposite directions! When three or more charges are present, the net force on any charge is the vector sum (superposition) of every pairwise push and pull.

Worked Example: Calculating Electrostatic Force

Two small spheres carry q₁ = +2 μC (2 × 10⁻⁶ C) and q₂ = −3 μC (−3 × 10⁻⁶ C) and sit r = 0.30 m apart in air.

  1. Convert microcoulombs: 1 μC = 10⁻⁶ C, so |q₁| = 2 × 10⁻⁶ C and |q₂| = 3 × 10⁻⁶ C.
  2. Substitute into Coulomb's law (using k ≈ 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²): F = (9.0 × 10⁹) × (2 × 10⁻⁶) × (3 × 10⁻⁶) / (0.30)².
  3. Compute numerator and denominator: 0.054 / 0.09 = 0.60 N.
  4. Determine direction from signs: opposite signs mean 0.60 N attractive on both charges.

4. How Coulomb Proved It: The 1785 Torsion Balance

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1736–1806) was a French physicist and military engineer. To measure electrostatic forces smaller than the weight of a grain of sand, he built a torsion balance: a needle-thin silver wire inside a draft-proof glass cylinder that twisted smoothly in proportion to the repulsive force between two small pith balls.

By twisting the top micrometer dial to squeeze the balls to half their separation, Coulomb measured that the wire twist—and therefore the force—jumped fourfold. He also touched a charged sphere to an identical uncharged sphere, splitting the charge in half (q → q/2) and proving force is proportional to each charge.

5. Mediums, Conductors & Electric vs. Gravity Scale

Coulomb's constant k describes charges in a vacuum (and almost identically in dry air). When charges sit inside an insulating material or near a metal surface, the surrounding space actively responds:

Air & Vacuum (εᵣ ≈ 1) Almost nothing gets in the way between the charges, so they feel the full Coulomb force. Only above ~3 million V/m does dry air undergo dielectric breakdown into a spark.
Dielectric Insulators (εᵣ > 1) In an insulator like glass (εᵣ ≈ 5) or pure water (εᵣ ≈ 80), molecules align to partially oppose the electric field, reducing the net force between two charges by a factor of εᵣ.
Conductors & Shielding Inside a conductor, free electrons rearrange almost instantly via electrostatic induction, reshaping external fields and canceling the electric field completely inside a Faraday cage.

6. Real-World Applications

⚡
Lightning & Breakdown Charge separation in storm clouds creates electric fields strong enough to ionize air and trigger dielectric breakdown—a multi-kilometer spark.
🖨️
Laser Printers & Copiers A laser draws a charge pattern onto a rotating drum; charged toner dust is pulled onto the exact pixels by Coulomb attraction and fused onto paper.
⚛️
Atomic & Chemical Bonding Coulomb attraction between protons and electrons holds atoms together, and electrostatic forces between ions and dipoles drive every chemical bond.
🩺
Electrostatic Air Filters HEPA and hospital filters use permanently charged fibers to pull microscopic smoke, pollen, and aerosols out of moving air without blocking airflow.
🎨
Electrostatic Spray Painting Car frames are grounded while paint droplets leave the nozzle negatively charged. Droplets repel each other into an even mist and wrap around onto the metal.
📱
Touchscreens & ESD Safety Capacitive screens sense tiny shifts in your finger's electric field, while wrist straps prevent sudden electrostatic discharge (ESD) from zapping microchips.

Quick glossary

Coulomb (C)

The SI unit of electric charge, equal to the combined charge of about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ protons or electrons. Everyday static charges are usually microcoulombs (1 μC = 10⁻⁶ C).

Coulomb's constant (k)

The proportionality constant in Coulomb's law: k ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² in vacuum or air.

Electric field (E)

The electric force per unit positive test charge at a point in space (E = F / q), pointing away from positive charges and toward negative charges.

Equipotential

A line or surface where electric potential (voltage) has the same value everywhere, always crossing electric field lines at right angles.

Dielectric constant (εᵣ)

A dimensionless factor measuring how strongly an insulating material polarizes and reduces the electric force between charges inside it.

Superposition

The rule that the net electric force (or field) on a charge equals the vector sum of the individual forces from every other charge.
