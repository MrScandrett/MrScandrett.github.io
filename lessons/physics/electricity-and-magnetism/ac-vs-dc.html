AC vs. DC Electricity

Both move electric charge and transfer energy. The key difference is direction: direct current keeps one polarity, while alternating current repeatedly reverses. That simple distinction shapes batteries, outlets, motors, electronics, and the power grid.

Start with the current

Electric current is the rate at which charge passes a point, measured in amperes (A). “AC” and “DC” describe how that current—and usually the voltage driving it—changes with time.

⎓

Direct current (DC)

Charge has one net direction. The voltage keeps the same polarity, though its value does not have to be perfectly constant.

  • Batteries and solar cells produce DC
  • Phones, computers, LEDs, and chips operate internally on DC
  • Shown as a flat line when the voltage is steady
∿

Alternating current (AC)

Charge reverses direction periodically. Utility AC is close to a sine wave and completes 60 cycles each second in North America.

  • Wall outlets and conventional power grids supply AC
  • Transformers easily change AC voltage
  • Described by frequency, amplitude, phase, and waveform
Electron motion is not energy delivery. In an AC wire, individual electrons mostly drift back and forth over tiny distances. Energy still travels from generator to load through the circuit’s electromagnetic field, while engineers still mark conventional current arrows from + to −.

How a generator creates one AC cycle

Spinning a wire coil inside a magnetic field induces a smooth sine wave via electromagnetic induction. Positive and negative voltage indicate opposite polarities—not “good” and “bad” electricity.

f = 1 ÷ T

Frequency f is measured in hertz. Period T is the time for one full cycle in seconds.

Vpeak = Vrms × √2

For a sine wave only, peak voltage is about 1.414 times the RMS voltage.

Vrms = Vpeak ÷ √2

RMS is the DC-equivalent heating value. A 120 V RMS sine wave peaks near 170 V.

Worked example: For 60 Hz AC, T = 1 ÷ 60 = 0.0167 s = 16.7 ms. The voltage completes a whole positive-and-negative cycle every 16.7 milliseconds.

Why high voltage reduces wire loss

For the same delivered power, raising transmission voltage lowers current: I = P ÷ V. Since resistive heating in wire resistance (Ω) is Ploss = I²R, reducing current sharply reduces wasted heat. Test real grid scenarios below.

Transformer ratio & magnetic flux lab

By Faraday’s law, changing magnetic flux in the iron core induces voltage across the secondary coil. Try switching to steady DC—watch what happens to the output!

Ideal model: input power equals output power. Real transformers lose a small amount of energy as heat, sound, stray magnetic flux, and eddy currents. Because a transformer requires changing magnetic flux, steady DC cannot be transformed directly.

Tesla, Edison, and the modern grid

In the late 1800s, Edison’s systems distributed low-voltage DC. Westinghouse deployed practical AC systems, while Nikola Tesla’s polyphase patents and induction motor helped make AC useful for industry. The contest involved many engineers, inventors, financiers, patents, and public-safety campaigns—not just two lone rivals.

AC became the foundation of public power systems because transformers could efficiently raise voltage for transmission and lower it near users. That history does not mean DC “lost forever”: batteries, electronics, solar arrays, EVs, data centers, and HVDC transmission make DC essential today.

1882 · NYCPearl Street DC

Edison lights lower Manhattan at 110 V DC, limited to ~1 mile by wire heating.

1886 · Mass.Stanley Transformer

William Stanley & Westinghouse demonstrate practical AC voltage step-up/step-down.

1888 · PatentsTesla Polyphase AC

Nikola Tesla’s induction motor and multi-phase patents unlock industrial AC power.

1895 · NiagaraAdams Hydro Plant

Westinghouse AC alternators send hydroelectric power 26 miles to Buffalo at 11 kV.

Today · HybridHVAC + HVDC

AC grids carry bulk power while semiconductors convert DC everywhere for chips, solar, EVs, and undersea HVDC.

Quick glossary

Amplitude

The maximum displacement of a waveform from its center or zero level; for voltage, this is its peak value.

Frequency

The number of complete cycles each second, measured in hertz (Hz).

Period

The time for one complete cycle. Period and frequency are reciprocals: T = 1/f.

RMS

Root mean square: a way to express AC by the DC voltage that would produce the same heating in a resistor.

Rectifier

A circuit, usually using diodes, that changes alternating input into one-direction output.

Ripple

The small remaining up-and-down voltage variation on a smoothed DC power supply between charging peaks.

Inverter

Power electronics that synthesize AC from a DC source.

Transformer

Coupled coils that use changing magnetic flux to transfer energy and change AC voltage/current levels.

Polarity

The sign or orientation of voltage between two points. DC holds it; AC periodically reverses it.

Phase

Where a repeating wave is in its 360° cycle at a given moment, measured in degrees or radians.
