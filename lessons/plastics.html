Plastics: Polymer Chemistry & Materials

Monomers & Polymers Thermoplastics vs. Thermosets Resin Codes Recycling Chemistry

Plastics are polymers — long, chainlike molecules built by linking together thousands of small repeating units called monomers. A single polyethylene chain might join 10,000 ethylene molecules end to end, yet the resulting material can be a flimsy grocery bag or a rigid pipe fitting, depending on how those chains are built and connected. This lab explores the chemistry that turns simple monomers into the plastics surrounding almost everything we touch.

From Monomers to Polymers

A monomer is a small molecule that can bond to other identical (or similar) molecules to form a long chain. A polymer is the resulting macromolecule — poly ("many") + mer ("part"). The number of repeat units in a chain is called the degree of polymerization (n), and it typically ranges from a few hundred to a few hundred thousand.

Addition Polymerization

Many common plastics form by addition polymerization: monomers containing a carbon–carbon double bond (an alkene) open that bond and link directly to their neighbors. No atoms are lost in the process.

n CH₂=CH₂ → [–CH₂–CH₂–]n
n = degree of polymerization
CH₂=CH₂ = ethylene monomer
[–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ = polyethylene chain

Condensation Polymerization

Other plastics form by condensation polymerization: monomers with two different reactive end groups link together while releasing a small byproduct molecule, usually water. Nylon and polyester (PET) form this way, joining monomers one small molecule at a time.

Key rule: The backbone of almost every plastic is a chain of carbon atoms held together by strong covalent bonds. That backbone is what makes plastics durable — and what makes most of them resistant to breaking back down in the environment.

Thermoplastics vs. Thermosets

Once a polymer chain exists, what happens between separate chains determines whether the material can be melted and reshaped — or not.

Thermoplastics

In a thermoplastic, individual chains are held to each other only by weak intermolecular forces and physical tangling — there are no permanent bonds between chains. Heat the material and those weak forces loosen, letting chains slide past each other: the plastic softens and flows. Cool it, and it hardens again in its new shape. This is why thermoplastics like PET, HDPE, PP, and PS can be melted down and remolded — the basis of most mechanical recycling.

Thermosets

In a thermoset, chains are linked to one another by permanent covalent bonds called crosslinks, forming a single giant three-dimensional network rather than separate strands. Heating a thermoset doesn't loosen it — there's nothing to slide past. Push the temperature high enough and the covalent bonds themselves break, charring and decomposing the material instead of melting it. Once cured, a thermoset can never be reshaped by heat again.

History: Bakelite, invented by Leo Baekeland in 1907, was the first fully synthetic plastic — a phenol-formaldehyde thermoset so heat-resistant it was used for early electrical insulators, telephones, and radio cabinets.

The Seven Resin Identification Codes

Click a code to reveal what it's used for and how recyclable it is.

♳ 1 PET Polyethylene Terephthalate
Water & soda bottles, food jars. The most widely recycled plastic — often reprocessed into new bottles or polyester fiber.
♴ 2 HDPE High-Density Polyethylene
Milk jugs, detergent bottles, pipe. Stiff, chemical-resistant, and one of the easiest plastics to recycle curbside.
♵ 3 PVC Polyvinyl Chloride
Pipes, siding, medical tubing. Contains chlorine and additives that make it difficult to recycle — rarely accepted curbside.
♶ 4 LDPE Low-Density Polyethylene
Plastic bags, squeeze bottles, shrink wrap. Flexible chains with more branching; usually needs special drop-off recycling.
♷ 5 PP Polypropylene
Yogurt cups, bottle caps, straws. Heat-resistant and durable; recycling acceptance is growing but still inconsistent.
♸ 6 PS Polystyrene
Foam cups, packing peanuts, disposable cutlery. Brittle and lightweight — breaks into fragments easily and is rarely recycled.
♹ 7 Other Polycarbonate, bioplastics, mixed layers
A catch-all category for everything else, including some bioplastics like PLA. Mixed materials make sorting and recycling hard.

Real-World Applications

📦
Packaging & Food Safety PET and HDPE containers are lightweight, shatterproof, and form a barrier that keeps food and medicine safe and fresh in transit.
🏥
Medical Devices Sterile, single-use syringes, IV bags, and tubing rely on plastics that can be mass-produced cheaply and never reused between patients.
🧵
Synthetic Textiles Polyester and nylon are polymer chains drawn into fibers — used in everything from athletic wear to parachutes to carpet.
🚗
Lightweight Engineering Replacing metal parts with engineered plastics and composites cuts vehicle and aircraft weight, improving fuel efficiency.

The Environmental Chemistry of Plastic

The same strong carbon-carbon backbone that makes plastic durable also makes it slow to break down. Most microbes have no enzymes evolved to digest a synthetic polymer chain, so plastic in the environment persists for decades to centuries rather than decomposing like a leaf or a scrap of wood.

Microplastics

Instead of biodegrading, sunlight (UV) and mechanical stress gradually fragment plastic into smaller and smaller pieces. Fragments under 5 mm are called microplastics, and they've been found throughout ocean water, soil, drinking water, and even inside living organisms — an area of active ongoing research.

Recycling Chemistry

Mechanical recycling melts and reforms thermoplastics, but each melt cycle shortens some chains through thermal degradation, gradually lowering material quality. Chemical recycling instead breaks the polymer all the way back down to its original monomers, which can then be repolymerized into plastic as good as new — but it takes more energy and infrastructure than most recycling programs currently have.

Bioplastics

Some newer plastics, like PLA (polylactic acid) made from corn starch, are designed to break down faster than petroleum-based plastics — but usually only under the high heat and controlled conditions of an industrial composting facility, not in a backyard bin or the ocean.

Big picture: The resin code on a plastic item doesn't guarantee it will be recycled — it only identifies the polymer. What actually gets recycled depends on local facilities, market demand for that material, and how clean and sorted the plastic is.

Practice Problems

Easy1. Thermoset plastics can be melted down and reshaped multiple times, just like thermoplastics. True or False?

Hint: Thermosets are held together by permanent crosslinks. Heating them breaks bonds and chars the material instead of melting it.

Easy2. What is the name for the small repeating molecule that links together to build a polymer chain?

Hint: "Poly" means many, and "mer" means part — a polymer is many of these small parts joined together.

Medium3. Addition polymerization joins monomers by opening a carbon-carbon double bond, releasing no byproduct. What is a defining feature of condensation polymerization instead?

Hint: Nylon and polyester form when two reactive end groups combine and squeeze out a small molecule, usually water.

Medium4. Which resin identification code marks HDPE — the widely-recycled plastic used for milk jugs and detergent bottles?

Hint: Check the resin code cards above — HDPE is the second code.

Challenge5. Polyethylene's repeat unit, –CH₂–CH₂–, has a formula mass of about 28 g/mol. If a single polymer chain contains 5,000 repeat units, what is its approximate molar mass in g/mol?

Hint: molar mass ≈ (repeat unit mass) × (number of repeat units) = 28 × 5,000
