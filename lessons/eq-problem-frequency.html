High-pass filter

Below the cutoff is reduced; above it passes. A steeper slope removes lows more quickly.

Bell and Q

High Q is narrow and surgical. Low Q is wide and changes more neighboring frequencies.

Shelves

A high shelf changes everything above its corner; a low shelf changes everything below it.

Dynamic threshold

The band waits until its detector crosses the threshold, then applies its cut or boost range.

Phase and timing

Conventional filters shift some frequencies in time. Linear-phase designs align them but add latency.

2. Seven equalizers: what changes and why

All EQs change frequency balance, but their controls, timing, phase behavior, and best uses differ. “More advanced” does not automatically mean “better.”

Core · fixed bands

Graphic EQ

Controls: gain for a row of fixed frequency bands.

The name comes from the response curve drawn by its physical vertical faders. It is fast and practical for live-sound feedback control and broad room/system tuning, but less precise than a parametric EQ.

Core · fully adjustable

Parametric EQ

Controls: center frequency, gain, and Q/bandwidth.

The most flexible general-purpose design. Use it to isolate mud, resonance, harshness, or other specific tonal problems.

Core · simplified

Semi-Parametric EQ

Controls: frequency and gain; Q is fixed or offers only a few choices.

Quicker and less expensive to operate, but it cannot independently choose exactly how wide the affected range will be.

Specialized · broad tone

Shelving EQ

Controls: corner frequency, gain, and high/low shelf.

Raises or lowers everything above or below the corner. Excellent for broad bass weight or treble brightness—not narrow resonances.

Advanced · level-dependent

Dynamic EQ

Controls: frequency, gain/range, Q, and threshold.

Acts only when energy in its band crosses above—or, in some modes, below—a threshold. It can tame harsh belting while leaving quieter vocals natural.

Advanced · mastering

Linear-Phase EQ

Controls: familiar EQ bands plus a linear-phase processing mode.

Uses look-ahead/FIR processing to preserve relative phase through the EQ, helping prevent phase smearing or cancellation in sensitive mastering and parallel paths. It adds latency and may create pre-ringing.

Specialized · analog circuit

Passive EQ

Controls: broad, interacting boost/cut bands that depend on the circuit.

Uses resistors, capacitors, and inductors for EQ rather than powered gain stages. Hardware pairs the passive network with amplification; models are valued for broad curves and vintage color.

3. The sweep-and-cut method

  1. Name the symptom: rumble, mud, boxiness, nasal tone, harshness, hiss, or something else.
  2. Narrow the Q and temporarily boost while sweeping frequency to make the suspected problem obvious.
  3. Stop when the ugly quality jumps out. That is evidence, not a final setting.
  4. Turn the boost into a modest cut, widen Q if needed, then compare bypassed and processed levels fairly.

A dramatic soloed EQ curve can be impressive and still make the full mix worse. Always judge the source in context.

4. Choose the right EQ

A. Eight fixed frequencies keep feeding back during a live show, and the engineer must react quickly.

B. A singer becomes harsh near 3.5 kHz only on the loudest notes.

C. A mastering engineer needs EQ on a parallel signal while keeping frequency components aligned.

D. A dialogue recording has one constant 520 Hz boxy resonance that must be isolated precisely.

E. An orchestra needs a gentle, broad increase in brightness above 8 kHz.

F. A simple mixer channel offers sweepable mids and gain, but no Q knob.

G. A mix needs broad, musical tone shaping with the character of a vintage analog circuit.

Frequency landmarks

20–80 Hz · Rumble

Handling noise, HVAC, footsteps, and wind often live here. A high-pass filter may be safer than a narrow bell cut.

120–350 Hz · Mud

Too much energy can obscure consonants and make sources compete.

350–800 Hz · Boxiness

Small-room and enclosure character often becomes obvious here.

800 Hz–1.8 kHz · Nasal

Excess can resemble a telephone or pinched voice.

2.5–6 kHz · Harshness

Important for intelligibility, but too much becomes tiring.

Rules professionals keep

  • Fix microphone choice and placement before reaching for EQ.
  • Never boost a live vocal blindly while the system is near feedback.
  • Compare at similar loudness; louder often seems “better.”
  • Protect hearing. Short, quiet comparisons reveal more than loud repetition.
