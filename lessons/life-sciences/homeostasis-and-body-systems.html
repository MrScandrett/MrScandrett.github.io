Life Sciences · Biological Systems (9–10)

Homeostasis & Body Systems

Right now, without you doing anything on purpose, your body is regulating core temperature, blood acidity, and oxygen delivery. None of that is an accident — it's homeostasis: the body's constant, automatic work of keeping its internal conditions inside a narrow livable range, no matter what the outside world throws at it. Walk into a cold gym, sprint a lap, skip breakfast — your circulatory, respiratory, and nervous systems instantly start negotiating with each other to pull things back to normal. This lesson pulls back the curtain on that negotiation.

Quick ID

  • Homeostasis = stability of the internal environment
  • Maintained by negative feedback loops
  • Main players: circulatory, respiratory, nervous systems
  • Regulated variables: temperature, blood O₂/CO₂, blood pressure, blood sugar
  • Regulation can be reset, strained, or disrupted by illness and other conditions

The Negative Feedback Loop: The Body's Thermostat Logic

Many homeostatic systems in the body run on a feedback loop. It resembles a house thermostat: notice a change, compare it to a target, trigger a response, and reduce the response as the variable returns toward its regulated range. Biologists call it negative feedback because the response always pushes against the direction of the original change rather than amplifying it.

1 Stimulus Something pushes a variable away from its normal range — you step outside into the cold.
2 Sensor A receptor detects the change — thermoreceptors in your skin and hypothalamus sense falling temperature.
3 Control Center The hypothalamus integrates temperature signals and coordinates a response around a regulated range near 37°C.
4 Effector & Response Muscles shiver, blood vessels narrow — heat is generated and conserved until the setpoint is restored.

As the variable returns toward its regulated range, the response weakens. The response opposing the original change is what makes this negative feedback.

Three Systems, One Job: Staying Stable

No single organ system runs homeostasis alone. The nervous system senses and decides, the circulatory system delivers and redistributes, and the respiratory system exchanges gases — and all three are constantly cross-talking to hold the body's internal conditions steady.

🧠 Nervous System — Sensor & Control Center

The hypothalamus acts as the body's master thermostat and command center. Chemoreceptors and baroreceptors constantly report blood chemistry and pressure; the autonomic nervous system fires off the correction — sweating, shivering, changing heart rate — often before you're consciously aware anything changed.

❤️ Circulatory System — Delivery & Redistribution

Blood vessels widen (vasodilation) to dump heat at the skin when you're hot, and narrow (vasoconstriction) to conserve it when you're cold. Heart rate and blood pressure adjust moment to moment to keep oxygen-rich blood reaching every cell, whether you're asleep or sprinting.

🫁 Respiratory System — Gas Exchange

Blood CO₂ and acidity are major signals for breathing control; low oxygen also matters, especially when it falls substantially. During exercise, ventilation increases to help remove CO₂ and support oxygen delivery.

🔗 Where They Meet: Exercise

Start running and the loop fires all at once: muscles produce CO₂ and heat → chemoreceptors and thermoreceptors alert the hypothalamus → heart rate and breathing rate both climb, and skin blood vessels dilate to shed heat as sweat. Three systems, one coordinated correction.

🩸 Blood Pressure Loop

Baroreceptors in major arteries sense pressure changes; the nervous system adjusts heart rate and vessel diameter within a heartbeat or two — the fastest homeostatic loop in the body.

🍬 Blood Glucose Loop

Pancreatic cells respond to blood glucose and release insulin or glucagon. This endocrine feedback helps regulate glucose; nerves and other hormones also influence the system.

When Loops Strain — or Fail

Immune Response

Fever: A Loop With a New Setpoint

During many infections, immune signals raise the temperature target. Shivering and narrowed skin blood vessels can make you feel cold while temperature rises. Fever is a regulated response, but it can also be dangerous; the temperature model above does not simulate a changing target.

Respiratory-Nervous Loop

Hyperventilation: Too Much CO₂ Removed

Breathing faster than the body needs can lower blood CO₂ and temporarily raise blood pH, sometimes causing dizziness or tingling. Similar symptoms can have other causes, so this example is for understanding gas regulation, not diagnosing a person or choosing a treatment.

Endocrine Loop

Diabetes: Different Glucose Regulation Problems

In type 1 diabetes, immune damage to pancreatic beta cells greatly reduces insulin production. In type 2 diabetes, cells respond less well to insulin, and the pancreas may eventually be unable to meet demand. These are different disruptions of glucose regulation; treatment varies by person.

Circulatory-Nervous Loop

Fainting: A Blood Pressure Overshoot

Standing up too fast can briefly outrun the baroreceptor reflex, so blood pressure to the brain drops before the heart rate/vessel response catches up — the momentary "head rush" or, in extreme cases, fainting, is the visible gap between stimulus and a feedback loop that hasn't caught up yet.

Discussion & Formative Check

  • Label the four steps of the negative feedback loop (stimulus, sensor, control center, effector) for what happens when you get overheated during recess. What plays each role?
  • Predict the result of switching feedback off under one setting. Record the temperature after five model minutes in each trial. What changed, and which part of the model caused it?
  • A fever is technically the hypothalamus working correctly, just aimed at a new target. Does that change how you'd explain fevers to a younger student who thinks a fever means the body is "broken"?
  • Pick one example above. Explain whether its target, sensing, or response changes; more than one part may be involved.
  • Exit ticket: during exercise, how do the nervous, circulatory, and respiratory systems coordinate temperature control and gas exchange? Name one job each system does.
