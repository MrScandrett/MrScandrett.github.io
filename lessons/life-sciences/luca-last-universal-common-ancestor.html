Meet LUCA

The Last Universal Common Ancestor of all life on Earth.

Who or What was LUCA?

LUCA stands for the Last Universal Common Ancestor. It wasn't the very first living thing to ever exist on Earth, but rather the most recent common ancestor that all currently living organisms—from bacteria to blue whales to humans—share.

Scientists estimate that LUCA was a single-celled microbe that lived about 3.5 to 3.8 billion years ago. It likely lived in a very hot, oxygen-free environment, such as the deep-sea hydrothermal vents at the bottom of the ocean.

How do we know LUCA existed?

We don't have fossils of LUCA, but we have something even better: its genetic legacy. By comparing the DNA of all three domains of life (Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya), scientists have found a set of genes that they all share. These universal genes must have been passed down from their common ancestor—LUCA.

DNA and RNA

All life on Earth uses the exact same genetic code (DNA and RNA) to store information. This points directly to a single common origin.

Proteins and Ribosomes

Every living cell uses ribosomes to read RNA and build proteins out of the exact same 20 amino acids.

ATP Energy

From bacteria to humans, all cells use the same molecule, ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate), as their cellular energy currency.

Shared Universal Traits Checklist

If two organisms share a trait just because it's useful, that's not proof of a common ancestor — it could have evolved independently (this is called convergent evolution, like wings evolving separately in insects, bats, and birds). But if all three domains of life share an arbitrary solution — one of many equally workable options — that's strong evidence they inherited it from a single shared ancestor rather than inventing it three separate times. Reflection Questions

  1. If LUCA wasn't the very first living thing, what do you think happened to the other early life forms that existed at the same time as LUCA?
  2. Why does the fact that all life uses ATP and DNA suggest a common ancestor? Could life have evolved these complex systems independently multiple times?
  3. How does studying deep-sea hydrothermal vents help us understand early life on Earth?
