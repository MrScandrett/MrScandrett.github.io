Ecosystems & Food Webs

Discover how energy flows through the natural world.

What is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a geographic area where plants, animals, and other organisms, as well as weather and landscape, work together to form a bubble of life. Ecosystems contain biotic (living) parts, as well as abiotic (nonliving) parts.

Biotic Factors

All the living components of an ecosystem. This includes plants, animals, fungi, and bacteria.

Abiotic Factors

The non-living components that shape the environment, such as sunlight, temperature, water, soil, and wind.

Trophic Levels: The Flow of Energy

Energy in almost all ecosystems originates from the Sun. As organisms eat one another, this energy flows through different trophic levels (feeding positions in a food chain).

1. Producers

Also known as autotrophs. They make their own food using sunlight (photosynthesis). Examples: Plants, algae.

2. Primary Consumers

Herbivores that eat producers. They are the first consumer level. Examples: Rabbits, grasshoppers.

3. Secondary & Tertiary Consumers

Carnivores and omnivores that eat other consumers. Examples: Foxes, owls, hawks.

4. Decomposers

Organisms that break down dead organic matter, returning nutrients to the soil. Examples: Fungi, bacteria.

Food Chains vs. Food Webs

A food chain outlines who eats whom in a single, straight line. A food web is all of the food chains in an ecosystem connected together. Because most organisms eat more than one type of food, food webs are a much more accurate representation of how an ecosystem works.

The 10% Rule

As energy moves up the food web, much of it is lost as heat from metabolic processes. Only about 10% of the energy from one trophic level is transferred to the next. This is why ecosystems have many producers, fewer primary consumers, and very few apex predators.

Predator-Prey Population Simulator

Populations don't just grow forever or crash to zero — predators and prey chase each other in cycles. When rabbits are plentiful, foxes have plenty to eat and their numbers climb. More foxes means more hunting, so rabbits decline — which then starves the foxes, letting rabbits recover, and the cycle repeats. Reflection Questions

  1. If a disease wiped out all the producers in an ecosystem, what would happen to the consumers?
  2. Why does the arrow in a food web point towards the predator, rather than the prey?
  3. Based on the 10% rule, why are there rarely more than four or five trophic levels in an ecosystem?
  4. In the population simulator, why does the fox population keep rising for a while after the rabbit population has already started to fall?
