Life as Information

A strand of DNA is a message. A cell is a machine that reads it. A body is a network of cells trading signals. An ecosystem is a network of bodies trading energy and information. At every scale, biology looks less like "stuff" and more like a process for storing, copying, and acting on information. So what does it actually mean to say something is alive?

This capstone spirals back across the whole Life Sciences module — DNA & heredity, cell types, ecosystems, and natural selection — and asks you to zoom out and connect them.

The Big Idea

Every living system you've studied this module can be described two ways: as physical stuff (atoms, molecules, membranes, organisms) or as an information process (code, copying, error-correction, signaling, search). Biologists increasingly favor the second lens, because it explains things the first one can't: why a caterpillar and the butterfly it becomes share a genome but not a body plan, why a forest keeps functioning after a storm kills half its trees, and why evolution can "try" billions of designs without ever drawing a blueprint.

The claim isn't that biology is only information — chemistry and energy still matter enormously. The claim is that information is the layer that ties every scale of biology together, from a single base pair to an entire food web. That's the thread this lesson pulls on.

Zoom Through the Scales

Click through each scale below. At every level, ask the same question: what is the "message," what reads it, and what happens if the message gets corrupted?

DNA as Code

At the smallest biological scale, information is startlingly literal. DNA stores instructions using a four-letter alphabet (A, T, C, G), read three letters at a time as "codons," each specifying one amino acid or a stop signal. That's not a metaphor — it's an honest-to-goodness digital code, and it's one all life on Earth shares. The genetic code is also redundant on purpose: 64 possible codons specify only 20 amino acids, so many small copying errors ("silent mutations") don't change the protein at all. That redundancy works like the check digits in a barcode or the parity bits in a file download — a built-in error-correction layer that lets a 3-billion-letter message get copied billions of times per organism, across billions of organisms, without collapsing into noise.

For the full chemistry of how that code is stored, copied, and inherited, see DNA & Heredity.

Cells as Processors

A cell doesn't just contain the genetic message — it's a machine built to execute it. Ribosomes read RNA transcripts and translate them into proteins the way a processor executes machine code. Signaling pathways relay "if this, then that" logic across the cell (a hormone binds a receptor → triggers a cascade → switches a gene on or off). Different cell types are, in a real sense, the same underlying code running different subroutines: a neuron and a skin cell carry identical DNA but read different parts of it, the way two apps can share a codebase but run completely different features.

Explore how specialization changes what a cell "processes" in Types of Cells.

Ecosystems as Networks

Zoom out past any single organism and the information framing doesn't stop — it changes shape. A food web is a network where energy flows along edges and "information" travels as population signals: a predator boom pushes prey numbers down, which pushes predator numbers down a generation later, the same feedback logic your cells use to keep chemistry in balance, just running on a much slower clock and a much bigger scale. Losing one species doesn't silently disappear — it propagates through the network like a corrupted packet, sometimes absorbed and corrected, sometimes cascading into collapse.

Trace those feedback loops in Ecosystems & Food Webs.

Evolution as Optimization

If DNA is code, evolution is the process that edits it — blindly, slowly, and without ever seeing the finished product. Natural selection behaves like a search algorithm running on the entire biosphere at once: random mutation proposes changes, the environment scores them by survival and reproduction, and the highest-scoring variants get copied into the next generation. Run that loop for billions of years and you get eyes, immune systems, and echolocation — solutions no single "designer" ever planned, arrived at the same way a computer might brute-force its way toward a good answer by testing millions of small variations.

    Open Discussion: Where Does "Alive" End?

    None of this settles the question — it sharpens it. Use these to argue it out as a class, in writing, or in a debate. There isn't a correct answer key.

    • Is a virus alive? It carries a heritable code and evolves, but it has no metabolism of its own and can't reproduce without hijacking a host cell's machinery. Does borrowing a processor still count as "having" one?
    • Is a computer virus alive? It copies itself, mutates, competes with antivirus software for survival, and even evolves resistance. What's missing besides chemistry — and does chemistry actually matter, if "life is information" is true?
    • Is an AI system alive, or could it become "alive" someday? It processes information, responds to input, and improves across "generations" of training. What would it need to gain — or lose — to count?
    • A fire consumes fuel, grows, spreads, and "dies" when it runs out of resources — closer to metabolism and reproduction than a rock is. Why do we still say, with total confidence, that fire isn't alive?
    • If you had to rewrite the definition of life today, knowing what you know about DNA, cells, ecosystems, and evolution from this module — would you lead with chemistry, or with information? Why?
