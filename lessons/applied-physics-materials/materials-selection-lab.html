Materials Selection Lab

Tradeoffs Constraints Weight & Strength Thermal & Cost

There is no single "best" material — only the material that best fits a specific job's constraints. This lab pulls together everything else in this module: the viscosity, elasticity, and conductivity you've already tested are exactly the kind of properties real engineers weigh against each other, every time they choose what to build with.

Every Choice Is a Tradeoff

No material wins on every property at once. Steel is incredibly strong, but heavy. Silicone is light and flexible, but weak. A good engineer doesn't ask "what's the best material?" — they ask "what does this specific job actually require, and which material satisfies those constraints without wasting money on properties nobody needs?"

Practice Problems

Easy1. A phone case needs to absorb drops without adding much weight. Which two properties matter most?

Hint: Absorbing an impact without breaking is flexibility; "not adding much weight" is the weight score.

Easy2. Why would steel be a poor choice for a pan handle?

Hint: Steel conducts heat well — great for the cooking surface, dangerous for the handle.

Medium3. Aluminum scores 4/5 weight and 4/5 strength. Why is it a common choice for bike frames instead of steel (1/5 weight, 5/5 strength)?

Hint: A bike frame doesn't need steel's extra strength badly enough to justify carrying its extra weight.

Medium4. Silicone and ceramic both score 5/5 on heat safety. Why is silicone used for pot holders while ceramic is used for pan handles' decorative knobs, not the whole handle?

Hint: Ceramic scores only 2/5 on strength — same heat safety, very different toughness under repeated stress.

Challenge5. Copper conducts electricity better than steel and is a decent structural metal, but it's rarely used for tool handles or bike frames. Using the table above, name the property that rules it out for those jobs.

Hint: Copper's high cost is the main reason it's reserved for wiring and plumbing, not general structural parts.
