The evolution of wood

Wood is not just "trees being tall." It evolved roughly 400 million years ago in small, ankle-high plants — not to hold anything up, but to move water. Height came later. And once lignin existed, something even stranger happened: for nearly 100 million years, almost nothing on Earth could break it down.

Start with the surprising part

Wood didn't evolve to make trees tall

It's tempting to assume wood evolved so plants could grow tall and out-compete each other for sunlight. Fossil evidence points to a different story. The oldest confirmed woody plant, Armoricaphyton chateaupannense (~407 million years old, from what is now France), was a small plant with narrow woody stems — no taller than its herbaceous neighbors. Researchers who scanned its stems concluded that its wood mainly improved water transport, likely a response to falling atmospheric CO₂, which forced plants to keep their pores open longer and lose more water. Height, and the towering trunks we associate with "wood," came later, as other plant lineages co-opted the same tissue for structural support.

A contested part of the story

Did fungi really take 100 million years to catch up?

Fungi as a kingdom are ancient — likely well over 500 million years old, possibly over a billion by some molecular-clock estimates, long before wood evolved. A widely repeated explanation for the Carboniferous coal boom says that existing fungi could only digest sugars and starches, not tough, irregular lignin, so dead wood piled up largely undecayed for tens of millions of years until white-rot fungi evolved lignin-degrading enzymes.

The "No-Rot" Hypothesis (2012)

A genomic study proposed that white-rot fungi evolved lignin peroxidase enzymes around 290 million years ago, and tied that timing to the end of major coal formation — implying a ~100-million-year lag between wood and the fungi that could fully break it down.

The Counter-Evidence (2016)

A later study (Nelsen et al., published in PNAS) argued lignin-degrading fungi likely existed earlier than that, and that wet, low-oxygen burial conditions and the tectonic position of tropical swamps mattered more to coal formation than any single fungal innovation.

Where this stands: Scientists agree wood, coal swamps, and eventually powerful lignin-degrading fungi are all real. What's still debated is how much of the Carboniferous coal boom is explained by an evolutionary lag in fungi versus by climate and burial conditions. Treat "fungi hadn't evolved yet" as one contested hypothesis, not a settled fact.

Common misconception, checked

Do all fossil fuels come from that gap?

Most coal burned today does trace back to Carboniferous swamp forests. Oil and natural gas do not — they mostly formed later, from an entirely different kind of source material.

Coal

Yes — largely from land plants

Formed largely during the Carboniferous period (~359–299 Mya) from lignin-rich trees and swamp vegetation buried in wet, low-oxygen sediment before it could fully decay.

Oil & natural gas

No — a different source entirely

Most of today's oil and gas reserves formed from marine algae and plankton buried in oxygen-poor ocean basins, largely during the Mesozoic era (~252–66 Mya) — tens of millions of years after the Carboniferous coal swamps.
