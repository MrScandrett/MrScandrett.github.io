Extension · Grades 6–12

Language as a Logic System

Older students can study language the way a programmer studies code — a structured, rule-governed system for encoding thought.

Greek & Latin Roots

Roots carry meaning across many words. bio- (life), graph- (write), port- (carry). One root unlocks dozens of words.

Affixes

Prefixes and suffixes act like reusable code blocks: un-, re-, -tion, -ly — modifying base words predictably.

Etymology

Word histories reveal migration, trade, and culture. English borrowed from Latin, French, Norse, and Arabic across centuries.

Grammar

Sentence structure is logic for thought. Subject, predicate, clause — these are the data structures of language.

Rhetoric

Word order, tone, and structure all affect how meaning lands. Language choices shape response as much as content does.

Encoding

Writing systems are compression formats for thought and memory. Letters are symbols that store spoken sound across time.

Lesson objective Students connect the five stages of language (sound → symbol → word → sentence → meaning) and demonstrate each step using the interactive tools.
Sound Lab Have students say the letter sound aloud before selecting a word. Hearing first, then choosing, reinforces phonemic awareness before decoding begins.
Phonics Builder Digraphs (sh, ch, th) are kept as single tiles — two letters that make one sound. Point this out as the key insight before students begin.
Syllable Lab Clap along with students. Say the word, tap once per beat, then count. Physical engagement deepens phonological awareness significantly.
Sentence Machine After building a sentence, ask: what changes if you move the period word last? What if you swap the subject? Explore word-order effects.
Read-Speak-Write Loop The loop mirrors four paths to word retention: visual, auditory, kinesthetic (typing), and generative (using). All four together cement a word in memory.
PhonemeThe smallest unit of sound in a language. "cat" has three: /k/, /æ/, /t/.
GraphemeThe written symbol — a letter or letter group — that represents a phoneme.
DigraphTwo letters that together make one sound: sh, ch, th, wh, ck.
CVC WordConsonant-Vowel-Consonant — the simplest English pattern: cat, sun, pin, dog.
SyllableA unit of spoken language built around one vowel sound. Every syllable is exactly one beat.
Phonological AwarenessThe ability to hear and manipulate spoken sounds — a key early predictor of reading success.
DecodeTo translate written symbols back into spoken sounds — what readers do with unfamiliar words.
EncodeTo translate spoken sounds into written symbols — what writers do when spelling.
FluencyAccurate, automatic, expressive reading — the bridge between decoding and comprehension.
SyntaxThe rules that govern word order and sentence structure in a language.
Sound Sorting Sort all 26 letters into vowels and consonants using the Alphabet Lab. Ask: what happens in a word with no vowel? Why does every syllable need one?
Beat Detective Pick any word. Predict the syllable count, clap it out, then check the Syllable Lab. Which words surprised you most?
Sentence Expand Build a short sentence in the Sentence Machine, then describe one word you could add that would change the meaning most. What part of speech is it?
Loop Challenge Complete the Read-Speak-Write Loop five times with different words. Which of the four steps is hardest? That's where to focus practice.
Digraph Hunt (Grades 3–5) Find every digraph in the Phonics Builder. How many words can you spell using only sh, ch, th, or wh?
Root Hunt (Grades 6–12) Take any word from today and trace it to its origin language. How many modern English words share that root?
