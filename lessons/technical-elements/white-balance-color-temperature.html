White Balance & Color Temperature

Kelvin Scale Warm vs. Cool Skin Tone CRI

Go Deeper: The Kelvin Scale & CRI

Color temperature runs from warm/orange at low Kelvin (candlelight ≈ 1900K) to cool/blue at high Kelvin (open shade ≈ 8000K) — the opposite of how "warm" and "cool" sound, since higher-temperature stars really do glow bluer. CRI (Color Rendering Index) is a separate number: it measures how accurately a light source reveals true colors, on a scale to 100. A light can be perfectly white-balanced and still have poor CRI — meaning skin and fabric colors look slightly wrong no matter how you correct the tint, because the light itself is missing parts of the color spectrum. That's why productions care about high-CRI bulbs, not just correct Kelvin.

Practice Problems

Easy1. Which light source has the lowest color temperature (warmest, most orange)?

Hint: Candlelight sits around 1900K — the lowest, warmest end of the scale.

Easy2. If your white balance is set way higher (cooler) than the actual light, the image looks...

Hint: Try it in the lab — set the slider much higher than the actual source and watch the preview.

Medium3. A crew films half a scene under tungsten light (3200K) and half under daylight (5600K) with the same fixed white balance setting. What's the risk?

Hint: One setting can only be correct for one color temperature — the other half will carry a visible tint.

Medium4. Two lights both read exactly 5600K, but one has CRI 95 and the other CRI 70. Does matching Kelvin alone guarantee matching color?

Hint: Kelvin describes the overall tint; CRI describes how completely the light source's spectrum reveals color.

Challenge5. A cinematographer intentionally sets white balance to 3200K while shooting outdoors in daylight (5600K). Why might they do that on purpose?

Hint: A white balance set cooler than the actual light pushes the image blue — sometimes exactly the mood a scene calls for.
