01

Fretboard

Read the grid

Six strings. Twelve frets per octave.

In standard tuning, the six strings ring out (low to high) E, A, D, G, B, e when played open — no finger pressing anywhere. Pressing a string down at a fret shortens the vibrating length, which raises the pitch by one semitone per fret. Twelve frets up from any open string lands you on the exact same note name, one octave higher — the whole system just repeats.

  • Open string No finger touching it. Rings at its tuned pitch: E, A, D, G, B, or e.
  • Fret One semitone step. Fret 1 is a half-step above open; fret 2 is a whole step above open.
  • ✕
    Muted string Not played at all — deliberately silenced with a finger or left out of the chord entirely.

Three kinds of numbers — keep them separate

1–6 String numbers

1 = thin high e; 6 = thick low E. Tune low to high: E2–A2–D3–G3–B3–E4.

0–12 Fret numbers

Where you press. This is the number written in tab. 0 = open.

1–4 Fretting fingers

1 index · 2 middle · 3 ring · 4 little finger, on either fretting hand. T sometimes means thumb. Picking hand: p thumb · i index · m middle · a ring; a pick can alternate down and up.

Hand tip: Place your fingertip just behind the fret wire, not on it. Use only enough pressure for a clear note. A barre uses one finger across several strings. If the hand tenses or hurts, release it and choose a smaller grip. Suggested fingers are starting points, not rules for every hand.

Read tablature from left to right

  • Line = a string (high e on top)
  • Number = fret, never a finger
  • 0 = play the string open
  • Stacked numbers sound together

Where your hand goes

Click any column in the tab, or press play.

Plain tab does not always specify rhythm; these examples use one column per beat. Click a column to step through it at your own pace.

Tab symbols you will meet next

5h7: hammer on from fret 5 to 7. 7p5: pull off from 7 to 5. 5/7: slide up. 7\5: slide down. b: bend (check the target pitch). ~: vibrato. x: muted/percussive note. These techniques change how a note begins or moves; learn the plain fretted notes first.

02

Intervals

The distance between notes is the whole story

Chords are built from a root, then measured distances.

Pick any note and call it the root. Every other note in a chord is defined by how many semitones above the root it sits — that distance is called an interval. Change the intervals and you change the chord, no matter what root you started from. That's why a major chord "feels" the same whether it's built on C or on F#.

The four distances almost every chord uses

Hear each one. Notice which feel dark, bright or stable.

0 — Root

The note the chord is named after.

3 — Minor 3rd

A minor-ish, darker color.

4 — Major 3rd

A major-ish, brighter color.

7 — Perfect 5th

The stable "backbone" of almost every chord.

3

Stack them into a triad

A basic major triad is root + major 3rd + perfect 5th — semitone intervals 0, 4, 7. Flip that middle interval to a minor 3rd — 0, 3, 7 — and the chord becomes a minor triad. Everything below builds on this one idea.

Spelling note: Interval names count letter names as well as semitones. C–E♭ is a minor third; C–D♯ sounds the same in equal temperament but is spelled as an augmented second. The fretboard uses compact sharp pitch labels; use the formulas and correctly spelled examples here to learn the harmony.

04

Builder

Place your own notes

Click frets. We'll tell you what chord you made.

Click any fret on a string to place a note there — you'll hear a synthesized pluck at that pitch. Click a string's label at the far left to toggle it between open (green dot) and muted (×). The lowest sounding pitch is the bass. If it is a chord tone other than the root, you have an inversion, such as C/E. A slash chord can also have a non-chord tone in the bass. Once you've placed some notes, use the playback buttons to hear the whole chord strummed or broken into an arpeggio.

Start from:

Try it: place open E (string 6), fret 2 on A, fret 2 on D, fret 1 on G, open B, open e — that's E major (or press E under “Start from”). Now lower the G-string note to open: which note changed, and what chord is it now? For F♯ major, move every note up two frets, including the open strings: 2–4–4–3–2–2. Finger 1 barres fret 2; fingers 3, 4 and 2 fret A4, D4 and G3. Try the open Em shape first if a barre is not comfortable.

06

Scales & modes

The palette chords are picked from

A scale is a chord's whole family of notes.

A scale supplies notes for melodies and harmony. Start with major or minor pentatonic, one small position at a time. A mode is more than a starting note: its tonic must sound like home. D Dorian shares C major’s notes, but centers D. Compare modes on the same root to hear their different intervals.

Major and minor pentatonic scales have no half-steps; the blues scale adds a chromatic passing tone and does have half-steps. The library includes all seven major-scale modes plus common pentatonic, blues, harmonic minor and jazz melodic minor scales. The one-octave tab below gives you a concrete route for every selection.

07

Voicings

Same harmony. Different arrangement.

Inversion tells you which chord tone is lowest. Voicing tells you the register, spacing, order and doubling of all the notes. C–E–G and C–G–E are both root position if C is lowest. Triads have three bass positions; seventh chords have four.

Choose a voicing for a musical job

Close

Distinct chord tones fit within an octave.

Try: Learn a three-string triad; compare every bass position.

Spread / open spacing

Chord tones span more than an octave. This does not mean open strings.

Try: Let bass and melody stand apart.

Drop 2 / drop 3

From four close voices, lower the second / third voice from the top by an octave.

Try: Compare the resulting bass position and string skips.

Shell

Use root, third and seventh; omit the fifth.

Try: Leave room for another instrument.

Rootless

Omit the root when a bass player or context supplies it.

Try: Cmaj7 can use E–G–B; alone this also sounds like Em.

Extensions / alterations

9 = 2 + octave, 11 = 4 + octave, 13 = 6 + octave; ♭/♯ alters a tone.

Try: C9 includes B♭; Cadd9 does not. C13 often uses C–E–B♭–A, omitting fifth, ninth and eleventh.

Guitar has six strings and four fretting fingers. A chord symbol describes harmony, not a demand to play every possible extension. Keep the defining third, seventh and requested color where appropriate; label omitted tones honestly.

Practice: choose C major, close voicing. Play root, first and second inversion. Sing the bass note each time. Then try Cmaj7 drop 2. Arpeggiate slowly to check every intended string rings.

08

Progressions

Turn shapes into a piece of music.

In major keys, the triads are I · ii · iii · IV · V · vi · vii°. Capitals mean major; lowercase means minor; ° means diminished. I is home, IV and ii often lead away, and V often leads back. These are useful tendencies, not rules every song must follow.

Start with Em–Am, one chord per bar. Count “1, 2, 3, 4.” Change on the next 1. Then try a numbered pattern, or add exact grips from either chord explorer. Move chords earlier or later and listen to how the ending changes.

    Ready. Each chord lasts one bar of four beats.

    Arrange with your hands: retain a shared finger where comfortable, move the other notes the shortest practical distance, and slow the tempo before a difficult change. A mathematically short path is not always the most comfortable one. Compare two grips for the same chord in your progression.

    Sources and scope

    Standard six-string tuning, equal temperament, no capo. Fingering is a suggestion; generated grips are screened for a four-fret window and at most four finger groups. This cannot certify comfort for every hand or instrument. Audio is synthesized, not a recorded performance.

    Can you think in intervals now?

    Play, explain, repeat: play a clean four-note tab phrase; name a chord’s third; play a scale slowly up and down; then perform a four-bar progression twice without stopping. Lower the tempo until you can keep a steady pulse.

    You now know the pattern

    Same shape, different root.

    A fully fretted shape can move as a unit; open-string notes must move too, usually with a barre or capo. Slide the F major barre shape up two frets and it becomes G major — nothing else changes. Try building chords in a song you know using only the encyclopedia above.

    Key vocabulary

    Root
    The note a chord is named after.
    Interval
    The semitone distance between two notes.
    Triad
    A three-note chord: root, 3rd, and 5th.
    Inversion
    A chord with a chord tone other than the root as its lowest sounding note.
    Slash chord
    Notation like C/E — a C chord with E in the bass.
    Barre chord
    A shape where one finger presses multiple strings, movable to any fret.
    Open chord
    A chord that uses at least one unfretted string.
    Voicing
    One specific arrangement of a chord's notes on the neck.
