Audio Compression: Taming Dynamics

A compressor is an automatic volume control. When a sound gets louder than a set level, the compressor turns it down, so the quiet parts and loud parts end up closer together. Set the controls, watch the level drop in real time, and learn to hear the difference between gentle control and a squashed mix.

2. The six controls

Threshold

The level where compression starts. Sound below the threshold passes through unchanged. Lower the threshold and more of the sound gets compressed.

Ratio

How strongly the compressor turns down sound above the threshold. At 4:1, a signal 8 dB over the threshold comes out only 2 dB over. At 1:1 nothing changes.

Knee

How gradually compression starts. A hard knee starts it all at once at the threshold. A soft knee eases in across a range of levels, so it's harder to hear.

Attack

How quickly the compressor reacts once the sound crosses the threshold. A slow attack lets the first hit of a drum through before it clamps down, which keeps the punch.

Release

How quickly the compressor lets go once the sound drops back down. Too fast and it can flutter or distort. Too slow and it's still turning down the next quiet note.

Makeup gain

Compression makes the loud parts quieter, so the whole track ends up quieter. Makeup gain turns everything back up afterward. Always compare at equal loudness: louder almost always sounds "better" at first.

3. Engineer challenges

A. Even out the singer

The vocalist whispers one phrase and belts the next. Make the phrases sit together: output range ≤ 10 dB, peak gain reduction ≤ 15 dB (don't crush it), and the loudest output between −6 and 0 dBFS.

Load the singer, then shape the controls.

B. Keep the drum punch

The drummer wants the kit controlled, not flattened. Get 3–10 dB of peak gain reduction while letting the first hit through: attack ≥ 20 ms. Listen for the "smack" at the start of each hit.

Load the drums, then shape the controls.

C. Build a brickwall limiter

A limiter is a compressor with a very high ratio and a very fast attack, used to guarantee nothing goes above a ceiling. Protect the stream: ratio 20:1, knee ≤ 3 dB, attack ≤ 1 ms, output peak ≤ −1 dBFS, with the threshold no lower than −12 dB so the music keeps its life.

Any source works. Try the bass.

4. Do the math

Above the threshold, only a fraction of the overshoot comes out:

output = threshold + (input − threshold) ÷ ratio

Work it out, then check. Bonus: how many dB of gain reduction is that?

5. Hear the mistakes

Pumping

With a low threshold and fast release on drums, the whole mix swells back up between hits. Try threshold −40, ratio 10:1, release 60 ms.

Dull transients

With a very fast attack, the front edge of each hit gets clamped and drums go soft. Try attack 0 ms on the drum loop, then 30 ms.

Noise lift

Heavy compression plus makeup gain also turns up breaths, room hum, and hiss in the quiet gaps. Compression can't tell the music from the noise.

Loudness trap

Pushing makeup gain feels exciting because louder sounds better. Bypass at matched loudness before deciding compression actually helped.

Compressor controls

Starting points

Rules of thumb, not recipes. Always trust your ears and the gain-reduction meter.

Vocals

3:1–4:1, medium attack (5–15 ms), release around 100–250 ms, 3–6 dB of gain reduction on the loud phrases.

Drums

4:1, attack 20–40 ms to keep the hit, release timed to the tempo so it recovers before the next beat.

Bass

4:1–6:1 to keep every note equally strong under the mix.

Limiter

20:1 or higher, near-instant attack, ceiling around −1 dBFS to prevent clipping.

Kinds of compressors

VCA

Clean and precise. The workhorse on mixing consoles and drum buses.

FET

Very fast and aggressive with some grit. Classic on rock vocals and drums.

Optical

A lamp and light sensor set the gain, so it reacts smoothly and a little slowly. Gentle on vocals and bass.

Multiband

Splits the sound into frequency bands and compresses each one separately. A de-esser is a one-band version that only reacts to harsh "s" sounds.

Sidechain

The compressor listens to a different signal. Ducking music under a DJ's voice, or bass under the kick, uses a sidechain.

Not the same "compression"

MP3, AAC, and ZIP files use data compression: they make the file smaller. This lesson is about dynamic range compression, which changes the volume of the sound. A WAV file can be heavily compressed dynamically, and a quiet classical MP3 can have almost no dynamic compression at all.
