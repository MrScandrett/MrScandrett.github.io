Stage plotting

Before moving the scenery, make a plan everyone can read. A ground plan shows the stage from above: where things stand, how much space they need, and where people can move. Design one scene of your musical, test the layout, then print it for your crew.

Worked example: from a libretto to a ground plan

Scripts describe the set in words. Here is how W. S. Gilbert opens The Pirates of Penzance (1879), a comic opera schools still perform, and how a designer turns that paragraph into a plan.

“A rocky seashore on the coast of Cornwall. In the distance is a calm sea, on which a schooner is lying at anchor. Rock L. sloping down to L.C. of stage. Under these rocks is a cavern, the entrance to which is seen at first entrance L. A natural arch of rock occupies the R.C. of the stage. As the curtain rises groups of pirates are discovered … FREDERIC is seated in a despondent attitude at the back of the scene. RUTH kneels at his feet.”

W. S. Gilbert, The Pirates of Penzance, Act I (public domain; Project Gutenberg). The ellipsis marks words left out.

Decode the directions

  1. “L.” means stage left: the performer’s left, so the right side of a plan drawn with the audience at the bottom. Gilbert writes from the actor’s point of view, as stage directions still do.
  2. “L.C.” and “R.C.” are left-center and right-center: halfway between the centerline and each side.
  3. “First entrance L.” is the gap between the wings nearest the audience on stage left. The cavern has to open there, so the rock must reach that far downstage.
  4. “In the distance” is painted scenery: a drop at the back, not something with a footprint on the floor.
  5. “Discovered” means the pirates are already in place when the curtain rises, so their spots go on the plan too.
  6. What Gilbert leaves out is your choice. He never says where the Pirate King enters. This plan sends him through the arch and keeps that route clear (item 4).

Check the plan like a stage manager
  • Can the pirates in group A reach the cavern mouth (item 2) without crossing the King’s route? Yes, along the downstage edge, but they must wait until he has passed.
  • The rock is 14 ft deep. Where do actors hide behind it? Stage left of the rock, in the wings. Check that space on your real stage.
  • What would change on a stage only 24 ft wide? Everything scales; the arch might move to 8 ft and the rock shrink to 7 ft wide. Measure first, then plot.

Your turn: set the designer to 30 × 20 ft and enter items 1–7, or plot a scene from your own musical the same way. Underline every location word in the script first.

Design, rehearse, revise

  1. Measure the usable stage. Enter its width and depth. Name your production, scene, and revision.
  2. Add scenery and equipment. Rename every item; enter its real width, depth, and center position.
  3. Place at least three performers. Add a clear area for an entrance or shift. Rotate items as needed.
  4. Use Check layout, then trace each actor’s route with a partner. Check views from audience seats and agree on clearance with your teacher.
  5. Write rehearsal changes in your notes. Print the plan and item list, then use Cue Sheets to record when items move.
