Cymatics: The Geometry of Sound

Standing Waves Resonant Modes Bessel Functions Nodes & Antinodes

Sprinkle sand on a metal plate, bow the edge, and the chaos-looking grains suddenly leap into a perfect geometric pattern — a mandala drawn by pure vibration. This is cymatics: making the invisible shape of a sound wave visible. Below is a physics engine, not a canned animation: it solves the equation for a real bending plate, in real units, for the metal, size and thickness you choose, and the sand obeys the same rule real sand does: it can only leave the plate where the plate accelerates faster than gravity.

What Is Cymatics?

When you vibrate a rigid surface at one of its natural resonant frequencies, most of it doesn't just shake randomly — the whole surface locks into a stationary interference pattern called a standing wave. Some regions barely move at all (nodes); others swing through the largest possible displacement (antinodes). Loose material on top gets thrown away from the antinodes and collects along the still, quiet nodal lines — tracing the invisible wave pattern in visible grains.

The key idea: a Chladni pattern is not drawn by anything — it is the literal shape of the plate's vibration, frozen in place by wherever the material happens to be undisturbed. Change the frequency and you excite a different resonant mode — a different standing-wave "shape" — and the whole picture reorganizes itself.

A short history

  1. 1680Robert Hooke ran a violin bow along the edge of a flour-dusted glass plate and watched the flour leap into geometric patterns — the first recorded observation of the effect.
  2. 1787Ernst Chladni, a German physicist and musician, systematized the technique with sand on bowed brass plates and published the patterns — still called Chladni figures today. He demonstrated them to Napoleon.
  3. 1831Michael Faraday observed a related standing-wave effect on the surface of a vibrating fluid — now called Faraday waves.
  4. 1800sLord Rayleigh worked out the mathematics of plate vibration in detail, explaining why nodal-line counts scale with frequency the way they do.
  5. 1967Hans Jenny, a Swiss physician, coined the word cymatics (from Greek kyma, "wave") and extended the experiments to fluids, pastes, and powders using electronic oscillators — the direct ancestor of the simulator below.

The Physics: Modes, Nodes & Resonance

A thin plate bends instead of stretching. Its motion obeys the Kirchhoff–Love plate equation, where w is how far the plate has moved up or down, ρ is the material's density, h its thickness, and p the push from the driver or bow:

D ∇⁴w + ρh ∂²w/∂t² = p(x, y, t)    with    D = E h³ / 12(1 − ν²)

D is the plate's bending stiffness. It grows with the material's stiffness E (Young's modulus) and with the cube of the thickness, so a plate twice as thick is eight times stiffer. ν (Poisson's ratio) says how much the material bulges sideways when you squeeze it.

Natural frequencies

Only certain shapes, the eigenmodes, can vibrate on their own. Each one has a fixed frequency:

f = (λ / 2π) · (h / s²) · √( E / 12ρ(1 − ν²) )

Here s is half the plate's width and λ is a pure number that depends only on the mode's shape and on ν. That one line explains the whole Plate panel: double the thickness and every pitch doubles; double the width and every pitch drops two octaves; swap steel for aluminium and almost nothing changes, because their stiffness-to-weight ratios E/ρ are nearly equal.

Bending waves are dispersive

Ripples on a plate are bending waves, and high pitches travel faster than low ones: speed = √(2πf) · (D/ρh)¼. Their wavelength shrinks only as 1/√f, so going up four times in frequency only halves the spacing between nodal lines. The simulator shows the bending wavelength live; nodal lines sit about half a wavelength apart.

Who can ring which mode

A wave driver bolted to the centre shakes the plate straight up and down, so it can only feed a mode that moves the plate's centre of mass. On a perfect square plate that's only the fully symmetric modes; on a circle, only the ring patterns with no diameters. A violin bow on the edge pushes at one point and can ring almost anything except the modes with a node right where you bow. This is a selection rule, and you can test it: switch the excitation and watch peaks appear and vanish on the response curve. Real plates are never perfectly symmetric, so in a classroom the "forbidden" modes still show up faintly.

How the simulator solves it: the circle's modes split exactly into rings × diameters, R(r)·cos nθ, and each R(r) is solved on the plate outside the bolt with the bolt clamped exactly. The square has no closed-form answer, so the engine solves it with the Rayleigh–Ritz method: it writes the shape as a sum of smooth functions around the bolt and finds the combinations with the least bending energy for their motion. It's checked in the site's test suite against the exact Bessel-function solution for a free circular plate (to 0.001%) and Arthur Leissa's published values for a free square plate (λ² = 13.468, 19.596, 24.270, 34.801 for ν = 0.3). Every frequency it reports is converged to better than 0.05%.
What's exact and what's modeled. Exact (within the thin-plate theory): every mode's frequency and shape, the physical units, how each mode responds to the drive, and how long it takes to ring up and die away. Modeled: one damping value (Q) for every mode; the bow as a pure sine push (a real bow's stick-slip locks onto a mode by itself); a perfectly rigid bolt; and sand too light to change the plate's pitch. Kirchhoff theory holds while the plate is much thinner than the bending wavelength, which is true for every setting here.

Why Sand, Powder, Water & Oobleck Look So Different

The plate's vibration is the same for all four. What changes is how each material responds to it.

🏜️
Sand → the nodes A grain resting on the plate leaves the surface only when the plate drops away faster than gravity can follow, when its acceleration ω²A beats g. The ratio Γ = ω²A/g is the key number. Where Γ > 1 grains hop and land somewhere new; where Γ < 1 nothing can launch them. So sand random-walks out of the loud regions and gets stuck in the quiet bands around the nodal lines. Turn the drive up and those bands get thinner.
✨
Fine powder → the antinodes In 1831 Michael Faraday noticed that very fine powder (lycopodium spores) gathers at the loudest spots instead. He showed why by pumping the air out: in a vacuum the powder went to the nodes like sand. The vibrating plate drives a steady breeze in the air next to it, from the nodes toward the antinodes, and a speck that light rides it. Flip the Air / Vacuum switch to repeat his experiment.
💧
Water film → the whole wave A thin liquid film can't settle anywhere, so it rises and falls with the plate and shows the whole standing wave, nodes and antinodes together. This view draws the plate's actual displacement, slowed down by a strobe. (Driven hard, a real film breaks into Faraday waves that ripple at half the drive frequency, which this view doesn't model.)
🧪
Oobleck → jamming spikes Cornstarch in water shear-thickens: shear it fast and it acts solid. Where the plate's acceleration is high it jams into rigid, momentary fingers. The real flow is chaotic and nonlinear, so this material is stylized: it's driven by the real acceleration field, but the jamming itself isn't solved.

440 Hz vs. 432 Hz: Who Actually Decided Concert Pitch?

Concert pitch — the frequency assigned to the note A above middle C, the reference every instrument in an orchestra tunes to — has never been a law of nature. It's a human agreement, and for most of musical history there wasn't one: it drifted for centuries and was fought over for just as long.

The pitch-inflation problem

Before any standard existed, orchestral pitch tended to creep upward — a brighter, sharper-tuned ensemble was often judged more brilliant and impressive than its rivals, so pitch kept rising, hall by hall, decade by decade. By the early 1800s "A" could mean anywhere from about 415 Hz (a Baroque-era pitch still used today in historically-informed performance) to well past 450 Hz, depending on the country, the concert hall, and the year. The same piece could sit nearly a semitone higher in one city than another.

Two real, practical fixes

In 1859, the French government legally fixed a national standard — the diapason normal — at A = 435 Hz, likely the first government-mandated tuning pitch in history, motivated partly by singers whose voices were being strained by the constant upward creep. In 1884, composer Giuseppe Verdi independently proposed his own diapason scientifico to the Italian government at A = 432 Hz — again for singers' vocal health, not for any mystical reason.

How 440 Hz became the international standard

In 1834, physicist Johann Scheibler had already proposed 440 Hz at a conference in Stuttgart, based on precise tuning-fork beat measurements. Pitch still stayed inconsistent across countries for another century. In 1939, an international conference in London — driven mainly by British and American standards bodies and broadcasters like the BBC, who needed one consistent pitch for radio, recordings, and touring orchestras with interchangeable instruments — recommended A = 440 Hz. It was formally ratified as international standard ISO 16 in 1955 and reaffirmed in 1975.

So nobody "decided" 440 Hz in the sense of a single choice. It's the settled output of a century-long, multi-country standardization fight, resolved by committee for practical reasons: interchangeable instruments, consistent broadcasts, and orchestras that could tour without retuning.

A popular myth, debunked: you may have encountered the claim that A = 440 Hz was imposed by Nazi propaganda minister Joseph Goebbels to induce anxiety in listeners. There is no primary documentation for this anywhere in the historical record, and the timeline contradicts it outright: France legally mandated 435 Hz in 1859, Scheibler proposed 440 Hz in 1834, and pitch had been drifting upward for purely acoustic and commercial reasons for a century before the Nazi party existed. The 1939 conference that recommended 440 Hz was led by British and American standards organizations. This is internet folklore, not history.

What about 432 Hz today?

Some musicians and audio engineers still tune to A432, often citing a subjectively "warmer" or "rounder" sound — a legitimate aesthetic preference, echoing Verdi's original practical proposal. The difference from 440 Hz is small (about 31.8 cents, roughly a third of a semitone) but audible, especially in direct comparison.

Claims that go further — that 432 Hz is mathematically "the frequency of the universe," aligns with the Schumann resonance or sacred geometry, or produces measurable healing or DNA effects — are not supported by any peer-reviewed acoustic or physiological research. Treat those as folklore layered on top of a real, practical 19th-century tuning debate, not as established science.

Try it in the simulator: the Signal Generator panel has quick-jump buttons for A415 (Baroque), A432 (Verdi), A440 (ISO standard), and A444 (many modern European orchestras tune slightly sharp). Real instruments are deliberately built to resonate where their maker wants, which is exactly why an orchestra needs one shared reference pitch.

Interactive Chladni Plate

This is a real plate with real numbers. Choose the metal, the size and the thickness, bolt it to a wave driver or pick up a bow, and dial the signal generator anywhere from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Everything you see comes from the plate equation above, in SI units: frequencies in hertz, displacement in micrometres, acceleration in multiples of g, sand that hops for as long as real sand would. It makes no sound; it shows what the plate does.

Why most frequencies do nothing: each push from the driver sends a bending wave across the plate. It reflects off the free edges and comes back. At almost any frequency the reflection returns out of step with the next push, partly cancels it, and the plate barely moves. At a natural frequency the reflection comes back exactly in step, so every push adds to the wave that's already there. The amplitude climbs until damping removes energy as fast as the driver adds it: about Q times larger than the push alone could make it. Stop the drive and watch it ring down: that takes about Q/(πf) seconds.

Experiments to Run

Each of these is a real lab you can do with the simulator, then check against a real plate if your class has one. Write your prediction before you try it.

  1. Count the modes. Square steel plate, 24 cm, 0.8 mm, wave driver. Use "Mode ▶" to step through the first five resonances. Write down each frequency. Do the patterns get more complicated as the pitch rises?
  2. Thicker plate, higher pitch. Note the frequency of one mode, then double the thickness. Predict the new frequency first. Then double the plate's width instead. What does the formula say?
  3. Steel vs. aluminium vs. glass. Aluminium is about a third as stiff as steel, and about a third as heavy. Predict whether its modes will be higher, lower or the same, then test it. Try acrylic too.
  4. Turn it up. Find a strong resonance with sand, then raise the driver amplitude in steps. Turn on the Γ < 1 overlay. What happens to the width of the sand lines, and why?
  5. The forbidden modes. With the wave driver, look at the response curve. Switch to the bow. Which new peaks appear? Move the bow along the edge until one of them vanishes: what is special about that spot?
  6. Faraday's vacuum. Choose fine powder and drive a strong mode. Where does it gather? Now switch to Vacuum and re-sprinkle. Explain the difference in one sentence.
  7. Q and the ring-down. At a resonance, press "Stop drive" and time how long the displacement takes to fall to about a third. Repeat with Q halved. Compare with Q/(πf).

Real-World Applications

🎻
Instrument MakingLuthiers sand and carve violin and guitar soundboards while checking their Chladni figures, tuning the plate's modes to match a target sound before final assembly.
🔊
Loudspeaker DesignEngineers use the same figures to find unwanted "breakup modes" in speaker cones — resonances that distort sound — and reshape the cone to push them out of the audible range.
🧲
Acoustic LevitationUltrasonic standing waves create stable nodes in mid-air strong enough to trap and hold small droplets or particles — used in contactless materials handling and microgravity research.
🌍
SeismologyAfter a huge earthquake, the entire Earth rings like a bell in its own normal modes for days — the same node/antinode mathematics, just planet-sized and far too low-pitched to hear.
🥣
Singing BowlsA struck or rubbed metal bowl vibrates in circular Chladni modes; water or rice placed inside dances along the very node/antinode pattern this simulator computes for circular plates.
🔬
Bioacoustics ResearchResearchers use cymatic imaging (e.g. the CymaScope) to visualize recorded animal sounds, such as dolphin vocalizations, as an exploratory tool for studying whether they carry structured patterns.

Try It At Home (Or In Class)

The simulator follows real physics, but nothing beats watching it happen. A plastic-wrap drum on a speaker is a stretched membrane, not a stiff plate, so its frequencies follow a different rule; the sand mechanism is the same. If your class has a wave driver and a metal Chladni plate, check the simulator's predictions against it.

  1. Stretch plastic wrap tightly over a large bowl, or use a thin metal baking sheet, and place it flat on top of a speaker or subwoofer (a phone speaker works for small-scale tests).
  2. Open a free tone-generator app or website and start around 40–60 Hz.
  3. Sprinkle a thin, even layer of table salt or fine sand on the surface.
  4. Slowly sweep the frequency upward. Patterns will snap into focus at specific frequencies and dissolve into chaos in between: those focused moments are the resonant modes. Turn the volume up on one and the sand lines get thinner, exactly as Γ predicts.
  5. Swap materials between sweeps and compare what each one reveals.

Practice Problems

Easy1. A standing wave has adjacent nodes spaced 0.25 m apart. Nodes on a standing wave are spaced λ/2 apart — what is the wavelength λ (in m)?

Hint: λ = 2 × 0.25 = 0.5 m

Easy2. On a sand-covered Chladni plate, the grains collect where the plate's vertical displacement is…

Hint: material is thrown off the moving parts and collects where it's undisturbed.

Medium3. Which material would you expect to reveal the full standing-wave height field — both crests and troughs — rather than only the nodal lines?

Hint: a thin liquid film can't "settle" — it just follows the surface it's sitting on.

Medium4. A 1.0 mm steel plate has a mode at 440 Hz. The plate is replaced by an identical one 1.5 mm thick. Using f ∝ h, where does that mode move (in Hz)?

Hint: frequency is proportional to thickness, so 440 × 1.5 = 660 Hz.

Challenge5. At a 500 Hz resonance the antinodes move ±20 µm. What is Γ = ω²A/g there (use g = 9.81 m/s² and ω = 2πf)? Will sand hop?

Hint: ω = 2π × 500 ≈ 3142 rad/s, so ω²A = 9.87×10⁶ × 20×10⁻⁶ ≈ 197 m/s², and 197 / 9.81 ≈ 20. Far above 1: the sand leaps.

On the Plate

NodeA place on the plate that stays (nearly) still. Sand collects in the quiet band around nodal lines.
AntinodeA place that swings through the largest displacement: the loudest, most energetic part of the pattern.
Γ (gamma)Peak plate acceleration divided by g, Γ = ω²A/g. A resting grain can only leave the plate where Γ > 1.
ResonanceDriving at a natural frequency, so every push adds to the wave already there. The plate's response grows about Q times bigger than off resonance.
Q factorHow sharp a resonance is: the peak is f/Q wide, and the plate takes about Q/(πf) seconds to ring up or die away. Metal plates with sand on them are roughly 50–500.
EigenmodeA shape the plate can vibrate in all by itself, at one exact frequency set by its geometry, material and mounting.
Bending waveThe kind of wave a plate carries. It's dispersive: speed grows as √f, so wavelength shrinks only as 1/√f.
Selection ruleA drive can only feed modes it overlaps with. A centred driver can't ring a mode whose up-moving and down-moving halves cancel.
Chladni's lawChladni's 1787 rule of thumb for circular plates: f ∝ (m + 2n)², with m nodal diameters and n nodal circles. Compare it with the Modes tab: it's close for big patterns, not exact.
