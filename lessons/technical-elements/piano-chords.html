01

Keyboard

Read the layout

Twelve keys per octave, laid out in a straight line.

An octave is seven white keys — C, D, E, F, G, A, B — with five black keys filling most of the gaps between them. Moving from any key to the very next key, white or black, raises the pitch by one semitone. Notice the two places with no black key between the whites: E→F and B→C. That's why those two pairs are only a semitone apart while every other white-key pair is a whole step (two semitones).

White key

The natural notes: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, repeating every octave.

Black key

The sharps/flats between most white keys — C#, D#, F#, G#, A#.

Octave

12 semitones. Same note name, letter repeats, pitch doubles in frequency.

This lesson's keyboard spans two full octaves plus a closing C, so you have room to build chords low, high, or spread across a wide range.

02

Intervals

The distance between notes is the whole story

Chords are built from a root, then measured distances.

Pick any note and call it the root. Every other note in a chord is defined by how many semitones above the root it sits — that distance is called an interval. Change the intervals and you change the chord, no matter what root you started from. That's why a major chord "feels" the same whether it's built on C or on F#.

Hear two notes in sequence, then together. Count semitone steps from C; include black keys.

0 — Root

The note the chord is named after.

3 — Minor 3rd

A minor-ish, darker color.

4 — Major 3rd

A major-ish, brighter color.

7 — Perfect 5th

The stable "backbone" of almost every chord.

A basic major triad is root + major 3rd + perfect 5th — semitone intervals 0, 4, 7. Flip that middle interval to a minor 3rd — 0, 3, 7 — and the exact same shape becomes a minor triad. On a keyboard you can see this directly: count keys, don't guess by ear.

03

Chord types

The formulas this lesson checks against

One interval formula per chord family.

The builder and encyclopedia below both check what you play against these formulas (semitones above the root). Add the 4th note of a 7th chord and the color gets more complex; swap the 3rd out entirely for a 2nd or 4th and you get a suspended chord that feels unresolved on purpose.

Major — 0, 4, 7

Bright, resolved. C, E, G

Minor — 0, 3, 7

Darker, moodier. C, E♭, G

Dominant 7th — 0, 4, 7, 10

Wants to resolve somewhere. C, E, G, B♭

Major 7th — 0, 4, 7, 11

Dreamy, jazzy. C, E, G, B

Minor 7th — 0, 3, 7, 10

Smooth, mellow. C, E♭, G, B♭

sus2 / sus4 — 0, 2, 7 / 0, 5, 7

No 3rd at all — neither major nor minor.

Diminished — 0, 3, 6

Tense, unstable. Two stacked minor thirds; the outer interval is a diminished fifth.

Power chord — 0, 7

Just root and 5th — no 3rd, so it's neither major nor minor.

Hand position

Where your fingers go before you play a note

Five fingers, five keys, one number each.

Method books number your fingers 1 through 5 starting from the thumb, for both hands. The most common starting shape is the "C position": one finger resting lightly on each of five neighboring white keys, curved like you're holding a small ball. Compare the two hands below — the finger numbers mirror each other even though the thumbs point toward each other in the middle.

Curved fingers

Flatter fingers (avoid)

05

Reading music

Lines, spaces, and ledger lines

The staff is a picture of "up and down."

Piano music is written on a grand staff: a treble staff (right hand, higher notes) and a bass staff (left hand, lower notes) joined together, with Middle C sitting exactly between them on its own short "ledger line." This lesson's keyboard stays in and above the treble staff's range, so the interactive staff below uses treble clef only — but the reading skill transfers directly to the bass clef your left hand will read on a real piano.

Treble lines (bottom→top)

E – G – B – D – F. Mnemonic: Every Good Boy Does Fine.

Treble spaces (bottom→top)

F – A – C – E. They spell FACE.

Ledger lines

Short extra lines added above or below the staff for notes that go past it — Middle C is the most common one.

08

Triad routes

Find the chord inside the scale.

A major key supplies seven notes. Stack every other scale note to make a triad; play those three chord tones one at a time to make an arpeggio. When the harmony changes, keep the key but change your target notes.

First experiment: play C major’s scale, then play I–ii–V–I. Select ii and find D–F–A. Those notes are 2–4–6 in C major, but root–minor third–fifth in D minor. Compare “Play as a chord” with “Play selected arpeggio.”

Seven notes, seven triads

Select a triad to explore it. Uppercase = major; lowercase = minor; ° = diminished. The chord family stays the same when you transpose to another major key.

Connect the change

“Nearest entry” minimizes the pitch jump into the next triad within C4–C6. It can start on any chord tone and reverse direction. An arpeggio’s first note and a block chord’s bass are different ideas: inversion is named by the lowest sounding chord tone.

Find the next target

  1. Hear the difference. Play the scale, a block triad, then its arpeggio. Name the scale notes the arpeggio leaves out.
  2. Name two systems. Select ii and V. Name each note’s key degree, then its role inside the chord.
  3. Keep the pulse. At 60, play I–ii–V–I with one hand, one note per beat. Say the chord name when a bar changes.
  4. Connect and transpose. Choose “Nearest entry.” Compare shared tones and actual pitch jumps. Rebuild the same Roman route in G or F major.
  5. Add the other hand. On your instrument, play a quiet root below middle C with the left hand while the right hand plays chord tones. Leave out the bass until the right hand is steady.
  6. Make a melody. Land on a chord tone at each change, then add nearby scale notes between targets. Listen for tension and resolution.

In major keys, I can establish home, ii can prepare V, and V can lead back to I. Other routes are possible. V here is a three-note major triad; V7 adds a fourth chord tone. Try the same route on guitar or explore harmony in Music Lab.

Theory references: diatonic chords in major and inverted triads, Music Theory for the 21st-Century Classroom. This workbench focuses on major keys; minor-key harmony has a different chord family.

09

Scales & modes

The palette chords are picked from

A scale is a chord's whole family of notes.

Where a chord is 3–4 notes stacked at once, a scale is the full set of notes — usually 5 to 7 of them — a melody or solo draws from over a chord progression. Modes are the seven scales hiding inside every major scale: center the same seven notes on a different tonic, making that note sound like home, and the relationships change, from the bright major sound of Ionian down to the unstable Locrian. Major and minor pentatonic scales use five notes without half-steps. The blues scale adds a chromatic passing tone and does contain half-steps. Click any key to hear that note, or use the playback buttons to hear the whole scale run ascending, descending, or both.

10

Voicings

Same chord, different spacing

Voicing is how you arrange the notes, not which notes you use.

A C major triad is always C, E and G — but you can stack those three notes tightly within one hand, or spread them wider across the keyboard for a more open sound. Inversion only tells you which note is lowest; voicing is the fuller picture: the spacing, register and doubling of every note.

Close position

Every chord tone packed within a single octave — the default encyclopedia voicing, and the easiest to read and play.

Open / spread position

The top note jumps up an octave, opening a gap in the middle. Common in hymn and ballad accompaniment for a fuller, less boxed-in sound.

Doubling

Repeating a chord tone (often the root) in a second octave adds weight without changing the harmony.

Omitting the 5th

The 5th adds little color, so it's the first note left out when a hand is full — the root, 3rd (and 7th, if present) carry the chord's identity.

Rootless (jazz)

On a 7th chord, dropping the root entirely and keeping 3rd–5th–7th still spells a clear, lighter-weight chord — useful when a bass player already supplies the root.

Progressions

Turn chords into a piece of music

String chords together and the room fills in.

In major keys, the triads built on each scale degree are I · ii · iii · IV · V · vi · vii°. Capitals mean major; lowercase means minor; ° means diminished. I is home, IV and ii often lead away, and V often leads back. These are useful tendencies, not rules every song must follow.

Start with Em–Am, one chord per bar. Count "1, 2, 3, 4." Change on the next 1. Then try a numbered pattern, or add exact voicings from the Encyclopedia or Voicings above.

    Ready. Each chord lasts one bar of four beats.

    Arrange with your hands: keep the right hand's shape close between changes where possible, and slow the tempo before a difficult change. Compare two voicings for the same chord using the Voicings section above.

    Key vocabulary

    Root
    The note a chord is named after.
    Interval
    The distance between two notes: count letter names for its number and semitones for its quality.
    Triad
    A three-note chord: root, 3rd, and 5th.
    Inversion
    A chord played with a note other than the root as the lowest note.
    Slash chord
    Notation like C/E — a C chord with E in the bass.
    Diatonic
    Built from notes of the selected key.
    Arpeggio
    Chord tones played one at a time.
    Key degree
    A note’s place in the scale, counted from the tonic.
    Common tone
    A note shared by two chords.
    Voicing
    One specific arrangement of a chord's notes on the keyboard.
    Closed voicing
    All chord tones stacked within a single octave.
    Octave
    The 12-semitone span between one note and the next with the same name.
    Finger numbering
    1 = thumb, 5 = pinky, for both hands.
    Staff
    The five lines and four spaces music is written on.
    Clef
    The symbol that tells you which pitches the staff's lines and spaces represent — treble (𝄞) for higher notes, bass (𝄢) for lower.
    Ledger line
    A short extra line added above or below the staff for a note that goes past it.
