Why robot engineers run many trials

A robot may sense the same doorway many times, but a sensor reading can vary with angle, lighting, or surface. Engineers record repeated tests before deciding how reliably a robot detects an obstacle. This simulator models repeated random trials; it does not model a real sensor or prove a robot is safe.

  1. Choose the fair coin. Predict how many heads you expect in 10 trials, then run the 10-flip preset. Record the observed percentage.
  2. Run 1,000 flips. Compare the percentage and the checkpoint graph. Does the larger run always land exactly on 50%?
  3. Try the loaded coin or Mystery Coin. Explain why more trials reveal a biased process instead of making the process fair.

Transfer to robotics: Test your robot's obstacle response repeatedly from the same starting position, then change one condition at a time. Count successes and failures; describe the conditions as well as the percentage. Continue to the Arduino bumper robot challenge.

Next: add many coin flips together

This simulator looks at one coin, die, or spinner at a time. Now picture a ball falling through 10 rows of pins, flipping a "left or right" coin at every row. Where it lands is the total of 10 flips. Teach the Math

Theoretical probability is what mathematics predicts before we experiment. For a fair coin, the probability of heads is 1/2. For a fair six-sided die, the probability of rolling a 6 is 1/6. For a draw where each suit is equally likely, the probability of hearts is 1/4.

Experimental probability is what actually happened in your trials. If heads appears 47 times out of 100 coin flips, the experimental probability is 47/100, or 47%.

Core idea

The law of large numbers says that as the number of trials gets larger, the experimental results tend to move closer to the theoretical probability. It does not say the results become perfect, and it does not say short runs have to look balanced.

Vocabulary Trial

One single attempt in an experiment, like one flip or one roll.

Vocabulary Relative frequency

The fraction or percent of times an outcome appears in the data collected so far.

Vocabulary Independent event

One result does not change the probability of the next result in the same model.

Misconception "It is overdue"

A streak does not force the next outcome to compensate. Check the "After 3+ misses" metric above to see independence live.

Check Your Understanding

Use the simulator or mental math to solve these five probability checks.

1. Theoretical Probability: A bag holds 3 red, 2 blue, and 1 green marble. What percent chance is P(Red)?

2. Experimental Probability: If you flip a coin 40 times and get 26 heads, what is the experimental percentage of heads?

3. Gambler's Fallacy: A fair coin just landed Heads 5 times in a row. What is the percent probability of Heads on flip #6?

4. Two Dice Sums: Out of 36 equally likely pairs on two fair dice, 6 pairs sum to 7. What percent of rolls sum to 7 (to 1 decimal place)?

5. Error Scaling (1/√N): To cut your margin of error in half, you must multiply your trial count by what factor?

Lesson Launchpad

This lesson works as a fast middle school introduction, a high school statistics warm-up, or an intervention for students who confuse a short run of outcomes with true probability.

Students Should Leave Able To

  • distinguish theoretical probability from experimental probability.
  • explain why small samples can look unfair even when a process is fair.
  • use repeated trials and the ±2 SE funnel to argue for the law of large numbers.
  • distinguish drawing with replacement from drawing without replacement.

5-Minute Start

  1. 1Ask students whether 10 coin flips should always split into 5 heads and 5 tails.
  2. 2Click +10, then Run 1,000, and use Overlay Run to compare two independent runs.
  3. 3Challenge the class with Mystery Coin and ask how many flips they needed to be confident.
Grades 4-5 Chance Noticer

Use coins and colored suit categories. Focus on more likely, less likely, equally likely, and simple fractions like 1/2 and 1/4.

Grades 6-8 Data Interpreter

Compare theoretical and experimental probability, calculate percentages, and explain why samples of 10 and 1000 tell different stories.

Grades 9-12 Statistical Thinker

Discuss convergence, 1/√N standard error, bias detection, independence, and Monte Carlo simulation.

Suggested Lesson Sequence

This structure fits a 40 to 50 minute period, or expands into a two-day investigation.

5 min Hook

Ask: "If I flip a coin 10 times, should I expect exactly 5 heads?" Let students commit to a prediction first.

10 min Model

Step +1 and +10, then run 1,000 trials. Watch the observed rate enter the ±2 SE funnel.

15 min Mystery Coin

Pairs test the Mystery Coin with 20 vs. 500 flips and write a claim-evidence verdict.

10 min Discussion

Compare groups. Why did "After 3+ misses" stay near the expected probability?

Discussion Prompts and Teacher Notes

What looks more convincing: 6 heads in 10 flips, or 510 heads in 1000 flips? Why?
If two classes run the same simulation, should their counts match exactly? Should their percentages be close?
Why does drawing marbles without replacement produce exact ratios every 6 draws?
How is simulation useful when a real experiment would take too long or cost too much?

Assessment and Extension

Quick formative check

Show 8 heads in 10 flips and ask whether the coin must be unfair. Students should answer no and justify using sample size.

Written response

"How did the simulation support the law of large numbers? Use one exact percentage from your run as evidence."

Extension task

Have students design a new experiment using Custom model and predict its long-run behavior before running 1,000 trials.

Science connection

Connect to genetics, radioactive decay, or particle detection where outcomes are probabilistic but patterns emerge over many observations.

Computer science connection

Introduce simulation as a tool when direct calculation is hard, especially in AI, games, and Monte Carlo methods.

Data literacy connection

Discuss why polls and surveys need enough responses, and why small samples can create misleading headlines.

Theoretical Foundations

Law of Large Numbers: Discovered by Jacob Bernoulli (1713), this theorem states that as the number of trials increases, the empirical relative frequency X̄n converges to theoretical probability p:

limn→∞ P(|X̄n − p| < ε) = 1

The Gambler's Fallacy: The mistaken belief that after a streak of heads, tails is "due". Coins and dice have no memory; each flip remains strictly independent with p = 0.5.

Error Convergence (1/√N): Standard error decreases inversely with the square root of trial count:

SE = √[p(1 − p) / N]

To halve your margin of error, you must collect 4 times as many observations!
