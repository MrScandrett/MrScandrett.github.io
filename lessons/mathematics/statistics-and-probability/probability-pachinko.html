The Galton Board: Where the Bell Curve Comes From

The simulator above is a digital Galton board. Sir Francis Galton (1822–1911), an English scientist, built one in the 1870s to show how the normal distribution arises. He described it in his 1889 book Natural Inheritance. He called it a quincunx, after the five-dot pattern on a die, because the pins sit in staggered rows: each pin is centered under the gap between two pins above it.

In this model, each ball makes a string of independent left-or-right choices with the same probability at every row. The landing bin follows a discrete binomial distribution. On a fair board with many rows, that shape is closely approximated by a smooth normal curve; a small sample may still look lumpy.

The big idea: the central limit theorem

Each landing bin counts the rightward choices across a fixed number of rows. If those choices are independent and each has the same probability, the count is binomial. With enough rows and probabilities away from 0 and 1, a normal curve is a useful approximation. Real-world data need their own evidence before we call them normal. See OpenStax's normal-approximation conditions.

Expected Value The +500 Edge Trap

Edge bins pay +500 pts while middle bins pay 0. Even huge prizes on rare edges barely lift the long-run average per ball.

Outer pegs nudge drifting balls back inward. Notice how engineering changes spread (σ) without removing randomness.

Teach the Math

Independent events: In the classic Galton model, each peg collision is a fresh Bernoulli trial. Previous left or right bounces do not force the next bounce to "even things out."

Binomial distribution: On a board with n rows, a ball makes n binary decisions and lands in bin k (0 ≤ k ≤ n) after k right bounces. There are C(n, k) distinct paths to that bin, each occurring with probability pk(1 − p)n−k.

Normal approximation: With enough independent rows and p away from 0 or 1, the binomial distribution aligns closely with a normal curve of mean μ = np and standard deviation σ = √(np(1 − p)). On a fair 10-row board, μ = 5 and σ ≈ 1.58, so roughly 68% of balls land in bins 4, 5, and 6. Explore the 68–95–99.7 rule further in How Spread Out?.

Expected value: When bins carry point values vk, the theoretical expected value is E[X] = Σ vk · P(Bin = k)—a long-run average, never a guarantee for a single drop.

Vocabulary Distribution

The pattern of counts or probabilities across all possible landing bins.

Vocabulary Standard Deviation (σ)

The typical spread around the mean; about 68% of drops fall within ±1σ.

Vocabulary Expected Value

The probability-weighted average payout per ball over the long run.

Misconception "Random means flat"

Each peg is fair (50/50), yet the bins are not flat—paths combine multiplicatively in the middle.

Check Your Understanding

Use the simulator or mental math to solve these four quick checks.

1. Pascal Paths: On a 4-row fair board, how many distinct left-right paths reach Bin 2?

2. Fair Mean: On a 12-row fair board (p = 0.50), what is the expected mean bin μ?

3. Tilted Mean: On a 10-row board tilted right with p = 0.70, what is the expected mean bin?

4. Expected Value: A 2-row fair board pays 40 pts for Bin 0, 0 pts for Bin 1, and 40 pts for Bin 2 (probabilities 0.25, 0.50, 0.25). What is the expected value per ball?

Pachinko History, K-12 Framing

Pachinko is the arcade cousin of Galton's board. It grew from earlier peg-and-ball amusements such as bagatelle and the Corinth game, which reached Japan in the early 1900s. By the 1930s, pachinko had become associated with amusement halls in Nagoya and later developed into a major Japanese entertainment industry after World War II.

Classroom boundary

In this lesson, pachinko is studied as a mechanical design and probability system. Keep discussion focused on culture, engineering, statistics, and responsible analysis rather than gambling behavior.

History Bagatelle to Pachinko

Peg boards, spring launches, and gravity created a family of games where motion and chance are inseparable.

Engineering Board Geometry

Pin spacing, nail angles, launch speed, and slope all alter real ball probabilities—just like clicking individual pegs in the lab above.

Culture Japan in the 1900s

Pachinko machines became a recognizable part of urban entertainment, especially in the postwar period.

Key Principles

Galton's Quincunx (1873): Sir Francis Galton engineered the quincunx to show order emerging from many small random nudges: bounce left (q = 1 − p) or bounce right (p).

Pascal's Triangle & Combinatorics: The number of distinct routes to bin k on row n is given by the binomial coefficient:

C(n, k) = n! / [k!(n − k)!]

Normal approximation: For independent rows with fixed p, binomial counts align with a normal curve of μ = np and σ = √(np(1 − p)).

Custom Pegs & Markov Chains: Tilting individual pegs breaks identical-row symmetry; the simulator computes exact bin probabilities row-by-row from top to bottom.
