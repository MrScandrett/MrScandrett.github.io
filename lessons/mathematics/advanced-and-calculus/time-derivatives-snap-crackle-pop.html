Snap, Crackle, Pop: The Ladder of Time Derivatives

d/dt Velocity & acceleration Jerk Snap, crackle, pop Where engineers use them

A time derivative answers one question: how fast is this thing changing, per second? Take it once and position becomes velocity. Take it again and you get acceleration. Keep going and the names turn into a cereal commercial. Some of those names are real engineering vocabulary, some are a joke, and some are internet folklore. This lesson sorts out which is which, and shows why a few of those “extra” derivatives decide whether an elevator ride feels smooth.

Learning Targets

  • Define a time derivative and write it in Leibniz (dⁿx/dtⁿ), Lagrange (f′, f″, f⁽ⁿ⁾) and Newton (ẋ, ẍ) notation.
  • Name the first six derivatives of position, give their units, and say which names are standard.
  • Compute higher derivatives of polynomials and see why each derivative lowers the power by one.
  • Explain why engineers limit jerk and snap, and what that costs in time.
  • Describe how every derivative of circular motion turns another 90°.

Prerequisite: Slope of a Curve (the power rule). Related: Kinematics.

What Is a Time Derivative?

Take any quantity that changes with time, call it x(t). Its time derivative is how quickly it changes at an instant: the slope of its graph against time, the limit of (x(t+h) − x(t)) ÷ h as h → 0. Time is almost always written t, and a derivative of a vector is taken one component at a time.

Spot the sloppy part. The famous board writes f(x) = position and then f′(x), f″(x)… But these are time derivatives, so the input should be time: f(t). If x were distance along a road, f′(x) would be a slope in metres per metre, not a velocity. The names only make sense when the variable is time.

Who Named These? Real vs. Joke vs. Folklore

Not every rung of the ladder is equal. Check the status column in the table above against this sort:

  • Standard Velocity, acceleration, jerk (derivatives 1, 2, 3). Jerk has SI units of m/s³ and is used seriously in engineering.
  • Used in engineering Snap, also called jounce (derivative 4, m/s⁴). The term appears in published work on motion planning.
  • Playful Crackle and pop (derivatives 5 and 6). They come from the Rice Krispies mascots Snap, Crackle and Pop. They are used “somewhat facetiously” and the naming is not standardized.
  • Folklore Lock, drop, shot, put (derivatives 7 to 10). These are jokes that circulate online as a continuation of the cereal gag. They have no standard status, and the Wikipedia article on higher derivatives of position doesn't list them. If you see them on a whiteboard, you are looking at a punchline, not a unit system.

Discuss: The video asks “how many did you know?” A better question is “how many of those can you source?” Where would you look to check a claim like this?

Lab 2 · Why Engineers Limit Jerk

An elevator rises 40 m. It can cruise at 2.5 m/s and never pushes harder than 1.0 m/s² (a bit over a tenth of a g). The only thing you control is the jerk limit: how quickly the acceleration is allowed to build up. A low jerk limit gives a gentle S-curve start; a high one is almost a jolt.

Comfort figures reported for elevator passengers: about 2 m/s³ of vertical jerk is generally acceptable and about 6 m/s³ is intolerable. Hospital lifts are recommended to stay under about 0.7 m/s³, and high-speed rail designs use roughly 0.2 to 0.6 m/s³. You can feel the tradeoff in the trip time: smoothness costs seconds.

Same idea, different machines: roller coasters and railways use clothoid (Euler spiral) transitions so jerk, rather than acceleration, changes gently. Limiting jerk and snap keeps cam followers from jumping, reduces wear on cutting tools, and gives smoother surface finishes. Quadcopter planners use minimum-snap trajectories to reduce control effort.

Lab 3 · Every Derivative Turns 90°

A particle circles at constant speed: r(t) = R(cos ωt, sin ωt). Differentiate a vector component by component and each derivative is the previous one rotated a quarter turn and multiplied by ω. The speed is constant, yet velocity is not zero, and acceleration points straight at the centre: a = −ω²r.

Notice: after four derivatives the arrow points the same way as position again, so snap is parallel to the position vector, with length ω⁴R. Jerk points along the path but backwards. Set ω = 1 and every arrow is the same length, only the direction changes; move ω and the higher derivatives grow or shrink fast, because each one multiplies by another ω.

Time Derivatives in the Wild

  • Mechanics: position → velocity → acceleration → jerk. Force is the time derivative of momentum.
  • Energy: power is the time derivative of energy.
  • Electricity: current is the time derivative of charge.
  • Economics: investment flow is the time derivative of capital stock, and a growth rate is a derivative divided by its own level.
  • Geometry: when a vector's direction changes, its derivative picks up that change too, which is why circular motion accelerates at constant speed.

Practice

Position x(t) is in metres and t in seconds. Answers within 0.05 count.

1. For x(t) = t³, what is the velocity at t = 2?

2. For x(t) = 5t², what is the acceleration?

3. For x(t) = 2t³, what is the jerk?

4. For x(t) = t⁴, what is the snap?

5. For x(t) = t⁵, what is the crackle?

6. For x(t) = t⁶, what is the pop?

7. Snap has units of m/s to what power?

8. A rider feels a steady jerk of 3 m/s³ for 2 s. By how many m/s² does the acceleration change?

Exit Ticket

  • A car drives at a perfectly constant 20 m/s. What are its acceleration, jerk and snap, and why?
  • Why can a rider feel jerk even when the acceleration is small?
  • Someone shows you a list that continues past “pop” with “lock, drop, shot, put.” How would you decide whether to trust it?
  • In circular motion, which derivative first points the same way as the position vector again?
