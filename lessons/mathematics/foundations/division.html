Division: think in groups.

A good divider sees structure before doing calculations. Learn to share, count groups, choose a shortcut, and prove your answer.

Grades 3–8 · 45–60 minutes · Work at your own pace

1. See it2. Think it3. Write it4. Extend it5. Try it

01 / What does division ask?

One equation, two questions

24 ÷ 6 = 4. Equal sharing asks: “24 counters shared among 6 people: how many per person?” Grouping asks: “24 counters packed in groups of 6: how many groups?” Same equation, different meaning.

The dividend is the amount you start with (24). The divisor is what you divide by (6). The quotient is the result (4). Division reverses multiplication: 6 × 4 = 24.

Predict first: if you add one counter, will every group grow or will one counter be left over?

The remainder must be smaller than the divisor. If enough counters remain for another complete round or group, you have not finished dividing.

What does a leftover mean?

25 students, buses with 6 seats each: 4 full buses and 1 student left → 5 buses needed. 25 beads, bracelets needing 6 beads: 4 complete bracelets, 1 bead left. 25 liters shared among 6 containers: 4⅙ liters each. The situation decides whether to round up, keep a remainder, or split the leftover.

Your universal check

divisor × quotient + remainder = dividend

25 ÷ 6 = 4 R1 because 6 × 4 + 1 = 25. When you give an exact fractional or decimal quotient, multiplication alone checks it.

02 / Shortcuts with reasons

Choose a friendly route

Estimate first. For 196 ÷ 4, use 200 ÷ 4 = 50: expect a little less than 50. A shortcut helps only when you understand what stays equal.

Use a multiplication fact

56 ÷ 7 asks “7 times what is 56?” Knowing 7 × 8 = 56 gives 8. Build fact families: 7 × 8, 8 × 7, 56 ÷ 7, 56 ÷ 8.

Split the dividend

84 ÷ 7 = (70 + 14) ÷ 7 = 10 + 2 = 12. Choose pieces that divide easily. Division distributes over addition in the dividend.

Use a nearby multiple

196 ÷ 4 = (200 − 4) ÷ 4 = 50 − 1 = 49. Subtract the extra quotient, not the extra dividend.

Divide by factors

96 ÷ 12 = 96 ÷ 3 ÷ 4 = 32 ÷ 4 = 8, because 12 = 3 × 4. Dividing by 8? Halve three times: 72 → 36 → 18 → 9.

Scale both numbers equally

150 ÷ 25 = 600 ÷ 100 = 6. Multiplying both numbers by 4 preserves the ratio. 240 ÷ 60 = 24 ÷ 6 = 4: both were divided by 10.

Fast 5s, 25s, and 50s

÷ 5 = × 2 then ÷ 10. ÷ 25 = × 4 then ÷ 100. ÷ 50 = × 2 then ÷ 100. Example: 85 ÷ 5 = 170 ÷ 10 = 17. These are equal scaling, not magic.

Strategy explorer

Can it divide evenly?

By 2: last digit is even. By 3: digit sum is a multiple of 3. By 4: last two digits form a multiple of 4. By 5: ends in 0 or 5. By 6: passes both 2 and 3. By 9: digit sum is a multiple of 9. By 10: ends in 0.

Why the 3 rule? 10 = 9 + 1 and 100 = 99 + 1, so a number and its digit sum leave the same remainder when divided by 3. For 4, hundreds are already multiples of 4, leaving only the last two digits to check.

Avoid the trap: 60 ÷ (3 + 2) = 12, but 60 ÷ 3 + 60 ÷ 2 = 50. You can split the dividend; you cannot split the divisor this way.

03 / A reliable written method

Long division is place-value bookkeeping

Work left to right: divide → multiply → subtract → bring down. Each quotient digit records hundreds, tens, or ones. An internal zero is a real placeholder.

Keep each digit in its place

Quotient above · dividend inside · divisor outside

  2. Multiply
  3. Subtract
  4. Bring down

Read all the worked steps

    Partial quotients work too: for 156 ÷ 12, take away 120 (10 groups), then 36 (3 groups). Add the groups: 10 + 3 = 13. Long division organizes those same groups by place value.

    04 / Beyond whole numbers

    Division describes a ratio

    Turn a remainder into a fraction

    17 ÷ 5 = 3 R2 = 3 + 2/5 = 3.4. The leftover 2 is shared among 5, so the fractional part is remainder/divisor. In long division, append a decimal point and a zero: the 2 leftover ones become 20 tenths; 20 ÷ 5 = 4 tenths.

    1 ÷ 3 = 0.333… repeats forever. A rounded decimal like 0.33 is approximate: 3 × 0.33 = 0.99.

    Dividing by a decimal

    4.2 ÷ 0.6 = 42 ÷ 6 = 7. Multiply both numbers by 10. Think: how many 0.6-length pieces fit into 4.2?

    Division can make a number bigger: 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12 because twelve halves fit into six wholes. Dividing a positive number by a positive number below 1 increases it.

    Dividing by a fraction

    3/4 ÷ 1/8 = 6: six eighths fit into three quarters. Multiplying by the reciprocal counts those pieces: 3/4 × 8/1 = 6.

    More generally, dividing by a nonzero fraction a/b means multiplying by b/a, since (a/b) × (b/a) = 1. Example: 2/3 ÷ 4/5 = 2/3 × 5/4 = 5/6.

    Zero, negatives, and order

    0 ÷ 5 = 0, since 5 × 0 = 0. But 5 ÷ 0 is undefined: no number times 0 equals 5. 0 ÷ 0 is also undefined: it has no unique answer.

    Same signs give a positive quotient; different signs give a negative one: −24 ÷ 6 = −4. Order matters: 12 ÷ 3 = 4; 3 ÷ 12 = ¼. Evaluate multiplication and division from left to right: 24 ÷ 6 × 2 = 8.

    05 / Build your divider toolkit

    Practice with a plan

    Estimate, choose a strategy, calculate, then multiply to check. There is no timer. Explain your reasoning to a partner before checking.

    Exit ticket

    1. Find 168 ÷ 8 in two different ways. Explain which is easier for you.
    2. Explain why 43 ÷ 6 = 7 R1, and why a remainder of 7 would be impossible.
    3. Without calculating, explain why 8 ÷ 0.25 must be greater than 8.
    Check the exit ticket

    1. 21: split 160 + 8, or halve 168 three times (84, 42, 21). 2. 6 × 7 + 1 = 43; a remainder of 7 contains another full group of 6. 3. Each whole contains four quarters, so 8 wholes contain 32 quarters.
