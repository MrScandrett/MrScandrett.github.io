Circles & Complex Roots

Completing the square Centre and radius The discriminant Complex numbers p ± qi

One move, completing the square, unlocks both halves of this lesson. Do it twice and a scrambled equation turns out to be a circle. Do it inside the quadratic formula and a parabola that never touches the x-axis still has two roots, just not on the number line.

Learning Targets

  • Explain why (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r² describes a circle, using the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Complete the square in x and in y to find the centre and radius of a circle written in general form.
  • Use the discriminant b² − 4ac to predict two real roots, one, or a complex pair.
  • Use i = √(−1) to write complex roots exactly as p ± qi, simplified.

Prerequisites: Completing the Square and Pythagoras & Triangles. Next: The Unit Circle & Waves.

Lab 1 · A Circle Is Pythagoras

A circle is every point P that sits exactly r away from a centre (h, k). Walk from the centre to P: you go x − h across and y − k up, and the straight-line distance is r. That is a right triangle, so (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². Drag the centre or the edge, or move P around the circle.

Notice: in the general form you can't see the centre or radius at all. Expanding the squares scattered them into the x, y and number terms. Lab 2 runs the film backwards.

Lab 2 · Un-hide the Circle

To get from general form back to standard form, complete the square twice: once for the x-terms and once for the y-terms. Whatever you add on the left, you add on the right. Solve it on paper, then enter the centre and radius. If you get stuck, reveal one step at a time.

    When it isn't a circle: if the right-hand side comes out 0, only the centre itself works (a "circle" of radius 0). If it comes out negative, no real point works, because two squares can never add to a negative number. That same dead end is what sends us into complex numbers in Lab 3.

    Lab 3 · When the Parabola Lifts Off

    The real roots of y = ax² + bx + c are where the parabola crosses the x-axis. Raise c until the parabola lifts off the axis. The roots don't vanish: they leave the number line and move into the complex plane, a sideways number line for real parts and an upright one for imaginary parts.

    Press Sweep c and watch the trail. The two real roots slide together along the axis, collide at the vertex when the discriminant hits 0, then turn a right angle and split up and down. The real part of a complex pair is always the vertex's x, p = −b/2a.

    The flipped twin: flip the parabola upside down about its vertex. The twin does cross the axis, at p ± q. That q is exactly the imaginary part: the roots of the original are p ± qi. Turn the twin on and check it against the complex plane.

    Lab 4 · The Quadratic Formula with i

    The formula x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a still works when the discriminant is negative. You only need one new fact: i = √(−1), so i² = −1 and √(−95) = i√95. Find the discriminant, then the real part −b/2a and the imaginary part √|b² − 4ac| / 2|a|.

      Answers can be typed as fractions and roots: 1/2, √95/10 or sqrt(95)/10. The solving variable here is x.

      Slips Seen on Real Homework

      Every one of these came from a real Algebra 2 worksheet. Each is a single small move, and each changes the answer.

      • ✗ (x² + 10x + 25) + (y² − 6y + 9) = −18✓ … = −18 + 25 + 9 = 16You added 25 and 9 on the left, so add them on the right too. Otherwise the equation changes.
      • ✗ (x + 5)² → centre x = 5✓ (x + 5)² = (x − (−5))² → h = −5The form is x minus h. A plus sign inside means the centre is on the negative side.
      • ✗ = 16, so radius 16✓ r² = 16, so r = 4The right side is r². Take the square root, and simplify it: r² = 12 gives r = 2√3.
      • ✗ 4(k − 1)² = 4k² + 4✓ 4(k² − 2k + 1) = 4k² − 8k + 4A squared binomial has a middle term. That missing −2k is the "corner piece" completing the square is all about.
      • ✗ 5 ± √(−95): no answer✓ √(−95) = i√95A negative discriminant means no real roots. Pull out i and keep going.
      • ✗ (5 ± i√95)/10 = 5 ± i√95/10✓ = 1/2 ± (√95/10)iThe 10 divides the whole top: both the 5 and the i√95.

      Going Further · Three Cube Roots on a Circle

      x³ = −8 has the real answer x = −2 (not 2: (−2)³ = −8). But a cubic has three roots. The other two are a complex pair, 1 ± i√3.

      All three sit on a circle of radius 2, evenly spaced 120° apart. Check one with Lab 1's idea: the distance from 0 to 1 + i√3 is √(1² + (√3)²) = √4 = 2. Circles and complex roots turn out to be the same subject. The Unit Circle lesson picks up from here.

      Practice

      Type exact answers or decimals: −1, 3/2, 2√3 and 3.46 all work.

      1. (x − 4)² + (y + 1)² = 9. What is k, the y of the centre?

      2. x² + y² − 6x + 8y = 0. What is the radius?

      3. x² + y² + 4x − 2y − 4 = 0. What is r² after completing the square?

      4. 2x² + 2y² − 8x − 10 = 0. What is the radius?

      5. x² + y² − 2x + 4y − 7 = 0. What is the radius? (Exact or decimal.)

      6. How many points (x, y) make x² + y² + 2x + 10 = 0 true?

      7. What is the discriminant of x² + 2x + 10 = 0?

      8. The roots of x² − 6x + 13 = 0 are 3 ± qi. What is q?

      9. What is the imaginary part of the roots of 2x² + 2x + 5 = 0?

      10. What is the real part of the roots of 5y² − 5y + 6 = 0?

      11. What is (2i)²?

      Exit Ticket

      • Write the equation of a circle with centre (−3, 4) and radius 6 in standard form, then expand it into general form.
      • Without solving, explain how you can tell that x² + 4x + 9 = 0 has complex roots, and where they sit in the complex plane.
      • A classmate says (x + 5)² + (y − 3)² = 16 has centre (5, −3) and radius 16. Find both mistakes and explain each in one sentence.

      Lesson Flow
      Warm-up Plot every point 5 away from (0, 0). What shape appears, and what rule do the points follow?
      Lab 1 Move P all the way round and point out that the Pythagoras line always totals r².
      Lab 2 Start with “From real homework”. Students solve on paper and reveal steps only to check.
      Lab 3 Ask for a prediction before pressing Sweep: where do the roots go when the parabola lifts off?
      Lab 4 Pairs: one finds the discriminant, the other the parts; swap each round.
      Close Slips card: have each student find which slip they are most likely to make.
