Mathematics · Exponents & Logarithms

Exponent Explorer

Repeated multiplication Exponent rules Scientific notation Logarithms Compound growth

Adding grows by steps. Multiplying grows by leaps. Exponents are the mathematics of leaps: a folded sheet of paper reaching the Moon, a virus and a galaxy on the same ruler, money that doubles while you sleep. Explore each idea with your hands, then learn the shorthand that scientists use to tame very large and very small numbers.

Learning Targets

  • Explain an exponent as repeated multiplication, and why a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
  • Use the product, quotient, and power rules and say why each one works.
  • Write and read numbers in scientific notation, and compare sizes across the universe.
  • Explain a logarithm as the question “what exponent gives this number?”
  • Tell exponential growth from linear growth, and use the Rule of 72.

Prerequisites: PEMDAS and Proportional Reasoning. This lesson prepares you for Measuring Length and How e Was Discovered.

Lab 1 · Leaps vs Steps

Two ways to grow. Steps: add 2 each time. Leaps: double each time. Slide the number of steps and watch who wins. Then switch to a log scale, where each gridline is 1000 times the last. Doubling becomes a straight line.

The paper-folding puzzle

A sheet of paper is about 0.1 mm thick. Every fold doubles the thickness, so after 10 folds the stack would be 10.2 cm thick.

Predict first: how many folds do you think it takes to reach the Moon? Then drag the slider and find out.

Lab 2 · Why the Rules Work

You never need to memorize exponent rules if you can see the factors. Each chip is one copy of the base. Choose a rule and change the exponents.

Lab 3 · Scientific Notation & the Size of Everything

Scientists write a number as m × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ m < 10. The exponent n tells you the “order of magnitude.” Slide along the ruler: every tick is ten times bigger than the one before. An atom and a galaxy fit on the same line.

Try a number

Move the decimal: a large number gets a positive exponent (count the places you moved left); a small number gets a negative exponent. Multiplying by 10 adds 1 to the exponent, which is why the ruler above is so compact.

Lab 5 · Compound Growth & the Rule of 72

Put $1,000 in an account. Simple interest adds the same amount every year (a straight line). Compound interest earns interest on the interest (an exponential). Pick a rate and slide through time.

Simple$3,100
Compound$7,612
Doubling time (exact)10.24 yr
Rule of 72 (72 ÷ rate)10.29 yr

The exact doubling time solves (1 + r)ᵗ = 2, which needs a logarithm: t = log 2 ÷ log(1 + r). The Rule of 72 is a mental shortcut that lands within a few percent. Push continuous compounding all the way and you meet the number e in How e Was Discovered.

Practice

Answers within 0.05 count. Use a calculator when you need one.

1. What is 2¹⁰?

2. What is 3⁴ · 3²?

3. What is 5⁰?

4. What is 2⁻³ as a decimal?

5. Write 6.2 × 10³ as an ordinary number.

6. What is log₁₀ 1000?

7. What is log₂ 64?

8. About how many years for money to double at 6% compound interest?

Exit Ticket

  • Why does 2⁰ equal 1 and not 0? Use the quotient rule to explain.
  • The Sun is about 1.4 × 10⁹ m across and a virus is about 1 × 10⁻⁷ m. About how many orders of magnitude apart are they?
  • Explain the difference between adding 5 each year and growing 5% each year, using a graph or a table.
