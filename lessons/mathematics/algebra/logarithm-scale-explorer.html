Mathematics · Exponential Inverses & Orders of Magnitude

Logarithm Explorer: Powers & Scales

The inverse exponent Decibels & hearing Richter magnitude pH acidity Slide rules

An exponent asks: “If I start with 10 and multiply 3 times, what do I get?” ($10^3 = 1000$). A logarithm asks the exact inverse: “What power of 10 gets me to 1,000?” ($\log_{10}(1000) = 3$). Logarithms compress gargantuan ranges of reality—from atoms to galaxies—into readable, human-scale rulers.

Learning Targets

  • State the equivalence: $\log_b(x) = y \iff b^y = x$ for positive base $b \neq 1$.
  • Understand why linear rulers collapse wide dynamic ranges while logarithmic scales distribute orders of magnitude evenly.
  • Explain why a +10 dB increase sounds twice as loud and represents $10\times$ more sound intensity.
  • Use the product property $\log(a \cdot b) = \log(a) + \log(b)$ to see how slide rules turned multiplication into addition.

Prerequisites: Exponent Explorer and Proportional Reasoning. Next: How e Was Discovered.

Lab 2 · Why We Need Logarithmic Scales

Imagine trying to show the mass or size of things in the universe on a standard linear ruler. If the ruler goes from $1\text{ mm}$ to $1\text{ km}$, a grain of pollen, a human, and an elephant all get squashed into the very first millimeter at 0!

On the Linear Ruler, 99.9% of all everyday items are jammed against the left edge. On the Logarithmic Ruler, each tick mark represents multiplying by 10 ($10^0, 10^1, 10^2, 10^3 \dots$), allowing every scale of reality to have its own visible neighborhood.

Lab 3 · Real-World Logarithmic Scales

Nature often operates logarithmically because human sensory systems (ears, eyes) compress huge physical ranges to prevent overload.

Sound decibels follow $dB = 10 \log_{10}(I / I_0)$. An increase of +10 dB means the sound wave has 10 times more power, and sounds roughly twice as loud to human ears.

Lab 4 · The Slide Rule Secret: Multiplying by Adding

Before pocket calculators, engineers designed the Apollo moon missions with slide rules. The secret? The fundamental logarithm identity:

log(A × B) = log(A) + log(B)

Because physical distances on a log ruler are proportional to logarithms, sliding a physical ruler by distance $\log(B)$ adds the lengths together—reading out the multiplication product directly!
