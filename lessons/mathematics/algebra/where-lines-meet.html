Where Lines Meet

Systems of equations Intersections Break-even Elimination No solution, one, or infinitely many

One equation with two unknowns has endless answers. Two equations can pin down exactly one. Graphically that is two lines crossing at a single point, and that point answers real questions: when does one phone plan become cheaper, and when does a faster runner catch a slower one?

Learning Targets

  • Explain a solution to a system as the point that makes both equations true, and find it on a graph.
  • Predict from slopes and intercepts whether two lines cross once, never, or are the same line.
  • Model a break-even problem with two linear equations and interpret the crossing point.
  • Solve a system by elimination and check the answer by substitution.

Prerequisites: Linear Equations & Slope. Next: Graphing Calculator.

Lab 1 · Make Two Lines Cross

Each line is y = mx + b. Change the slope m and the y-intercept b of both lines. The point where they cross is the solution: the only (x, y) that works in both equations. Try to make the lines parallel. Try to make them the same line.

Lab 2 · Break-Even: Which Plan Wins?

Plan A costs a fixed $20 plus $3 per gigabyte. Plan B has a different fixed fee and a different price per gigabyte. Set Plan B's numbers. The lines show the total cost for each amount of data, and the crossing is the break-even amount where both plans cost the same.

Discuss: a student uses 8 GB a month. Which plan should they choose? What if they use 20 GB? The crossing point is a decision boundary, and businesses design prices around it.

Lab 3 · Solve by Elimination

Graphing gives an approximate answer. Algebra gives an exact one. In elimination you scale the equations so one variable cancels when you subtract, then solve for the other. Press “New system” and try it yourself first. The lines are drawn so you can see the answer, and “Show steps” walks through the algebra.

Three Ways to Solve a System

  • Graphing: quick and visual, but only as exact as your eye.
  • Substitution: when one equation says y = something, put that in the other. Setting 2x + 1 = −x + 7 gives x = 2.
  • Elimination: add or subtract the equations so a variable cancels. Best when both are in the form ax + by = c.

Always check by putting your (x, y) back into both original equations. If either fails, there is an arithmetic slip.

Practice

Answers within 0.05 count.

1. y = 2x + 1 and y = −x + 7 cross where x = ?

2. y = x + 3 and y = 2x − 1 cross at a point. What is y?

3. How many solutions do y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x + 5 have?

4. Plan A: $20 + $3 per unit. Plan B: $50 + $1 per unit. At how many units do they cost the same?

5. x + y = 10 and x − y = 2. What is x?

6. 2x + y = 11 and x − y = 1. What is y?

7. y = 3x and y = x + 6 cross where x = ?

8. Runner A starts at 0 ft and runs 8 ft/s. Runner B starts 100 ft ahead and runs 6 ft/s. After how many seconds does A catch B?

Exit Ticket

  • Without graphing, decide how many solutions y = 3x − 2 and y = 3x + 4 have. Explain how you know.
  • Write a system that has the solution (2, 5). Then explain how you can be sure it works.
  • Describe a real decision where the break-even point changes the best choice.
Solution Types
Different slopes one solution
Same slope, different b no solution
Same slope and b infinitely many
Elimination
1. Scale so a variable has matching coefficients.
2. Subtract (or add) to cancel it.
3. Solve, substitute back, then check both equations.
