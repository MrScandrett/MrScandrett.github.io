Algebra Tiles: Factoring

Area model Multiplying binomials Factoring trinomials Special patterns Toward completing the square

Factoring feels like guesswork until you see it. Every product of two binomials is the area of a rectangle, and factoring is the puzzle of arranging tiles into that rectangle. Build it, spot the pattern, then use the pattern without the tiles.

Learning Targets

  • Represent x², x, and 1 as areas, and multiply binomials with an area model.
  • Factor x² + bx + c by finding two numbers with product c and sum b.
  • Recognize a difference of squares and a perfect square trinomial.
  • Use factoring to solve a quadratic equation, and see why it sets up completing the square.

Prerequisites: Balance, Variables & Equations and Area & Perimeter. Next: Completing the Square.

Lab 1 · Meet the Tiles

There are three kinds of tile. The big square is x², the long strip is x, and the small square is 1. The strip is exactly as long as the big square's side, but nobody knows how long that is. Drag the slider: the strip and the big square change size together, while the unit tile never does.

x (area)2.60
1 (area)1.00

Notice: you can never make an x² tile out of x strips and unit tiles alone, because x is not a whole number. That is why they are different colors: x² and x are different kinds of things.

Lab 2 · Multiply with an Area Model

A rectangle that is (x + a) wide and (x + b) tall is built from four pieces. Add the areas and you have expanded the product. Change a and b.

The pattern: the middle coefficient is a + b (the x strips from both sides) and the last term is a × b (the unit block). That is the whole trick, read backwards, in Lab 3.

Lab 3 · Factor by Building the Rectangle

Now go the other way. You are handed a pile of tiles: one x², some x strips, some units. Your job is to arrange them into a rectangle. The sides of that rectangle are the factors. Use the sliders to choose the width and height, and see whether your rectangle uses exactly the tiles in the pile.

The two-number rule

To factor x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. Then the factors are (x + p)(x + q). Negative numbers work the same way: for x² + 2x − 15 the numbers are 5 and −3, because 5 × (−3) = −15 and 5 + (−3) = 2.

Lab 4 · Two Special Patterns

Difference of squares. Start with a big square of side x and cut a k × k corner out of it. The L-shaped leftover has area x² − k². Cut it, slide the pieces, and it becomes a rectangle that is (x + k) by (x − k). Drag k.

Perfect square trinomial. A square of side x + k has one x² tile, two strips of k, and a k × k block: (x + k)² = x² + 2kx + k². If the middle term is twice the square root of the last, you are looking at one.

A Factoring Checklist

  1. Pull out the greatest common factor. 2x² + 8x = 2x(x + 4).
  2. Two terms, both squares? Difference of squares.
  3. Three terms? Look for the two-number rule (or a perfect square).
  4. Check by expanding: the area model should give the original back.

Why it matters: solving equations

If a product is zero, one of its factors must be zero. So x² − 5x + 6 = 0 becomes (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0, and x = 2 or x = 3. Those are where the parabola crosses the x-axis. Not every quadratic factors neatly. When it does not, Completing the Square always works, and it is the same rectangle idea with a missing corner.

Practice

Enter a number. Answers within 0.05 count.

1. x² + 7x + 12 = (x + p)(x + q), with p < q. What is q?

2. x² + 2x − 15 = (x + p)(x + q). What is the positive number?

3. x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + k). What is k?

4. x² + 10x + 25 = (x + k)². What is k?

5. Expand (x + 4)(x − 2). What is the coefficient of x?

6. Expand (x − 5)(x − 3). What is the constant term?

7. 2x² + 8x = 2x(x + k). What is k?

8. Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0. What is the larger solution?

Exit Ticket

  • Draw the area model for (x + 5)(x + 2) and label all four pieces.
  • Why can you not turn one x² tile and some unit tiles into a rectangle of x strips alone?
  • Explain why x² − 16 factors but x² + 16 does not (over the real numbers).
Key Patterns
(x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab
x² − k² = (x − k)(x + k)
x² ± 2kx + k² = (x ± k)²
Two-Number Rule
Product = c, sum = b
Zero-Product Property
If A × B = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0.
