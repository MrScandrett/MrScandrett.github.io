How 𝑒 Was Discovered

𝑒 ≈ 2.71828… Compound growth Self-derivative 3 interactive labs

Euler's number, 𝑒, wasn't invented on a chalkboard for its own sake — it fell out of a 17th-century question about money. It went on to become the natural rate of growth for everything from bacteria to bank accounts to the shape of a bell curve. In this lesson you'll rediscover 𝑒 the way it was first found, see why calculus can't live without it, and find it hiding in the world around you.

What Is 𝑒?

𝑒 is a number, just like π — a fixed decimal that never ends and never repeats. It doesn't measure circles; it measures growth. Specifically, 𝑒 is the base of "natural" exponential growth: growth that compounds continuously, at every possible instant, rather than in chunky yearly or monthly steps.

𝑒
≈ 2.71828 18284 59…
the limit of continuous compounding
Like π, 𝑒 is irrational — and even transcendental, meaning no algebra equation with whole-number coefficients can ever produce it exactly.

Unlike π, 𝑒 has no simple geometric picture like "circumference over diameter." It shows up instead as the answer to a question: if something grows in proportion to how big it already is, and it grows continuously instead of in steps, what number describes that growth? The answer is always 𝑒.

Why That Formula Converges

Compounding once a year at 100% doubles your dollar: (1 + 1)1 = 2. Compound twice a year at 50% each time and you get (1 + 0.5)² = 2.25 — splitting the interest into smaller, more frequent pieces actually earns more, because you start earning interest on interest sooner.

Bernoulli noticed the pattern kept climbing — 2, 2.25, 2.37, 2.44 — but it didn't run away to infinity. It crept toward a ceiling and never crossed it. That ceiling is 𝑒. Fifty years later, Leonhard Euler proved it rigorously and gave the number its name.

A 350-Year Hunt for a Hidden Number

𝑒 was found, lost, and rediscovered several times before anyone realized it deserved a name of its own.

  • 1618 John Napier's tables of logarithms, published to speed up navigation and astronomy calculations, contain a constant that is — in hindsight — 𝑒, though Napier never identified it as a number in its own right.
  • 1683 Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli studies compound interest and discovers that (1 + 1/𝑛)ⁿ approaches a fixed value between 2 and 3 as 𝑛 grows — the first real "sighting" of 𝑒, though he never named it.
  • 1731 Leonhard Euler uses the letter 𝑒 for the constant in a letter to Christian Goldbach — likely because it was simply the next available vowel after 𝑎, not (as popular legend has it) for "Euler."
  • 1748 In Introductio in Analysin Infinitorum, Euler calculates 𝑒 to 18 decimal places, proves it equals the infinite sum 1 + 1/1! + 1/2! + 1/3! + …, and shows it is irrational — cementing 𝑒 as one of mathematics' fundamental constants.
  • 1873 French mathematician Charles Hermite proves 𝑒 is transcendental — it cannot be the solution to any polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients, making it "more irrational" than numbers like √2.

𝑒 in the Real World

Any time something grows or shrinks continuously, proportionally to its current size, 𝑒 is quietly running the math underneath.

💰
Compound Interest & Loans Banks, mortgages, and investment growth all use A = Pe^(rt) for continuous compounding — the same idea Bernoulli was chasing in 1683.
🦠
Population & Bacteria Growth Cell cultures, bacteria, and unchecked population growth all follow exponential curves built on 𝑒 — and so, in reverse, does an epidemic curve flattening out.
☢️
Radioactive Decay & Carbon Dating Half-life formulas use 𝑒 with a negative exponent. Archaeologists date fossils and artifacts by measuring how far carbon-14 has decayed along that curve. Try it in Radioactive Decay →
📊
The Bell Curve The normal distribution — grades, heights, measurement error — is built from 𝑒−𝑥². Statistics quite literally cannot be written without 𝑒.
💻
Computing & Algorithms Natural logarithms (base 𝑒) appear in information theory, machine-learning loss functions, and the "37% rule" for optimal stopping — all rooted in 𝑒.
✨
Euler's Identity e^(iπ) + 1 = 0 links 𝑒, π, 𝑖, 1, and 0 — the five most important constants in mathematics — in a single equation often called the most beautiful in all of math.

Practice Problems

Use 𝑒 ≈ 2.71828. Round answers to two decimal places unless it works out exactly.

1. You invest $100 at a continuous interest rate of 5% for 10 years. Using A = Pe^(rt), what is A?

Hint: A = 100 × e^(0.05 × 10) = 100 × e^0.5 ≈ 164.87

2. What is 𝑒0?

Hint: any nonzero number raised to the power 0 equals 1 — including 𝑒.

3. A bacteria culture of 500 cells grows continuously at a rate of 8% per hour. Using P = P₀e^(rt), how many cells after 6 hours?

Hint: P = 500 × e^(0.08 × 6) = 500 × e^0.48 ≈ 807.5

4. What is the slope of the curve 𝑦 = 𝑒𝑥 at the point where 𝑥 = 0?

Hint: 𝑒𝑥 is its own derivative, and 𝑒0 = 1 — so the slope at 𝑥 = 0 is 1.

5. In the compound interest lab, what value does (1 + 1/𝑛)𝑛 approach as 𝑛 gets very large?

Hint: that limit is the definition of 𝑒 itself.
