Try a 10-minute dive

Choose one familiar equation and one unfamiliar equation. Name the quantities, read the picture, then explain when the equation is useful. Depth is a metaphor for prerequisites, not importance; the deepest cards include open problems and schematic summaries.

Try it: A cyclist travels 150 metres in 30 seconds. Find the average speed, then predict the distance in 60 seconds if that speed stays constant.

Check the calculation and assumption

Speed = distance ÷ time = 150 m ÷ 30 s = 5 m/s. At a constant 5 m/s, distance = speed × time = 5 × 60 = 300 m. The first answer is an average; it does not prove that the cyclist kept the same speed throughout.

Explain your dive: “My equation relates ___ to ___. Its diagram shows ___. One condition or limitation is ___.” Use the deeper cards to interpret an idea; you do not need to solve every equation.

Sea level · 0m

Everyone, Every Day

You already use these — often without calling them "math." These ideas are useful across almost every kind of work.

Percent

Percent is Latin per centum — "per hundred." The % sign itself grew out of an Italian scribe's shorthand for "per 100," written so many times it collapsed into a symbol by the 1400s.

p = (part ÷ whole) × 100
p percent part what you have whole the total

Divide the part you have by the whole amount, multiply by 100, and the result is the percent — literally, how many pieces you'd have if the whole were always cut into exactly 100 equal slices.

EveryoneRetail & SalesTeachers

Area of a Rectangle

Area comes from Latin area — an open, level piece of ground, like a threshing floor. It's always meant "a measurable flat space."

A = l × w
A area l length w width

Multiply the length by the width and you get the area — the amount of flat surface a rectangle covers, measured in little unit squares stacked in rows and columns.

ContractorsReal EstateDIY & Home

Speed

Velocity comes from Latin velocitas — "swiftness." Speed is velocity's simpler cousin: how fast, without caring which direction.

v = d ÷ t
v speed d distance t time

Take the distance you traveled and divide by the time it took — the steeper that line climbs, the faster your speed.

Drivers & PilotsLogisticsCoaches

Simple Interest

Interest comes from Latin interesse — "to be between, to matter." It's the money that accrues in the gap between lending and repayment.

I = P × r × t
I interest P principal r rate t time

The interest — money earned or owed — is the principal (the original amount) multiplied by the rate, multiplied by the time it's been sitting there. Notice interest grows in equal steps each year — that's what makes it "simple."

Anyone with a bank accountLoan Officers

Unit Rate / Proportion

Proportion is Latin pro portione — "for its share, in relation to a part." Two ratios kept in the same relationship to each other.

a ⁄ b = c ⁄ d
a, b known ratio c, d matching ratio

Keep a to b equal to c to d, and you can scale a recipe for 4 up to 40, read a map's scale, or get a medicine dose right for a patient's weight.

CooksNursesCartographers
Just below the surface · −15m

School Algebra

Most adults learned these once and forgot them the day after the test — but they're the first tools that model something changing instead of just measuring something fixed.

Slope-Intercept Form

Slope traces to Old English roots shared with "slip" — literally how steeply a line slips upward or downward as it runs left to right.

y = mx + b
y output m slope x input b y-intercept

Start at b (where the line crosses the y-axis), then climb by the slope m for every step of x you take — the result is y. This is the first tool for predicting "if this keeps going…"

EconomistsData AnalystsRealtors

Pythagorean Theorem

Named for Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570–495 BCE) — though Babylonian scribes were already using this relationship over 1,000 years before he was born.

a² + b² = c²
a, b the two legs c the hypotenuse

Square the two legs a and b, add them together, and that equals the square of the hypotenuse c — the diagonal side straight across the right angle. It's the straight-line distance across any right triangle.

CarpentersArchitectsSurveyors

Quadratic Formula

Quadratic comes from Latin quadratus — "made square." The highest power in the equation is x², literally x "squared."

x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) ⁄ (2a)
a, b, c the coefficients x where it crosses zero

Plug in the three a, b, c coefficients from ax²+bx+c=0, and out comes x — the roots of the quadratic (with a ≠ 0). A positive discriminant gives two real roots, zero gives one repeated root, and a negative discriminant gives two complex roots. Real roots locate crossings of y = 0; the vertex locates a maximum or minimum.

Ballistics EngineersGame Developers

Distance Formula

Coordinate is Latin co- (together) + ordinatus (arranged) — numbers arranged together to pin down one exact point.

d = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²)
d distance x₁,x₂ horizontal positions y₁,y₂ vertical positions

The Pythagorean theorem rebuilt for a coordinate grid: the horizontal gap and vertical gap become the two legs of a right triangle, and the straight-line distance between the points is the hypotenuse.

CartographersRobotics Engineers

Compound Interest

Compound is Latin componere — "to put together." Interest put together with the interest that already accumulated.

A = P(1 + r ⁄ n)ⁿᵗ
A final amount P principal r annual rate n times/year t years

Every period, the rate is applied to the principal plus whatever interest already piled up — so growth curves upward instead of climbing in a straight line. The single most important formula behind retirement accounts and mortgages.

Financial PlannersActuaries
Mid-depth · −200m · sunlight zone ends

Intro Science & the Trades

The point where math stops being abstract and starts being how electricians, engineers, and scientists actually do their jobs day to day.

Newton's Second Law

Named for Sir Isaac Newton (1642–1727). Force traces to Latin fortis — "strong."

F = m × a
F force m mass a acceleration

Apply a net force to a constant mass, and its velocity changes — its acceleration — in proportion. A heavier mass needs more force for the same acceleration. Designs everything that has to push, stop, or crash something safely.

Mechanical EngineersAutomotive Engineers

Ohm's Law

Named for Georg Ohm (1789–1854), who published the relationship in 1827.

V = I × R
V voltage I current R resistance

Like water pressure pushing flow through a pipe: voltage is the push, current is the flow, and resistance is how narrow the pipe is. For an ohmic resistor at constant temperature, voltage and current are proportional. Other components, such as diodes, behave differently.

ElectriciansElectrical Engineers

Density

Density comes from Latin densus — "thick, crowded, packed close together."

ρ = m ÷ V
ρ density (rho) m mass V volume

Pack more mass into the same volume and density goes up. Decides whether something floats, and which material belongs in a hull, a wing, or a building.

Naval ArchitectsMaterials EngineersGeologists

Exponential Growth & Decay

Exponent is Latin exponere — "to put out, expose, set forth" — displaying how many times a number multiplies itself.

N(t) = N₀ · e^(kt)
N(t) amount at time t N₀ starting amount k rate constant t time

Starting from N₀, the amount changes by a fixed rate k compounding on itself over time — models population booms, radioactive decay, and how fast an outbreak spreads before it's contained.

EpidemiologistsEcologistsNuclear Techs

Kinetic Energy

Kinetic comes from Greek kinesis — "motion." The energy something carries purely because it's moving.

KE = ½mv²
KE kinetic energy m mass v speed

Energy scales with mass but with the square of speed — so doubling your speed doesn't double the kinetic energy, it quadruples it. That's why a crash at 60 mph does far more than twice the damage of one at 30.

Automotive Safety EngineersRide Designers

Deep · −1,000m · twilight zone

College STEM Core

Every science, engineering, and data-heavy degree runs through this layer. These are less "an answer" and more "a way of thinking about anything that changes or varies."

The Derivative

Derivative is Latin derivare — "to draw off," like drawing a stream off a river. It "draws off" the instantaneous rate from the whole curve.

f′(x) = lim(d→0) [f(x+d) − f(x)] ⁄ d
f′(x) rate of change d a shrinking step

Shrink the step d down toward zero, and the wobbly average slope between two points settles into f′(x) — the exact, instantaneous rate of change at a single point. Velocity from position, marginal cost from total cost.

Engineers (every kind)Economists

The Integral

Integral is Latin integer — "whole, complete." Reassembling infinitely many thin slivers back into one whole area.

∫ab f(x) dx
f(x) the function dx a thin slice

Slice the space under f(x) into infinitely thin strips of width dx, and add them all up — the definite integral from a to b measures signed accumulation. For a nonnegative function it is the area under the curve; below-axis contributions count negatively.

PhysicistsActuariesCivil Engineers

Normal Distribution

Called "normal" because 19th-century statisticians kept finding this bell shape everywhere in nature and measurement error, until it looked like the default — the "normal" pattern.

f(x) = (1 ⁄ (σ√(2π))) e^(−(x−μ)²⁄(2σ²))
μ mean (center) σ standard deviation x a measured value

Most values cluster near the mean μ, spreading out by an amount controlled by the standard deviation σ — describes how heights, test scores, and manufacturing tolerances distribute themselves.

Data ScientistsQuality EngineersResearchers

Bayes' Theorem

Named for the Reverend Thomas Bayes (c. 1701–1761); his theorem was published after his death, in 1763.

P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) ⁄ P(B)
P(A|B) what we want P(A) how common A is P(B) how common B is

Combine how rare the condition A is with how reliable the test B is, and Bayes updates your belief into the true probability. Even a good test can produce more false alarms than real cases if the condition is rare — that's why it runs medical diagnosis and spam filters.

Data ScientistsDoctorsML Engineers

Ideal Gas Law

Combines three earlier laws — Boyle's, Charles's, and Avogadro's — into one, with R the "universal gas constant" tying them together.

PV = nRT
P pressure V volume n moles of gas T temperature

Pressure times volume stays tied to the amount of gas (n) and its temperature — at fixed n and temperature (measured in kelvin), reducing volume raises pressure. Designs engines, HVAC systems, and weather balloons.

Chemical EngineersHVAC EngineersMeteorologists
Deeper · −4,000m · midnight zone

Specialized Engineering & Physics

Whole careers are built on a single equation from this layer. Most people will never write one down — but every one of them is running invisibly inside a device you used today.

Maxwell's Equations

Named for James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879), who unified electricity, magnetism, and light into one framework in the 1860s.

∇·E = ρ/ε₀
∇·B = 0
∇×E = −∂B/∂t
∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t
E electric field B magnetic field ρ charge density J current density

Coupled changes in the electric field and magnetic field propagate as electromagnetic waves — that ripple is light itself. Every antenna, Wi-Fi router, and MRI machine is built from these four equations.

RF EngineersMRI Technologists

Navier–Stokes Equations

Named for Claude-Louis Navier (1785–1836) and George Gabriel Stokes (1819–1903), who independently derived how fluids move.

ρ(∂v⁄∂t + v·∇v) = −∇p + μ∇²v
ρ fluid density v velocity field p pressure μ viscosity

The velocity of every parcel of fluid depends on the pressure pushing it and the viscosity dragging on it. The displayed form assumes an incompressible Newtonian fluid with constant density and viscosity and no body force; it is paired with ∇·v = 0. Related forms model wings, weather, and blood flow.

Aerospace EngineersMeteorologistsBiomedical Engineers

Schrödinger Equation

Named for Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961), who published it in 1926.

iℏ ∂Ψ⁄∂t = ĤΨ
Ψ wave function (psi) Ĥ total energy ℏ Planck's constant

Instead of a tiny billiard ball on a fixed path, a particle is described by Ψ — a wave whose squared magnitude |Ψ|² gives probability density. Integrating that density over a region gives the probability of finding the particle there. Without it, no one could design a semiconductor chip.

Semiconductor PhysicistsQuantum Computing Researchers

Fourier Transform

Named for Joseph Fourier (1768–1830), who showed any repeating signal can be rebuilt from simple sine waves.

F(ω) = ∫−∞∞ f(t) e^(−iωt) dt
f(t) signal over time F(ω) signal as frequencies ω frequency (omega)

Any messy signal can be rebuilt by adding up simple sine waves at different frequencies. Behind MP3 and JPEG compression, MRI image reconstruction, and noise-canceling headphones.

Audio EngineersSignal Processing Engineers

Heat Equation

Also derived by Joseph Fourier — his 1822 paper on heat flow is what led him to invent the transform above.

∂u⁄∂t = α∇²u
u temperature α thermal diffusivity

The temperature at each point smooths itself out over time, spreading faster through materials with high diffusivity. Keeps a CPU from melting and a jet turbine blade from failing.

Thermal EngineersHVAC Designers

The abyss · −11,000m · Challenger Deep

The Research Frontier

At this depth, equations describe fundamental physics and lead to open mathematical questions. The last two cards ask for proofs that researchers are still seeking.

Einstein Field Equations

Published by Albert Einstein in 1915 as the core of general relativity — gravity reimagined as geometry, not a force.

Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG⁄c⁴) Tμν
Gμν spacetime curvature gμν shape of spacetime Tμν matter & energy

Mass and energy (Tμν) tell spacetime how to curve (Gμν) — and that curve is what we feel as gravity. Without correcting for it, GPS satellite clocks would drift by miles within a day.

AstrophysicistsGPS Systems Engineers

Standard Model Lagrangian

Named for Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813), whose variational method later became central to field theory. The Standard Model describes known elementary particles and three fundamental interactions; it does not include gravity.

ℒ = −¼FμνFμν + iΨ̄γμDμΨ + …
ℒ Lagrangian density F force-carrier fields Ψ matter particles

One (very long) expression built from force-carrier fields and matter particles that describes electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions. The displayed expression is schematic: Higgs and interaction terms are omitted. Tested daily at the Large Hadron Collider.

Particle Physicists

Navier–Stokes Smoothness

One of the seven Millennium Prize Problems named by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000 — six remain unsolved, each with a $1,000,000 reward.

Does a solution always exist — and stay smooth?

For three-dimensional incompressible flow with suitable smooth initial data, must a smooth solution exist for all time, or can a singularity form? Turbulence alone is not proof of a singularity. This is the Clay Mathematics Institute’s existence and smoothness problem.

MathematiciansUnsolved

Riemann Hypothesis

Proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, in an eight-page paper about the pattern hidden in prime numbers.

ζ(s) = 0 and 0 < Re(s) < 1
⇒ Re(s) = ½ ?
ζ(s) zeta function s a complex number

The hypothesis says every nontrivial zero of ζ(s) has real part ½. Many have been checked, but checking examples is not a proof. The trivial zeros −2, −4, −6, … are excluded. A proof would sharpen our understanding of how prime numbers are distributed.

CryptographersNumber Theorists
