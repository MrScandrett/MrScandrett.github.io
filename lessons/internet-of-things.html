📜 History Files: from vending machines to 50 billion devices
1982 · The internet-connected Coke machineGrad students at Carnegie Mellon wired a vending machine to the early internet so they could check from their desks whether a soda was cold before walking over — often cited as the first "smart" internet-connected object.
1999 · The term "Internet of Things"Technologist Kevin Ashton coined the phrase while pitching RFID tags to Procter & Gamble, arguing computers should gather their own data from the physical world instead of waiting for humans to type it in.
Today · Tens of billions of devicesSensors now sit in shipping containers, cow collars, running shoes, streetlights, and hospital beds — most of them running the exact same five-stage pipeline you just explored.

Field Report · Beyond Connectivity

It's not just the "internet" of things anymore.

Early IoT devices just moved sensor readings to an app. Today's devices do a lot more with that connection — and it's worth asking who benefits each time a "dumb" object gets smart.

💰

The Monetization of Things

Many "smart" devices are sold near cost — or even at a loss — because the real business is the data they collect, or a subscription required to keep basic features working.

See real examples
  • A robot vacuum maps your home's floor plan, then that map can be sold to furniture and advertising companies.
  • Some printers refuse to print with off-brand ink, or push a monthly "ink subscription."
  • Connected cars can log driving habits and sell that data to insurance companies.
📣

The Social Media of Things

Devices increasingly share, broadcast, and gamify what they sense — turning private data into a public feed, leaderboard, or neighborhood alert.

See real examples
  • Fitness trackers post workouts to public leaderboards and friend feeds by default.
  • Smart doorbells can share clips to neighborhood safety apps — sometimes without a warrant needed.
  • A fitness app's public activity heatmap once accidentally revealed the locations and layouts of secret military bases.
🤖

The AI of Things

Devices no longer just report numbers — onboard AI now predicts, recognizes, and decides, often without asking permission for each individual choice.

See real examples
  • A basic thermostat cycling heat on and off around a set temperature isn't AI — that's the same simple feedback loop as homeostasis. A learning thermostat like the Nest is different: it logs your manual adjustments and motion-sensor activity for about a week, then uses that pattern to predict and pre-adjust your schedule on its own.
  • Doorbell cameras use facial recognition to flag "familiar" versus "unfamiliar" visitors.
  • Factory sensors predict when a machine will fail days before it actually breaks down.

Goal

Students trace the sensor-to-cloud pipeline, build a rule-based automation, compare wireless protocols by trade-off, spot insecure IoT defaults, and critique the business models behind "smart" devices.

No real devices needed

Everything in this lesson runs as a simulation in the browser — no student needs to connect to a real network, camera, or account.

Facilitating the closing panel

The three "X of Things" cards are meant to spark debate, not deliver a verdict. Push students past "companies are bad" toward specific trade-offs: what convenience did the user get in exchange for their data?

Extension

Have students research one real device's privacy policy and identify which of the three closing categories it fits.

Answer aloud or on paper

  1. Name the five stages a temperature reading travels through in an IoT device.
  2. Write one IF/THEN automation rule for a device in your own home.
  3. Why might a farm sensor use LoRaWAN instead of Wi-Fi?
  4. Name one setting that made Mirai-style attacks possible.
  5. Give one real example each of the monetization, social media, and AI of things.

Expected ideas

Sensor → microcontroller → network → cloud → app; a plausible IF/THEN rule; long range and low power over speed; default password, no updates, or open internet exposure; any concrete example matching each category.
