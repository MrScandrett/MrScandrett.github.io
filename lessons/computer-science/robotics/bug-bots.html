MIT AI Lab · 1989 · Now at the Smithsonian

Meet Genghis: The Robot That Walked Without a Brain

In 1989, roboticist Rodney Brooks and his team at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory built a small six-legged robot and named it Genghis. It was about the size of a large shoe box, covered in sensors, and had six independently controlled legs.

What made Genghis revolutionary was what it didn't have: a central brain. Traditional robots of the time used a single processor to plan every move — calculate where each leg should go, then send commands. Genghis used a completely different approach: each leg had its own small controller, and the robot's walking behavior emerged from those simple local rules interacting.

It could scramble over rough terrain, recover from stumbles, and explore uneven surfaces — behaviors that centrally-controlled robots of the era couldn't manage. Genghis now lives in the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum.

Rodney Brooks went on to co-found iRobot in 1990 — the company that makes the Roomba vacuum cleaner. The same ideas inside Genghis are inside every Roomba navigating your floor today.

The Key Idea · Behavior-Based Robotics

Subsumption Architecture: Building a Brain from Layers

Traditional AI robots of the 1980s worked like this: sense → model → plan → act. First, build a complete model of the world. Then plan the best path. Then execute. The problem: the world changes while you're planning, and modeling everything is slow. Compare SLAM mapping and motion planning to see how modern robots blend both worlds.

Brooks proposed the opposite: don't model anything. Stack simple behaviors on top of each other. Lower layers handle basic survival. Higher layers handle more complex goals. Each layer can suppress the output of the layer below when its sensors trigger — that's where "subsumption" gets its name.

In the studio above, turn off Layer 0 (Stand Up) and watch the whole robot collapse, or drop a rock in Genghis's path and watch Layer 4 (Whiskers) suppress Layer 7 (Steered Prowling) at the last moment!

How Insects Walk · Emergent Motion

Tripod, Ripple, and Wave Gaits: Static Stability

Most six-legged insects walk with the tripod gait (3+3): Group A (FR, ML, BR) plants on the ground while Group B (FL, MR, BL) swings forward, then they switch. Three points define a plane — and as long as the robot's center of mass stays inside that triangle (the green support polygon in the simulator), it cannot tip over.

When climbing rough terrain or surviving a damaged leg, insects automatically slow down into a ripple gait (4+2) or a wave gait (5+1), keeping five feet on the floor at all times for maximum stability.

Emergent Behavior

No central planner schedules all six legs. Each leg follows local phase rules ("lift only if my neighbor is planted") and coordinated walking emerges automatically — the same principle behind ant trails and bird flocks.

Why 6 Legs Beat 4 on Rough Rock

Six legs provide continuous static stability even when one leg is lifted or jammed. Try clicking a leg in the simulator above to jam it and test Tripod vs. Wave stability!

Top-down tripod gait: three feet always form a stable ground triangle while the opposite three swing forward.

Hands-On Activity · Classroom Build

Build a Bristle Bot

A bristle bot turns high-frequency vibration into forward travel using angled bristles as a one-way ratchet. On the downstroke, angled bristles flex backward and grip; on the upstroke, they spring forward. Test bristle angles and battery balance in Tab 2 of the studio above before cutting your toothbrush!

Materials

🪥
Toothbrush HeadAngled stiff bristles work best; flat bristles shake in place.

Advanced Build · Parallel LED Eyes + Buzzer

Popsicle Stick Bug: Add Eyes and a Voice

Upgrade to a rigid popsicle-stick chassis with two glowing LED eyes and a phone buzzer wired in parallel across one CR2032 battery. Because parallel branches share the same 3 V rails, both LEDs glow while the buzzer vibrates the angled bristle legs.

Long LED leg = + (red rail); short LED leg = − (black rail). All three loads receive 3 V in parallel.

Boston Dynamics · 1992–Present · From Static to Dynamic Balance

From Genghis to Spot & Atlas: Dynamic Balance

Founded by Marc Raibert in 1992 out of the MIT Leg Lab (right down the hall from Rodney Brooks), Boston Dynamics evolved legged robotics from statically stable six-legged walkers to dynamically balanced quadrupeds and humanoids that use momentum like running animals.
