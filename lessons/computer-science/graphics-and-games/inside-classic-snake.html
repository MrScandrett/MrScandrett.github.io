03 / UNDER THE HOOD

Four decisions, over and over.

1 · TIME

Tick

Eric's game calls its update about every 100 milliseconds at first. Eating gradually shortens that wait, down to 50 milliseconds.

2 · POSITION

New head

Add the direction to the first body coordinate. Moving right from (2, 3) makes (3, 3).

3 · SAFETY

Collision

If that square is outside the 20 × 20 board or overlaps the current body, the round ends. A turn straight back is blocked.

4 · GROWTH

Tail or food

Add the new head. If it is food, keep the tail and gain 10 points. Otherwise remove the last body square.

See the small piece of Eric's code that moves the body

The body is an ordered list of grid coordinates. The first item is the head.

snake.unshift(head); // add a new first square
if (head.x !== food.x || head.y !== food.y) {
  snake.pop();        // remove the tail unless food was eaten
}

Adapted from Eric's source. The full game checks collisions before these lines.

K–2 · Act it out

Use three paper squares as head, middle, and tail. Move the head one space and lift the last square.

Grades 3–5 · Map it

Write the snake's coordinates before and after a move. Predict when the tail stays.

Grades 6–8 · Trace it

Follow the ordered body list through an ordinary move and a food move. Explain why changing the order of checks matters.

Grades 9–12 · Improve it

Eric's food picker retries if it lands on the snake. What happens when few empty squares remain? Design a way to choose only from empty squares, including a full-board win.

04 / WHERE IT CAME FROM

From arcade trails to pocket games.

Gremlin's Blockade (1976) used a related grid-and-trail idea: players lost when they hit a trail. Later single-player versions added food and a growing body. Taneli Armanto designed Nokia's Snake for the 6110 phone in 1997. A small screen and four direction controls helped make a short, repeatable game.

Sources: Taneli Armanto's history of Snake and his account of Nokia Snake. The genre predates Nokia's version.
