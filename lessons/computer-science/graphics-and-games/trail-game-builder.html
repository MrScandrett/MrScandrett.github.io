Trail Game Builder: Make Your Own Oregon Trail

Game state Data-driven design Weighted randomness Balancing Spin-offs

A wagon, five travelers and 2,000 miles: The Oregon Trail has no twitch reflexes and no fancy graphics, yet millions of students have never forgotten it. That is because it is a risk-and-resource game, and those are built from a few simple ideas. In this lesson you build the whole engine in about 80 lines of JavaScript, then change the data to turn it into your own spin-off: a trip to Mars, a Silk Road caravan, a dive to the ocean floor, or something nobody has made yet.

1 · Why this game works

Every day of the trail is the same small question: do I spend something I need to get somewhere I want to be? Food, time, health and money all trade against distance. The fun comes from three design ideas.

  • Scarcity. Nothing is free. Pushing hard saves days but costs health. Resting heals but eats food.
  • Uncertainty. A random event table means two trips are never the same, but the odds are fair enough that good planning wins most of the time.
  • Stories from numbers. "A wheel cracked" is just days += 2 wearing a costume. Short sentences let a student's imagination do the graphics.

The program that runs it is the same loop every day. Learn this loop and you can build almost any survival or management game:

Choosepace, rations, rest or hunt
→
Spendeat food, lose health, gain miles
→
Event?roll the dice on the event table
→
Landmark?river, fort, shop, story
→
End?won, dead, or out of time

…then the next day begins. It is a state machine: the game is always in exactly one state, and each button press moves it to the next one.

TRY IT FIRST

2 · The Trail Lab: play it, then break it

This is the finished engine. Everything about the game, including its story, items, events, landmarks and balance numbers, lives in the config on the left. Pick a preset, press Run my game, and play a few days. Then change something small (the party names, the title, one event) and run it again. You are already designing.

Reading the config. It is JSON: names in quotes, numbers without, lists in [ ], objects in { }. Click Run and an error message will tell you which line is broken (usually a missing comma). Things to try:
  • "deadline" smaller = a tighter race. "eventChance" (0 to 1) = how dangerous each day is.
  • Each event has a weight. A weight of 4 happens four times as often as a weight of 1.
  • "fixWith": "parts" means a spare part cancels that event's lost days and miles. That is a decision the player gets to make when shopping.
  • Landmark type can be "shop", "river" or "info".
  • Add "seed": 7 and the dice roll the same every time, which is handy for testing your balance.
BUILD IT

3 · Build the engine yourself in seven steps

The Lab above has extras (paces, rations, a shop, hunting). To understand it, first build the core. Make a file called trail.html, paste the page skeleton below, and then add each step inside its <script> tag in order. After every step, save, reload, and run it. Don't move on until the checkpoint is true.

Page skeleton


          
Step 1 · Put the whole game in one data object

          
Before any logic, write down what the game is. Keeping the content in GAME and the rules in functions means a spin-off only needs to change this one block. This is called data-driven design, and every engine you will ever use works this way.

          

          
Checkpoint: Open the browser console (F12) and type GAME.party.length. It should print 3.


          
Step 2 · Track the state

          
The state is everything that changes while the game runs. If you can list the state, you can save the game, undo a move, or replay a bug. Notice that health is a list with one number per traveler.

          


          
Step 3 · Small helpers

          
say() writes a line to the trail log, hurt() changes a traveler's health and keeps it between 0 and 100, and weighted() is the trick behind event tables: add up the weights, pick a number in that range, and walk down the list until it runs out.

          

          
Why weights? If three events have weights 3, 2 and 1, the total is 6. A roll of 0 to 6 lands on the first event half the time (3 of 6), the second a third of the time, and the last a sixth. You choose the odds by choosing numbers instead of writing a pile of if statements.


          
Step 4 · One day

          
This function is the game. Everyone eats; if there is not enough food they starve. Then you either travel (random miles near your normal pace, never past the next stop) or rest (everyone heals). After that there is a 40% chance of an event, and finally the day counter moves on.

          

          
Checkpoint: Press Travel five times. The mileage should climb about 15 a day, food should drop by 6 each day, and now and then the log should show an event.


          
Step 5 · The event table

          
Events are rows of data, so adding a new danger is one line. The fixWith trick gives the player a reason to have bought spare parts. Preparation pays off, and that is a real choice, not just luck.

          


          
Step 6 · Landmarks and choices

          
When your miles pass a landmark, the game stops and asks a question. The choose() helper shows buttons and waits. A river crossing is a perfect risk vs. cost decision: fording is free but risky, the raft is safe but costs a day.

          


          
Step 7 · Win, lose, and wire it up

          
A game needs an ending. Check the three ways the trip can finish after every day, then connect the buttons.

          

          
Checkpoint: You can win (reach 600 miles), lose by starvation (set start.food to 20 and travel), and lose by time (set deadline to 10). If all three endings work, your engine is finished.

          THINK LIKE A DESIGNER
          
4 · Balance it with arithmetic

          
A game that is impossible is frustrating and one that is trivial is boring. You can check balance before you play, with a back-of-the-envelope calculation: how many days will the trip take, and how much food does that need? Compare the answer with the deadline and the starting supplies.

          

            
            
            
            
            
            
          

          

          
Notice that the starting food is less than the trip needs on purpose. The gap is what hunting, forts and berries are for. A good rule of thumb is to make a careful player's best case about 20% under the deadline, so that events and mistakes use up the slack. Then playtest: set "seed" and ask a friend to play three times while you stay quiet and watch where they get stuck.

Twists that change the rules, not just the pictures

          
    
            
  • Reverse it: the player is the one running the store at the fort and has to decide what to stock.
    • 
            
  • A rival: a second party travels ahead of you and buys out each shop first.
    • 
            
  • Morale: add a new meter. Good news raises it, and a low value triggers bad events.
    • 
            
  • A map: let the player choose between a short dangerous route and a long safe one at forks.
    • 
            
  • Seasons: make event weights depend on the day, so winter is deadlier than summer.
    • 
            
  • Co-op: two players on one keyboard decide each day together or vote on a river crossing.

          YOUR ART
          
6 · Art guide: what you need to draw

          
The Trail Lab uses emoji (🐂 🍖 🛞) as stand-ins so the game works on day one. A finished game replaces them with your own art. Before you open a drawing tool, make a list. Most of the pictures a trail game needs are the same, whatever your world looks like, so here is the whole shopping list.

          
Draw in this order: every Must first (the game can ship with only those), then Should, then Bonus. A finished game with a dozen pictures beats an unfinished one with thirty.


          

            
1280 × 720
Title screen Must
1 picture · The first thing players see: game name, your world, a mood. Leave a quiet area for the Start button.

            
128 × 64
Your vehicle Must
1 sprite · Wagon, rocket, camel, sub. Side view, facing right, transparent background. It slides along the trail every day.

            
960 × 540
Landmark scenes Must
1 per landmark · Fort, mountain pass, city: each stop gets its own picture so players feel the journey. Four landmarks is four scenes.

            
960 × 540
River or hazard scene Must
1 picture · Your river crossing (or asteroid belt, desert, storm). The scariest picture in the game; it shows when the player must choose.

            
960 × 540
Shop scene Should
1 picture · The store where players spend money. Shelves of your three supplies tell them what matters.

            
128 × 128
Traveler portraits Should
1 per traveler · Give each person a face and one detail (hat, glasses, helmet). Players care when someone gets sick.

            
64 × 64
Supply icons Must
1 per resource · Food, spare parts, money. These replace the emoji in the status bar, so they must read clearly when small.

            
600 × 400
Event cards Should
1 per event · Broken wheel, storm, lucky find. Five events is five cards; bad ones can be dark and good ones bright.

            
1280 × 720
Ending screens Must
3 pictures · Win, starved, out of time. Players replay to see them all, so make each different and memorable.

            
1600 × 400
Trail strip Bonus
1 tileable strip · A scrolling landscape behind the vehicle. Left and right edges must match so it repeats without a seam.

            
128 × 128
Tombstone Bonus
1 sprite · Shown where a traveler dies. Players can write a message on it.

            
800 × 600
Route map Bonus
1 picture · The whole journey from start to goal, with each landmark marked.

          


          
How many pictures is your game?

          
Use the numbers from your design sheet. The totals include 1 title, 1 vehicle, 3 endings and 3 supply icons.

          

            
            
            
          

          


          
Rules that make it look like one game

          
    
            
  • Pick a palette first. Five to eight colours for the whole game, written down as hex codes (#b45309). Every picture uses only those. This single rule does the most to make student art look professional.
    • 
            
  • One style. Pixel art, flat shapes or ink and watercolour: choose one and never mix. If your vehicle is pixel art and your scenes are photos, the game looks broken.
    • 
            
  • Same viewpoint. Vehicle and portraits face the same way every time. Scenes all use the same horizon height, so the trail seems to continue from one to the next.
    • 
            
  • Draw big, show small. Make the picture at the size in the cards and check it again at half size. If you can't tell what it is, simplify it. This matters most for icons.
    • 
            
  • Leave room for words. Event and landmark text appears over or beside the picture. Keep the busiest part of the scene away from the bottom third.
    • 
            
  • Transparent or solid? Vehicle, icons, portraits and the tombstone need a transparent background (save as PNG). Scenes and screens fill the whole rectangle.
    • 
            
  • Only your own art. Don't paste in pictures from a search. If you use a photo or asset you didn't make, write down who made it and its license.
    • 
            
  • Draw hazards, not enemies. Show weather, rivers and broken equipment. If real people appear in a real place or time, draw them with respect, never as a monster or a target (see designing with care).
    • 
          


          
Name and save every file

          
Make a folder called art next to your trail.html. Save each picture as a PNG with a plain lowercase name, no spaces: title.png, wagon.png, fort-kearny.png, portrait-ana.png, icon-food.png, event-wheel.png, end-win.png. Keep each file under about 200 KB so the game loads fast.


          
Put it in the game

          
Your art belongs in the GAME data object next to the thing it shows, so the logic stays untouched. Same idea as the rest of the lesson: content in data, logic in code.

          
landmarks: [
  { at: 150, name: "Fort Kearny", type: "shop", art: "art/fort-kearny.png" },
  { at: 400, name: "Platte River", type: "river", art: "art/platte-river.png" }
],
vehicleArt: "art/wagon.png",
resources: {
  food: { label: "Food", icon: "art/icon-food.png", start: 250 }
}

          
Then, in your own trail.html, show the picture when the player arrives. (The Trail Lab above still draws emoji; it doesn't read these art fields.)

          
<img id="scene" alt="" width="480" height="270">

function arrive(landmark) {
  const pic = document.getElementById("scene");
  pic.src = landmark.art;
  pic.alt = landmark.name;      // describe it for screen readers
}

          
Always write the alt. One short sentence about what the picture shows ("A wooden ferry crossing a wide brown river"). It is what a player using a screen reader hears, and it is what shows if a file name is mistyped.

8 · Where the original came from, and designing with care

          
The Oregon Trail began in 1971, when a student teacher named Don Rawitsch and two classmates, Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger, wrote a text game for an eighth-grade history class in Minneapolis. A Minnesota education company later turned it into the Apple II version that filled school computer labs through the 1980s and 1990s. It is one of the most famous educational games ever made, and its core is exactly the loop you just built.

          
It is also a good example of why who is left out of a game matters. In the real history, the trail ran through the homelands of Native nations, and the migration brought disease and conflict. Early versions of the game told that story thinly or badly, and later versions tried to improve it. When you design a spin-off set in a real place or time:

          
    
            
  • Ask whose story the game tells, and whose it skips. A landmark can teach something true, not just be scenery.
    • 
            
  • Don't turn a real group of people into an enemy event that you can shoot. Make hazards come from weather, terrain, supplies and choices.
    • 
            
  • When you write a bit of history on a landmark card, check it with a second source, and say where it came from.
    • 
          

          
Invented worlds (Mars, the deep sea, a fantasy kingdom) are a great way to practise the same design while giving you the freedom to write your own rules.

Key Vocabulary

          

            
State

            
Everything about the game that can change: the day, miles, supplies, each traveler's health.

            
Resource management

            
A game genre about spending limited supplies wisely to reach a goal.

            
Data-driven design

            
Keeping content and numbers in data rather than buried in code, so they can change without rewriting the rules.

            
Weighted random

            
Picking from a list where some items are more likely than others, according to their weights.

            
Seed

            
A starting number that makes a random generator repeat the same sequence, so a bug can be reproduced.

            
Game balance

            
Tuning numbers so a game is challenging but fair.

            
Playtest

            
Watching real players try your game, without helping, to find what confuses or bores them.

            
Spin-off

            
A new game that reuses the structure of an existing one in a different setting or with a new rule.
