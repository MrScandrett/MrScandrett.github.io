The Mathematical Secret Behind the Game

The 20-Question Mind Reader: Claude Shannon, Information Theory & AI

How 20 simple yes/no questions can pinpoint 1 in a million possibilities — and how modern AI uses entropy to make optimal decisions.

1. The 1988 Handheld Miracle: Robin Burgener's 20Q

In 1988, inventor Robin Burgener designed a tiny computer program running on a four-bit microcontroller that played the traditional game of Twenty Questions. By the early 2000s, it was manufactured as the iconic spherical handheld toy 20Q by Radica Games. Millions of players around the world were shocked: they could think of something completely obscure — like a platypus, a telescope, or a stapler — and within 20 questions, the palm-sized plastic orb would reveal their exact thought on its retro liquid-crystal display.

Later, web algorithms like Akinator expanded this concept to millions of fictional and real characters. But neither 20Q nor Akinator possesses supernatural intuition. Instead, they exploit one of the most profound mathematical breakthroughs of the 20th century: Claude Shannon’s Mathematical Theory of Communication, published at Bell Laboratories in 1948.

2. Claude Shannon and the Birth of the “Bit” (1948)

Before 1948, the word information was vague. People spoke of messages having meaning, wisdom, or noise, but there was no mathematical scale to measure how much information a message actually contained.

Claude Shannon realized that information is fundamentally about surprise and uncertainty reduction:

  • If someone tells you, “The Sun will rise in the East tomorrow morning,” they have told you something with near 100% probability. You learned virtually nothing new: zero surprise = zero information.
  • If someone flips a fair coin under a cup and tells you, “It landed on Heads,” you were completely undecided between two equally likely outcomes (50/50). Learning the result gives you exactly one unit of information.

Shannon, collaborating with statistician John Tukey, gave this fundamental unit a name: the binary digit, or bit. One bit is the amount of information required to choose between two equally likely alternatives.

Exponential Scaling

3. Why 20 Questions? The Power of 220

Most people are amazed that 20 questions is enough to guess virtually anything. But the math of binary branching explains why:

220 = 2 × 2 × 2 × … × 2 = 1,048,576

Every time a question splits the remaining possibilities in half, the search space shrinks exponentially:

Distinguishable Items (2N): 1,048,576
Over a million distinct items!

With just 10 questions, an ideal split separates 210 = 1,024 possibilities. With 20 questions, you can theoretically pinpoint one specific object out of over one million possibilities!

The Formula

4. Shannon Entropy: Measuring Uncertainty in Bits

How do we mathematically measure the uncertainty of a system when candidates aren't all equally likely?

Shannon defined the Information Entropy H(X) of a probability distribution P = {p1, p2, …, pn} as:

H(X) = − ∑ pi log2(pi)

pi (Probability)

The current belief or likelihood that item i is the one the user has in mind. The sum of all pi always equals 1.0.

log2(pi) (Bits)

Logarithm base 2 converts probabilities into bits. Because pi ≤ 1, log2(pi) is negative, so the leading minus sign makes entropy positive.

H(X) (Total Entropy)

The weighted average number of bits of surprise remaining. When H = 0, certainty is 100% — we know the answer!

Why the peak at 50/50? If an answer is guaranteed to be Yes (p = 1.0) or No (p = 0.0), asking the question gives you zero new information. The question that teaches the AI the most is always the one that divides the remaining candidates in half!

The AI Algorithm

5. How Decision Trees & Machine Learning Pick the Perfect Split

How the 20Q engine and machine learning algorithms decide what to ask next.

When a machine learning algorithm builds a decision tree (such as ID3, C4.5, CART, or Random Forests), it doesn't choose questions at random. It calculates the Information Gain (IG) for every candidate question:

IG(Q) = Hcurrent − [ P(Yes) · H(X | Yes) + P(No) · H(X | No) ]

The question with the highest IG is the one chosen. In our 20Q engine:

  1. 1Evaluate Candidates: For all 52 questions not yet asked, calculate what the entropy would be if the user said “Yes” versus “No”.
  2. 2Maximize Gain: Pick the question that brings the remaining entropy closest to zero the fastest.
  3. 3Bayesian Update: When the user responds, multiply each candidate's likelihood by their probability and re-normalize the distribution.

Connection to Modern AI

6. How Large Language Models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) Use Information Theory

Information theory is not just for 20 Questions — it is the exact mathematical foundation used to train modern frontier AI.

Cross-Entropy Loss (L)

When training an LLM, the loss function is Cross-Entropy. It measures how many unnecessary bits of surprise the model suffered when trying to guess the next word of human text. Lower entropy means better language understanding.

Perplexity (2H)

In AI research papers, model accuracy is reported in Perplexity, defined as 2H. A perplexity of 16 means the language model is as confused as someone making a blind choice between 16 equally likely words.

Temperature & Entropy

When you set an AI's temperature to 0.0, it always chooses the token with lowest entropy (most predictable). Higher temperature increases the entropy, making responses more creative and varied.
