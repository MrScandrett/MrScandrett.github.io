The Mathematical Secret Behind the Game

1. From Bell Labs (1948) to Robin Burgener’s 20Q

In 1988, inventor Robin Burgener wrote a small neural-network program that played the classic parlor game Twenty Questions. It moved online in the 1990s where player answers continually trained its weights, and in the early 2000s Radica Games turned it into the iconic handheld spherical toy 20Q. Soon after, web systems like Akinator scaled the idea to millions of fictional and historical figures.

Neither 20Q nor Akinator reads minds. Both exploit a profound mathematical framework published at Bell Laboratories in 1948 by Claude Shannon in A Mathematical Theory of Communication: measuring uncertainty in bits (a name suggested by statistician John Tukey) and picking the question that cuts remaining uncertainty the fastest.

Information Theory & Decision Trees

2. Entropy, Information Gain & Fault-Tolerant Bayes

Before 1948, “information” had no unit. Shannon realized information measures surprise and uncertainty reduction: learning that the Sun rose in the east carries virtually zero bits of surprise, whereas uncovering a fair coin toss removes exactly 1 bit because two equally likely possibilities collapse to one.

H(X) = − Σ pi log2(pi)   ·   IG(Q) = Hbefore − [ P(Yes)·H(X|Yes) + P(No)·H(X|No) ]

1. Why 220 = 1,048,576

Each ideal 50/50 question halves the candidate pool. Ten balanced questions distinguish 210 = 1,024 items; twenty distinguish over one million.

2. Why 50/50 Beats 99/1

Decision tree learners like ID3 and C4.5 (and CART / Random Forests using Gini impurity) maximize Information Gain. An unbalanced 1-vs-7 question wastes turns compared to a 4-vs-4 split.

3. Soft Bayesian Updates

Instead of deleting items permanently on a single click, our engine multiplies each prior P(e) by soft likelihoods (0.95 / 0.78 / 0.50 / 0.22 / 0.05) + smoothing ε = 0.005, then re-normalizes. One mistaken clue lowers a candidate’s rank rather than erasing it forever.

Worked Trace A: Balanced 4 / 4 Split

Starting from 8 equal items (H = log2(8) = 3.000 bits), ask “Living organism?” (4 Yes, 4 No):

  1. P(Yes) = 0.5, H(Yes) = log2(4) = 2.000 bits.
  2. P(No) = 0.5, H(No) = log2(4) = 2.000 bits.
  3. Expected entropy after: 0.5(2) + 0.5(2) = 2.000 bits.
  4. Information Gain: 3.000 − 2.000 = 1.000 bit (maximum possible).

Worked Trace B: Unbalanced 1 / 7 Split

From the same 8 items (H = 3.000 bits), ask a narrow clue like “Has a ring around a planet?” (1 Yes, 7 No):

  1. P(Yes) = 1/8, H(Yes) = log2(1) = 0.000 bits.
  2. P(No) = 7/8, H(No) = log2(7) ≈ 2.807 bits.
  3. Expected entropy after: (1/8)(0) + (7/8)(2.807) ≈ 2.456 bits.
  4. Information Gain: 3.000 − 2.456 = 0.544 bits — nearly half a bit slower!
