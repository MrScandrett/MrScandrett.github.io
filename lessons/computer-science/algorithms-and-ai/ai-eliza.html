Inside MIT Project MAC (1966): Real Hardware, Not Sci-Fi AI

Between 1964 and 1966 at MIT’s Project MAC, computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA to run on an IBM 7094 mainframe under the Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS). Users typed on clattering 110-baud electromechanical teletypes (such as the IBM 1050 or Model 33) onto green-bar tractor-feed paper—long before desktop monitors existed.

Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA in MAD-SLIP (Michigan Algorithm Decoder extended with his Symmetric List Processor). Crucially, the program’s engine had no built-in personality or therapy knowledge. Its famous psychiatrist persona lived entirely inside a separate plain-text rule file called DOCTOR. Swap out that script file, as you can in Tab 2 above, and the exact same 1966 machine instantly becomes a pirate captain or a blunt rule debugger.

Why did Weizenbaum pick a Rogerian psychotherapist? Because a nondirective therapist can keep a dialogue flowing simply by turning a client’s own phrasing back into an open question—needing zero real-world facts about weather, politics, or physics.

Worked Machine Traces (Verified Against 1966 DOCTOR)

Walk step by step through two famous executions of Weizenbaum’s exact January 1966 Communications of the ACM script—including the subtle hardware hash behind ELIZA’s “memory.”

Trace A: Precedence Hijack (“I feel that my computer dislikes me.”)

  1. Clause & Scan: No comma or BUT splits the sentence. Pronoun rules swap I → YOU, MY → YOUR, and ME → YOU, yielding YOU FEEL THAT YOUR COMPUTER DISLIKES YOU.
  2. Why FEEL Does Not Win: In DOCTOR_1966, FEEL is defined only as (FEEL DLIST(/BELIEF))—rank 0 with zero templates—so it never enters KEYSTACK!
  3. KEYSTACK Ordering: I (rank 0) enters first, MY (rank 2) jumps above it, and COMPUTER (rank 50) jumps straight to the top: COMPUTER(50), MY(2), I(0).
  4. Wildcard Sweep: COMPUTER’s decomposition rule is simply (0)—absorbing the entire sentence and emitting reply #1: “DO COMPUTERS WORRY YOU”.

Trace B: Turn 3 → Turn 15 Memory (36-Bit BCD SLIP HASH)

  1. Turn 3 Input: “Well, my boyfriend made me come here.” Because WELL has no keyword before the comma, ELIZA skips it and keeps MY BOYFRIEND MADE ME COME HERE.
  2. 36-Bit BCD Hash: When MY(2) fires, ELIZA inspects the last word HERE, pads it to 6 characters (HERE ), packs six 6-bit Hollerith codes into octal 302551256060, squares the 35-bit magnitude, and extracts the middle 2 bits: Form #3.
  3. Silent Storage: It appends “DOES THAT HAVE ANYTHING TO DO WITH THE FACT THAT YOUR BOYFRIEND MADE YOU COME HERE” into the MEMORY queue while printing “YOUR BOYFRIEND MADE YOU COME HERE”.
  4. Turn 15 Callback: Twelve turns later, the user enters “Bullies.” Zero keywords match and the 4-turn LIMIT clock hits 4—popping the stored Turn-3 sentence to create an eerie illusion of long-term empathy!

The ELIZA Effect: 1966 vs. the Age of Modern LLMs

Soon after releasing DOCTOR, Weizenbaum was shaken when his own secretary—someone who had watched him build the rule list—asked him to leave the room so she could speak privately with ELIZA. Practicing psychiatrists even proposed deploying automated ELIZA stations to treat hundreds of patients an hour. Weizenbaum coined his warning in Computer Power and Human Reason (1976): humans possess a powerful instinct to project empathy, intent, and moral agency onto any system that answers in grammatical prose—a habit now known as the ELIZA Effect.

Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) built on Transformer architectures are genuinely light-years beyond ELIZA’s tiny 1966 rule file. Trained across trillions of tokens, they form rich statistical representations of syntax, analogy, code structure, and context across tens of thousands of words. Yet they remain probabilistic next-token predictors, not conscious minds that feel care or verify truth by lived experience. Precisely because an LLM’s grammar is almost indistinguishable from an expert human’s, the ELIZA Effect grows stronger, not weaker: students and adults routinely mistake fluent confidence for factual verification or genuine emotional understanding.

AI & Computing Timeline

  • 1950 — Turing Test: Alan Turing proposes evaluating machine behavior through text conversation.
  • 1964–1966 — ELIZA & DOCTOR: Joseph Weizenbaum builds MAD-SLIP ELIZA on MIT’s IBM 7094 CTSS.
  • 1972 — SHRDLU: Terry Winograd couples natural-language parsing to a virtual Blocks World.
  • 1990s — Statistical NLP: N-gram language models replace brittle hand-written grammars with corpus counts.
  • 2013 — Word2Vec: Mikolov et al. map words into continuous semantic vector spaces.
  • 2017–Present — Transformers: Vaswani et al. introduce self-attention, launching modern instruction-tuned LLMs.
