Walt Disney

Persistence of Vision Synchronized Sound Animator & Studio Founder Theme Park Engineering

Walt Disney (1901–1966) was an American animator, film producer, and entrepreneur who turned a rough new medium — hand-drawn animation — into a technically rigorous art form and a global entertainment business. From synchronized-sound cartoons to the first full-length animated feature to the engineered environments of Disneyland, his career is a case study in how science and showmanship reinforce each other.

Who Was Walt Disney?

Born December 5, 1901 in Chicago, Walt Disney spent part of his childhood on a farm in Marceline, Missouri. The small-town main street he remembered from those years later shaped the design of "Main Street, U.S.A.," the entrance to Disneyland. As World War I drew to a close, a teenage Disney served briefly as an ambulance driver for the Red Cross in Europe.

Back in the United States, Disney started an early animation business, Laugh-O-Gram Studio, in Kansas City. It went bankrupt around 1923, and Disney moved to Hollywood with almost nothing, founding what became the Walt Disney Studio with his brother Roy O. Disney later that year. His early character Oswald the Lucky Rabbit found success — but in 1928, a contract dispute with his distributor cost Disney the legal rights to Oswald and most of his animation staff. It was a painful early business lesson about owning what you create.

Disney responded by creating a new character, Mickey Mouse, who debuted that November in "Steamboat Willie" — one of the first cartoons built around synchronized sound. Nearly a decade later, in 1937, the studio released Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, the first full-length cel-animated feature film in American history. Many in Hollywood expected it to fail; some in the press mockingly called it "Disney's Folly." It was both a technical and financial gamble, and it succeeded, opening the door to Pinocchio, Fantasia, Dumbo, and Bambi in the years that followed. Along the way, Disney's studio pioneered the multiplane camera, a device that photographed layered artwork at different distances to create an illusion of depth in flat, hand-drawn scenes.

In the 1950s Disney expanded into nature documentaries and live-action film, and in July 1955 he opened Disneyland in Anaheim, California — a theme park built with then-novel attention to immersive "themed" environments, sightlines, and crowd flow, drawing on ideas from engineering and urban design as much as from entertainment. Before his death he had begun planning what became Walt Disney World in Florida, along with an experimental planned community concept known as EPCOT. Walt Disney died December 15, 1966, in Los Angeles, at age 65, from lung cancer.

The Technology of Animation

Animation works because of a quirk of human perception called persistence of vision (closely related to the phi phenomenon): when the eye and brain are shown a rapid sequence of still images, they perceive continuous motion instead of a series of separate pictures. Each drawing in a cartoon differs only slightly from the one before it, and shown quickly enough in sequence, the brain fills in the gaps as smooth movement.

How quickly is "quickly enough"? Sound film settled on a standard frame rate of 24 frames per second — 24 individual still images flashed on screen every second. Slower rates make motion look jerky or flickery; 24 fps became the industry standard because it was fast enough to look smooth to the eye while still being efficient to shoot and project.

24 × 1 = 24
24 frames/second × 1 second of animation
= 24 individually drawn images

A single second of classic hand-drawn animation could require two dozen separate drawings — a big part of why a feature-length film like Snow White took years and hundreds of animators to complete.

The Multiplane Camera

Disney's studio also solved a problem no amount of clever drawing alone could fix: flat, hand-drawn scenes look flat. The multiplane camera stacked layers of artwork — background, midground, foreground — on separate panes of glass, each spaced at a different distance from the camera lens. As the camera moved toward the scene, closer layers shifted position faster than distant layers, mimicking parallax: the same effect that makes roadside trees appear to whip past a moving car while distant mountains barely seem to move. The result was 2D animation with a real sense of depth.

Why did Snow White seem like such a big risk in 1937?
No studio had ever attempted a full-length cel-animated feature. It required roughly 1.5 million drawings, cost far more than a typical cartoon short, and many in Hollywood assumed audiences wouldn't sit through 83 minutes of animation — hence the mocking nickname "Disney's Folly." Its success proved animated features could be both artistically serious and commercially viable.
Was 24 fps always the standard?
Silent films were often shot and shown at lower, more variable frame rates. Once film needed to carry synchronized sound in the late 1920s, a fixed rate was required to keep picture and audio in sync, and 24 frames per second became the standard that has persisted in cinema ever since.

Disney's Key Contributions

🎣
Steamboat Willie (1928)

Debuted Mickey Mouse and helped popularize synchronized-sound animation, timing music and sound effects precisely to on-screen action.

🎥
Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937)

The first full-length cel-animated feature film in American history — a technical and financial risk that reshaped what animation could be.

📷
The Multiplane Camera

Layered artwork at different distances from the lens to create a parallax illusion of depth in flat, hand-drawn scenes.

🏢
Founding a Major Studio

Built the Walt Disney Studio with brother Roy O. Disney into a lasting film and entertainment company.

🌡️
Disneyland (1955)

Engineered an immersive theme park around themed environments, controlled sightlines, and deliberate crowd flow.

🐢
True-Life Adventures

Produced an Academy Award-winning series of nature documentaries, expanding the studio beyond animation and fiction film.

Disney's Legacy Today

Disney's willingness to bet his studio's finances on the technical risk of Snow White changed what audiences and investors believed animated film could be — a serious, feature-length art form rather than a short novelty before the main picture. That bet cleared the path for nearly a century of animated features that followed, from other studios as well as his own.

Disneyland's design principles — themed lands, controlled sightlines, engineered crowd flow, and an obsessive attention to guest experience — became the template that theme parks worldwide still follow. The company Disney co-founded grew from a small Kansas City animation studio into one of the largest media and entertainment companies in the world, spanning film, television, theme parks, and consumer products.

Practice Problems

Think about animation technology, film history, and Disney's life.

Easy1. In what year did Mickey Mouse debut in "Steamboat Willie"?

Hint: This was the same year Disney lost the rights to his earlier character, Oswald the Lucky Rabbit.

Easy2. What was the first full-length cel-animated feature film in American history?

Hint: Released in 1937, some in the press mockingly called this project "Disney's Folly" before it succeeded.

Medium3. Standard sound film runs at 24 frames per second. How many individually drawn frames are needed for a 10-second animated sequence at that rate?

Hint: Multiply frames per second by the number of seconds: 24 × 10.

Medium4. What perceptual effect explains why a rapid sequence of still images looks like continuous motion?

Hint: It's closely related to what's called the phi phenomenon.

Challenge5. What effect did the multiplane camera create by moving layered art panels at different speeds relative to the camera?

Hint: Think about how closer objects appear to move faster than distant ones when you look out a car window.

Medium6. Snow White required roughly 1.5 million drawings. If a team of animators could complete 500 drawings a day, roughly how many days would it take to draw that many frames (rounded to the nearest thousand days)?

Hint: Divide 1,500,000 by 500, then round to the nearest thousand.

Easy7. What theme park did Walt Disney open in Anaheim, California in July 1955?

Hint: This was the first Disney theme park, built years before Walt Disney World in Florida.

Challenge8. What early business setback in 1928 taught Disney a lasting lesson about owning what he created?

Hint: A distributor dispute cost him the rights to a character he had created, prompting him to create Mickey Mouse soon after.
