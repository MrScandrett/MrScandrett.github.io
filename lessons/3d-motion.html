Physics · Lesson 17

3D Motion

Kinematics Vector Decomposition Projectile Motion Trajectories

In the real world, motion happens in three dimensions simultaneously. A basketball arcs through the air while spinning; a satellite orbits while drifting; a billiard ball bounces off walls while rolling. Understanding position, velocity, and acceleration as 3D vectors is the foundation of mechanics, aerospace, and robotics.

Who Was Isaac Newton?

Isaac Newton (1643–1727) published Principia Mathematica in 1687, establishing that the same laws of motion work in all directions simultaneously. His three laws of motion and the universal law of gravitation — combined with the calculus he invented to describe them — form the bedrock of classical mechanics. For the first time, motion of planets, cannonballs, and falling apples could all be described by a single unified framework.

Core Concepts

Position, Velocity, and Acceleration as Vectors

r = (x, y, z)  |  v = dr/dt  |  a = dv/dt
r = position vector (m)
v = velocity vector (m/s)
a = acceleration vector (m/s²)

Projectile Motion Equations

In projectile motion (no air resistance), gravity acts only in the vertical (z) direction. Horizontal components are constant.

z(t) = z₀ + vz·t − ½g·t²
x(t) = x₀ + vx·t (constant)
y(t) = y₀ + vy·t (constant)
z(t) = vertical, affected by gravity g = 9.8 m/s²

Range Equation

R = v₀² · sin(2θ) / g
R = horizontal range (m)
v₀ = initial speed (m/s)
θ = launch angle above horizontal
Maximum range at θ = 45°

Real-World Applications

🚀
Ballistics & Aerospace Artillery trajectories and rocket launch windows require precise 3D vector calculations accounting for Earth's rotation.
⚾

🤖

🛸
Spacecraft Navigation Orbital mechanics and gravitational slingshots rely on 3D trajectory calculations to navigate between planets.

Practice Problems

Apply kinematic equations in 3D. Use g = 9.8 m/s².

Easy1. In projectile motion (no air resistance), gravity acts in which direction?

Hint: Gravity always points downward — the vertical (z) direction.

Easy2. For projectile motion with no air resistance, the horizontal velocity component is:

Hint: No horizontal force acts (no air resistance), so horizontal velocity stays constant.

Medium3. A ball is launched at 20 m/s at 45° above horizontal. What is the initial vertical velocity component in m/s? (1 decimal)

Hint: vz = v₀ × sin(45°) = 20 × 0.707 ≈ 14.1 m/s

Medium4. At what launch angle (degrees) is the horizontal range maximised (no air resistance)?

Hint: R = v₀²·sin(2θ)/g — maximised when sin(2θ)=1, i.e. 2θ=90°, θ=45°.

Challenge5. A projectile is launched at 30 m/s at 30°. What is the maximum height in metres? (1 decimal) [h = (v₀sinθ)²/2g]

Hint: vz = 30×sin(30°) = 15 m/s. h = 15²/(2×9.8) = 225/19.6 ≈ 11.5 m
