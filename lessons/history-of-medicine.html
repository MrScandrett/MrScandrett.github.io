The History of Medicine

For most of human history, doctors could describe illness far better than they could cure it. Healers reasoned from the best systems of knowledge available to them — spirits, humors, and authority — long before anyone could see a microbe. Medicine's real turning points came not from new drugs but from new ways of testing ideas: dissection instead of ancient texts, controlled comparison instead of tradition, and germs instead of guesswork.

Big Question

How did healing become a science?

Medicine is a case study in how a whole field can be wrong, confidently, for centuries — and how it slowly replaced authority with evidence.

Core Tension

Every era's doctors believed they had the answer. The four humors, miasma, and germ theory all explained the same symptoms differently. What actually changed was not confidence — it was the willingness to test ideas against reality.

Key Ideas

What Changed Medicine From Guesswork to Science

1. Look, Don't Just Read

For 1,400 years, doctors trusted Galen's ancient texts over their own eyes. Vesalius dissected real bodies in 1543 and found Galen had never actually dissected a human.

2. Compare, Don't Just Guess

James Lind's 1747 citrus trial on sailors with scurvy was one of the first controlled experiments in medicine — testing several treatments side by side instead of trusting tradition.

3. Trace the Cause, Not Just the Symptom

Germ theory finally explained why washing hands, boiling water, and sterilizing instruments worked — something miasma ("bad air") theory could never fully account for.

Case Study

John Snow and the Broad Street Pump

In 1854, a cholera outbreak was killing residents of London's Soho district. The dominant theory blamed "miasma," or bad-smelling air. Physician John Snow instead mapped every case by hand and noticed they clustered around one water pump on Broad Street. Try removing the pump handle and watch what happens to new cases.

Case Study

Ignaz Semmelweis and the Doctors Who Wouldn't Wash Their Hands

A few years before Snow's map, Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that mothers were dying of childbed fever far more often in wards run by doctors who had just come from autopsies than in wards run by midwives. He proposed that doctors were carrying "cadaverous particles" on their hands. Requiring handwashing with a chlorine solution cut deaths dramatically — years before germ theory existed to explain why.

Rejected by his own profession

Many doctors were offended by the idea that they themselves were spreading disease. Semmelweis's evidence was ignored for decades.

Semmelweis argued that childbed fever was caused by "decomposed animal organic matter" carried on the hands of doctors and students — and that a simple wash could prevent it. His own colleagues largely dismissed him. Germ theory would not be accepted for another generation, and Semmelweis died in 1865 without seeing his idea vindicated.

That is the deeper History, Civilization & Power question here: evidence alone does not automatically change a system. It takes time, replication, and often a generational shift before a society's institutions accept an uncomfortable truth — even when lives depend on it.

Two Ways to Decide What's True

Authority-based medicine

  • Trust ancient texts (Galen, Hippocrates) over direct observation.
  • Explain illness with unseen forces: humors, miasma, curses.
  • Treat every patient with the same standard remedy, such as bloodletting.

Evidence-based medicine

  • Test ideas with dissection, controlled trials, and statistics.
  • Trace disease to specific, verifiable causes like bacteria and viruses.
  • Update treatment when new evidence contradicts old assumptions.

Discussion Prompts

Use these questions to connect the history of medicine to how we evaluate claims and evidence today.

Why did humoral theory last 2,000 years? It matched some symptoms, came from respected authorities, and had no easy way to be disproven at the time. What makes a wrong idea feel convincing?
Why did doctors resist handwashing? Semmelweis's evidence was strong, but his colleagues rejected it. What makes it hard for experts to accept that their own profession is causing harm?
What would a John Snow of today look like? Snow solved an outbreak with a hand-drawn map before germ theory existed. Where today could patient observation and data outrun the current scientific consensus?
