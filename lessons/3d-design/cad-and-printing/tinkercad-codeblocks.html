Tinkercad Codeblocks: Shapes from Code

Variables Loops Extrude Ring Stack Locked Star Fidget

In ordinary Tinkercad you drag every shape by hand. In Codeblocks, which Autodesk added to Tinkercad in 2019, you snap together blocks of code and the code builds the shapes. That changes what a design is: not a pile of shapes but a recipe. Change one number in the recipe and the whole form rebuilds itself. That's parametric design, and today you'll use it to build a stack of rings, then lock the rings together so the stack becomes a fidget that stays in one piece when you pick it up.

Why Design with Code?

Picture a stack of ten rings, each a little smaller than the one below. By hand that's ten shapes to place, size and line up. Now your teacher says, "Make it twelve rings, and a bit taller." By hand you start over. With code you change two numbers.

Architects and engineers worked this way long before computers. Antoni Gaudí found the shapes of his arches with hanging chains: move one weight and the whole model rebalanced itself. Gustave Eiffel controlled 18,038 iron parts from one set of wind calculations. Parametric CAD does the same thing on a screen: you write the rules, and the computer draws the result.

Key idea: a parameter is a number your design depends on. A parametric design is one where you can change the parameters and get a correct new design, without redrawing anything.

Three Ideas: Variables, Loops, Extrude

1 · Variables: names for numbers

A variable is a labelled box holding a number. In Codeblocks you use Create variable to make one (say, radius) and Set or Change by to update it. Any shape block can use the variable instead of a typed number, so when the variable changes, every shape that uses it changes too.

2 · Loops: do it again, a little differently

A loop repeats the blocks inside it. Repeat 10 times does the same thing ten times. Count with i from 1 to 10 also hands you a counter, i, that goes up each time, so each repeat can be different: move up by i × height, or shrink by i × 2 mm. One loop and a counter can place a hundred shapes.

3 · Extrude: from a flat outline to a solid

Most 3D shapes start as a flat 2D outline that is pushed straight up to a height. That push is called an extrusion. A circle extruded becomes a cylinder. A ring (a circle with a smaller circle cut out of it) extruded becomes a tube. In Codeblocks you don't press an Extrude button; a shape's height setting is its extrusion. Change the height and the same outline grows taller or flatter.

Lock It: Why Ring Stacks Fall Apart

Try the ring stack in your hands, in your head. Lift the outer ring and the rest stay on the table. Nothing joins them. A star fidget built that way, eight star rings nested with a gap between each, is just a pile of loose rings. Worse, the thin inner stars can pop out of the print altogether.

A fidget needs the opposite: parts that can move and can't leave. Engineers call that capture. A part is captured when, to get out, it would have to squeeze through an opening narrower than itself. The fix is a ridge on each ring that sits inside a groove in the next ring out.

Four rules that make it work

  1. The ridge is deeper than the gap. If the ridge sticks out 2 mm and the gap is 1 mm, the ridge can't squeeze through the 1 mm mouth. Make the ridge shallower than the gap and the rings slide apart; the lab will tell you.
  2. Slopes, never shelves. A printer can't build a flat ledge in mid-air. A wall that leans at 45° or less prints with no support. So the ridge climbs and falls in small steps of 0.5 mm out for 0.5 mm up. Write that as a loop.
  3. Everything sits on the bed. Every ring starts at height 0, so the whole fidget prints in place in one go. Each ring out is a bit taller than the last, and that extra height is how far the rings can slide.
  4. Leave a real air gap. About 0.5 mm of clear space between neighbours. Smaller and the printer fuses them into one lump; much bigger and the fidget rattles.

Then drag lock steps down to 0, watch the red falls apart message, and use the lift test slider on a locked design to see the whole stack come up with the outer ring.

Why a star? Round rings can spin; star rings can't, because the points catch. A star fidget slides up and down in clicks instead. A star's flat sides sit about 0.7 of the way out to its points, so the lab reports the air gap measured across a side, which is the one that counts.

Build It in Tinkercad Codeblocks

Sign in to Tinkercad and choose Create → Codeblocks, then start a blank design. Blocks live in drawers on the left (Shapes, Modifiers, Control, Math, Data); drag them into the workspace in the middle and press ▶ Run to watch them build. Block names can differ slightly between versions; look for the closest match.

  1. Make the variables. From Data, use Create variable three times: rings, radius, height. Add Set blocks to give them values (try 8, 30, 5).
  2. Add the loop. From Control, drag Count with i from 1 to … by 1, and put the rings variable in the "to" slot.
  3. Add a tube inside the loop. From Shapes, drag a Tube into the loop. Put radius in its radius slot and height in its height slot; set the wall to 3. (No Tube? Use a Cylinder, then a smaller Cylinder set to hole, and group them.)
  4. Move it up. From Modifiers, add Move after the tube and set its z to (i − 1) × height, built from the Math blocks.
  5. Shrink for next time. From Data, add Change radius by −3 as the last block in the loop.
  6. Run it. Press ▶. Then change only the Set blocks and run again. That's parametric design.
  7. Export. Use Tinkercad's export to download an STL, or send it to a regular Tinkercad design to add a base or a name. Check it in a slicer before printing (see 3D Printing).
Debugging tip: if every ring lands in the same place, your Move is outside the loop or isn't using i. If the rings get bigger instead of smaller, check the sign on Change radius by. If they merge into a solid lump, the radius has shrunk smaller than the wall.
Print it and you'll find out: these rings are not joined. Lift one and the rest stay behind. To make a fidget that stays in one piece, go on to Lock It and Build the Star Fidget.

Build the Star Fidget in Codeblocks

The ring stack used one loop. The fidget uses three small programs that call each other: a slab (one star tube, a thin slice), a ring (a stack of slabs that climbs, runs straight, then steps back in) and the main loop that makes ring after ring. Use Define Template for the first two, then Create from Template to call them. Templates share your variables, so set a variable before you call one. The lab above, in Locked fidget mode, shows this exact program with your numbers filled in.

  1. Make the variables. rings 6, radius 14, wall 3, flat 2, run 0.7, lock 4, plus three the templates use to keep their place: r, z, thick. For round rings use run 0.5. run is how much the radius grows per 0.5 mm slab; for a star it is 0.7 so the sloping side still leans at 45°.
  2. Define Template slab. Add a Star (Sides 8, Radius r, Inner Radius(%) 0.76, H thick) then Move Z-axis min to z. Add a second Star the same way but set to hole, Radius r − wall, and move it to the same z. Group Selection. Finish with Change z by thick, so the next slab lands on top. That is the star-and-hole trick from your first star, now one slice thick.
  3. Define Template ring. Set r to radius, Set z to 0, Set thick to 0.5. Then Repeat lock Times: Create from Template slab, Change r by run. This climbs the slope outward. Next Set thick to flat, Create from Template slab: the straight middle. Then Set thick to 0.5 and Repeat lock Times: Change r by −run, Create from Template slab. This steps back in. End with Group Selection to join the slabs into one ring.
  4. The main loop. Set the variables, then Count with i from 1 to rings: Create from Template ring, then Change radius by wall + gap (4.5 for the star) and Change flat by 1. Each ring out is wider by one wall plus one gap, and taller by 1 mm. Don't group the rings together: they must stay separate parts.
  5. Test small first. This builds about a hundred shapes, so set rings to 3 and press ▶ Run. Tinkercad will think for a few seconds. Raise it to 6 once you like what you see.
  6. Check the design. In the lab, the same numbers must say locked and show an air gap of about 0.5 mm. In Tinkercad, orbit to a side view: each ring's middle bulges outward and the next ring bulges around it.
  7. Export and slice. Download the STL (or export from Tinkercad), put it flat on the bed exactly as modelled, and slice with 0.2 mm layers, no supports, and no brim. In the layer preview every ring should be its own outline with a clear line between neighbours.
When it prints stuck: if the rings are fused all the way up, raise gap by 0.2 and print again. If they are stuck only at the very bottom, that's the first layer squashing out; turn on elephant-foot compensation in the slicer or snap them loose with a gentle twist and push. If the fidget rattles loudly, lower gap by 0.1. See Print Fidgets for how to test a clearance with a comb before you commit.
Fix-it list for a "non-final" star fidget: (1) Rings that only nest are loose; give each one a ridge and groove. (2) A 2 mm gap rattles; aim for 0.5 mm of air. (3) Thin walls on small inner stars can come out of a print; keep wall the same on every ring, and the lab warns you when the innermost ring gets too small. (4) Mirroring two halves leaves rings floating in mid-air at print time; build upward from the bed instead.

The Pi Inside Every Ring

Every ring is circle math. The distance around the outside of a ring is its circumference, C = 2πr. The flat ring outline has area πR² − πr², the big circle minus the hole. Extrude it by height h and the volume is that area times h. The lab above uses exactly these formulas to tell you how much plastic your design needs (PLA weighs about 1.24 g per cm³). For more on π, visit the Pi lesson.

V = π(R² − r²)·h
R = outer radius · r = R − wall = inner radius
h = extrusion height

A ring with R = 30 mm, wall 3 mm and h = 5 mm: π(900 − 729)·5 ≈ 2,686 mm³ ≈ 2.7 cm³.

Practice Problems

Easy1. A loop runs Count with i from 1 to 6. How many rings does it make?

Hint: i takes the values 1, 2, 3, …

Easy2. Pushing a flat outline straight up to a height to make a solid is called…

Hint: Toothpaste squeezed from a tube keeps its outline as it gets longer.

Medium3. Each ring is 5 mm tall and Move sets z to (i − 1) × 5. At what height (mm) does ring 4 start?

Hint: (4 − 1) × 5.

Medium4. Radius starts at 30 mm and changes by −3 each loop. What is the radius of ring 5?

Hint: Ring 1 is 30. The change happens after each ring, so ring 5 has had 4 changes.

Medium5. What is the circumference of a 20 mm radius ring, to the nearest mm?

Hint: C = 2 × π × 20.

Challenge6. In Concentric mode with a 3 mm wall, what should change radius by be so the rings just touch with no gap and no overlap?

Hint: The next ring's inside edge should sit exactly on this ring's outside edge.

Challenge7. What makes a design parametric?

Hint: Think about what happened when you moved the rings slider.

Easy8. A ridge sticks out 2 mm and the gap between rings is 1 mm. Will the inner ring lift out?

Hint: Compare 2 mm with 1 mm. Capture means the opening is narrower than the part.

Medium9. Each slab is 0.5 mm tall. A ring climbs for 4 slabs, runs straight for 2 mm, then steps back in for 4 slabs. How tall is the ring (mm)?

Hint: 4 × 0.5 up, plus 2 straight, plus 4 × 0.5 down.

Medium10. The main loop does Change flat by 1 each time round. Ring 1 is 6 mm tall. How tall is ring 6 (mm)?

Hint: Ring 6 has had 5 changes of +1.

Challenge11. In the star fidget code, wall is 3 and the gap is 1.5, both typed as numbers out to the star's points. The main loop says Change radius by wall + gap. What is that change (mm)? (Across a flat side the true sizes are only about 0.7 times as big, but the code uses the point numbers.)

Hint: Add the wall and the gap. The 0.7 only changes what you measure across a side, not what you type.
