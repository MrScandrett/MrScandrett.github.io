Study the makers

  • Ancient Greece. A neck jar has a distinct break where the shoulder meets the neck. Spin the Greek neck jar study and make that transition stronger by collaring the upper wall. Terracotta neck-amphora at The Met.
  • Joseon Korea. Potters joined two separately thrown porcelain halves to make a moon jar. In the Joseon moon jar study, compare the broad belly with the narrow foot and rim. It starts with the halves already joined. Moon jar at The Met.
  • San Ildefonso Pueblo, 20th century. Maria and Julian Martinez became known for black-on-black pottery with matte and polished areas. Build up the Coil bowl base, blend the seams, and choose Burnished clay to study the difference in sheen. National Park Service, Bandelier.

Jōmon pottery · What was for dinner?

A broken pot can keep two kinds of evidence. On the outside, a potter could press twisted cord into soft clay. That pattern gives Jōmon its name, “cord-marked.” On the inside, burnt food sometimes survives. Researchers analyzed fats in early pots from Japan and found traces of aquatic animals. People were cooking their catch in pottery thousands of years before farming spread through Japan.

Build a cooking vessel. Choose Coil bowl base, press Start form, then use Add coil to raise the walls. View it from above. Could you reach inside to stir? Compare your model with the linked cord-marked pot. Which clues could you recover from a drawing of the rim, and which would need a lab?

What can a pot tell us?

Imagine finding only the rim of this vessel. You could draw its curve, estimate the opening, and compare its profile with other rims. A piece of painted wall might preserve a horse but give you little evidence for the height of the neck. Archaeologists call a broken piece of pottery a sherd.

Working with groups of objects from Egyptian graves, Flinders Petrie arranged groups in a relative order by comparing the pottery types they contained. His sequence numbers told him which groups probably came earlier or later. A calendar year required another clue. Try it with three studio vessels: sketch their rims, trade sketches, and see which features actually distinguish the pots.

Public-domain field reading: Flinders Petrie, Methods and Aims in Archaeology (1904). See “Arranging,” especially sequence dating.

Two ways to make a surface speak

These vessels were once known as daeho, large jars for practical use. The poetic name “moon jar” came later. In the studio, start with the moon jar and sketch its silhouette before you change it. Would making both sides perfectly even strengthen the resemblance to the moon?

In San Ildefonso Pueblo, ancient pottery recovered in excavations helped prompt a different experiment. Maria and Julian Martinez worked out how to produce deep black surfaces. Maria shaped and polished pots, and Julian painted designs. Restricting oxygen during firing helped blacken the vessel. Gloss beside matte lets a design appear when the viewer turns the pot toward light. National Park Service account of Maria Martinez.

Compare the ingredients. Water lets clay particles slide as a potter pulls a wall. A slip is clay suspended in water. A glaze forms a glassy coating in the kiln. Burnishing smooths the clay surface by rubbing it. Which could leave an unglazed pot shiny? In the studio, compare the same shape with two finishes under the same light.

Studio challenge

  1. Throw a vessel. Choose Wheel lump and start the wheel. Touch the lump with Center, then Open. Use Pull wall and drag upward near the side. Move right to widen, or left to bring the wall in.
  2. Build another by hand. Stop the wheel. Start with a pinch cup and work the rim, or build height on the coil bowl base one coil at a time. Smooth the raised seams with the rib.
  3. Trim and fire. Compare both vessels from above in Orbit. Firm one to leather-hard and trim its foot on the wheel. Pick a finish, dry it, and fire it. Compare the wet and fired heights.

The firing preview uses a fixed shrinkage estimate for each clay body and previews the chosen finish. Actual results depend on the clay, glaze and firing process.

Take your model out of the studio

  • Keep editing. Save a .clay project to keep your geometry, clay body, and firing stage. Open it here to continue.
  • Bring in your own mesh. Open a closed STL, OBJ, 3MF, or self-contained GLB. The studio turns it into editable clay you can add to, scoop, and smooth. Imported surfaces lose textures and may lose very thin details.
  • Print a study. Export STL and open it in your slicer. Confirm the size, thin walls, and any supports with the layer preview.
  • Make a game prop. Export GLB for a Y-up model in metres with its clay color and finish, or OBJ for geometry you can texture yourself.
