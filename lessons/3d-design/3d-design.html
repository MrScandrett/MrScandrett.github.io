First project

Make a desk helper

Design something that holds one school item: a pencil, a note, a book, or a phone. Your object needs a stable base and an opening, slot, or raised edge that does the holding.

Before you build

Pick one item: a pencil, note, book, or phone. Use paper and cardboard, or open a blank model in Workbench. A printer is optional.

Your goal: the item goes in and out easily, and the holder stays upright.

See the item, opening, and base together

This pencil helper is one possible answer. Use it to understand the base and opening. Measure your own item, build a quick version, and change what the test reveals.

Compare real stationery from two views

  1. Choose and measure

    Measure the part of the item your holder will touch. Write the size in millimeters (mm). If you do not have a ruler, trace the item and label the sketch “not measured.”

    Keep: the item name and one measurement or tracing.

  2. Sketch, then build

    Draw a top view and a front view. Add a base and an opening, slot, or edge. Build a quick version from cardboard or simple 3D shapes.

    Keep: your sketch and first model. Save an editable digital file before exporting.

  3. Test the fit and base

    Put the item in, take it out, and watch for tipping. With a digital-only model, compare the measured sizes and inspect two views. Label your result “preview only” until you test a physical version.

    Keep: a photo, screenshot, or short note about what happened.

  4. Change one thing and retest

    Fix one problem: widen the base, resize the opening, or change the height. Repeat the same test with the same item.

    Keep: both versions and one sentence: “I changed ___ because the first test ___.”

What should I show for my grade?

Grades 6–8

Show two views, one measured size, your first version, and your improved version. Explain what your test helped you change.

Grades 9–12 · Add a measurable target

Write a target before testing, such as “holds my phone upright through five removals.” Record the result for both versions and explain the tradeoff you made.

Need a different way to explain? Use a labeled drawing, photos with captions, or a spoken explanation. Include the same plan, test, and change.

Choose a test before you build

Pick one item from your desk. Measure it or trace around it, then decide what a successful holder must do. Record the first result and one change; a labeled drawing or spoken explanation can record your result.

  • Fit: Can the item enter and leave without forcing it?
  • Stability: Does the helper stay upright when the item is added and removed?
  • Use: Can someone find and reach the item easily?

Design record: “My first version ___ when ___. I changed ___ so that ___.” Test the revised version with the same item.

Words you will use

Six useful 3D design words
Model
A physical or digital version of your idea.
Prototype
A quick first version you build to test an idea.
CAD
Computer-aided design: making a model with measured shapes on a computer.
Clearance
The empty gap between parts that need to fit or move.
Slicer
Software that turns a 3D model into layers and printer instructions.
Render
An image made from a 3D model using a camera, surfaces, and lights.

Shared skills

Learn to see and change a model

Stuck on a step? Open the workshop for that skill. Practice on its sample, then return to your own desk helper. You do not need to finish every workshop before building.

  1. Inspect three views

    Practice orbiting the sample, then show the front, side, and top of your helper.

  2. Draw and measure

    Sketch your helper from top and front; add one measurement. Use the drafting studio if you want a measured digital drawing.

  3. Change one size

    Practice on the sample block, then resize your helper and save or photograph both versions.

  4. Make an opening, if needed

    Practice cutting the sample wall. Add a slot or pocket to your own helper only if the design needs it.

Ready for a route: Show your helper from two views, identify one size, and explain what changed after testing. Reopen a digital project if you made one.

Physical objects

Make it real: a physical object

Design a measured object, inspect its export, and plan a print. A printer is optional: a slicer preview and a proposed test count as planning evidence.

Use the measured drawing you made in the shared skills.

  1. Build in Forge

    Save the editable model. Export an STL (a file describing the outside shape), open it in the slicer, and check one size against your plan.

  2. Prepare the print

    Compare orientations, support needs, and bed contact in the slicer. Use the teacher-approved printer and material profile.

Optional extension: moving parts

Test the clearance for a fidget before designing parts that move.

Finish when: The imported size matches your plan and you can explain a print decision using a preview or test result.

Printer handoff: A teacher runs the printer and handles its hot nozzle, heated bed, tools, and finished part until it cools. Students prepare and discuss the design, then follow classroom equipment rules.

Game-ready objects

Make it play: a game object

Build reusable objects that fit a game world, then test the export in its destination. Game Asset Studio has the full project briefs.

  1. Create textures

    Make a small material set with a consistent visual style.

  2. Build a prop

    Reuse a chair or other object you already modeled.

  3. Assemble a room

This quarter's challenge · The Trio

Moves · Useful · Likeness: show all three

Your quarter project must show movement, a useful job, and a recognizable shape. You can use one, two, or three objects. Each trait must appear at least once. Start this project when your teacher assigns it; the desk helper is practice for the useful-tool trait.

Trait 1 · Moves, and stays in one piece

A hinge, an axle, a ball joint, a chain of articulated segments, a captive spinner or a sliding drawer. Print it already assembled, or with parts captured so they can't come out: no glue, screws or snap-on extras.

Success test: It moves 20 times by hand and nothing separates.

Start with: Design a moving fit · Forge hinge, captured ring, wheel and axle, gears

Trait 2 · A useful tool

It does a real job for a real person: holds, measures, opens, sorts, winds, dispenses or protects. A tool needs a stable base, a working feature (a slot, cavity, hook or spool) and enough strength for the job.

Success test: A named user does the job with it, and it doesn't tip, bend or drop what it holds.

Start with: the desk helper · Forge phone stand, cable clip, box

Trait 3 · In the likeness of something

Make it look like an animal, person, vehicle, or plant. First get the outline (silhouette) and the relative sizes of the parts right. Add small details after the shape is recognizable.

Success test: Show the outline to a classmate without saying its name. Record what they recognize; aim for an answer within three seconds. A spoken description or tactile model can be used with your teacher.

Start with: In the Likeness: the Marble Studio · Sculpt a surface · Workbench

Stretch · All three in one object

A turtle tape dispenser whose shell hinges open. An owl pencil cup with a turning lid. A rover that winds a cable as its wheels roll. One design that does all three is harder to get right, so test the moving part first.

Success test: It passes all three tests above.

Start with: Forge rolling creature tool

Plan before you print

Your Trio plan

Name your first object. Check the traits you plan to include. Add a second or third object only if you need one. Checking a trait makes a plan; you still need to build and test it.

Trio evidence for grades 6–8 and 9–12

Grades 6–8

Keep your Trio plan, design files, and one test result for each trait. For moving parts, test a small gap sample with your teacher before printing the full object.

Grades 9–12

Also record the gap dimensions, printer and material settings, and the result of 20 movement cycles. Test the tool with its user. Explain one design change using your results.

Printing moving parts? Check these settings with your teacher

Keep the moving space empty

A slicer turns the model into printer instructions. Use the classroom's approved printer and material profile, then inspect the layer preview together.

  • Gap: Print a Clearance Comb first. Record which gap moves freely. There is no one gap size that works for every printer and material.
  • Supports and brim: Look for added material that bridges moving parts. Ask your teacher to block or adjust it while keeping the rest of the print supported.
  • Strength: Inspect the walls around pins and axles. Choose walls and infill with your teacher for the force the part must carry.
  • First layer: A spread-out first layer can close a gap. Check the printer profile's first-layer compensation with your teacher.

Record: printer, material, tested gap, orientation, supports, bed adhesion, walls, and infill. Save the slicer preview with your plan.

Reference: Prusa's guide to fit and clearance. Continue to the moving-parts print worksheet.

Optional: how 3D design developed

How we got here

From drawings to moving 3D worlds

Designers have long used drawings and physical models to test an idea before building it. Computers made those models editable, repeatable, and eventually movable. These milestones connect the tools in this pathway to the people who developed them.

  1. 1963

    Draw and revise on a screen

    Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad let a person draw with a light pen and change structured drawings. That idea still lives in CAD: adjust a dimension, then inspect the result. Computer History Museum: Sketchpad

  2. 1972

    Make a form move

    At the University of Utah, Ed Catmull and Fred Parke built polygon models of a hand and faces, then calculated moving views with shaded surfaces. A 3D object could be animated from changing poses rather than redrawn from every angle. Computer History Museum: early 3D animation

  3. 1975

    Model a familiar object

    Martin Newell turned a teapot into 3D coordinate data. Researchers reused it to test surfaces, lighting, and rendering. Today you also begin with a simple object, then change how it is modeled or shown. Computer History Museum: Utah Teapot

  4. 1983

    Turn a digital design into a part

    Chuck Hull made an early 3D-printed part using stereolithography, which hardens liquid resin with light. The classroom's filament printer uses a different process, but both build from a digital model one layer at a time. 3D Systems: early printing history

  5. 1986

    Give a model character

    Pixar's Luxo Jr. used animated desk lamps to tell a story through movement and timing. A model's shape matters, but its poses can communicate just as much. Pixar: Luxo Jr.

  6. 1995

    Build an entire feature in CG

    Toy Story became the first feature-length computer-animated film. Modeling, materials, lighting, animation, and rendering had to work together across a whole story. Pixar: studio history

  7. 2002

    Share the tools

    Blender became open source. More learners could study and change a complete 3D creation tool, and use it for modeling, animation, and rendering. Blender Foundation: history

Discuss: Which step made it possible to revise a design, which made it possible to move one, and which helped more people make their own?
