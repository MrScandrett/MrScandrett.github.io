History

The Short Origin Story

Chess began as a war-and-strategy game in ancient India, then changed as it traveled across continents and centuries.

  • India — c. 6th century CE: An ancestor called chaturanga appears, representing infantry, cavalry, elephants, and chariots.
  • Persia: The game becomes shatranj. The words check and checkmate descend from Persian and Arabic roots.
  • Islamic world & Europe: The game spreads along trade, scholarship, and conquest routes over several centuries.
  • Late medieval Europe: The queen and bishop gain long-range movement, creating the fast, tactical game we play today.

Goal of the Game

Checkmate: the enemy king is under attack and has no legal escape. Capturing pieces helps, but checkmate is the only real win.

Rules

How a Turn Works

White moves first. Players alternate. Each turn is one legal move by one piece.

1 · Move

Move one piece legally. Pieces cannot jump over each other — except the knight, which is the unique exception.

2 · Capture

Move onto an enemy piece's square to capture it. Pawns move forward but capture diagonally — the most unusual rule.

3 · Protect the King

You may never leave your own king in check. If attacked, you must move the king, block the attack, or capture the attacker.

Special Rules

Three Moves Worth Knowing

These special cases come up in almost every game. Knowing them early prevents surprises.

Castling The king and rook move in a single turn — the king slides two squares toward a rook, and the rook jumps to the other side. Used to protect the king and activate the rook. Only legal if neither piece has moved yet, and the king is not in check.
En Passant When a pawn uses its two-square first move to land beside an enemy pawn, the enemy pawn may capture it as if it had only moved one square. The capture must happen immediately on the next turn or the right expires.
Promotion When a pawn reaches the far edge of the board it becomes any piece the player chooses — almost always a queen. Promotion is a powerful threat that shapes endgame strategy.
Opening Theory

Six Famous Openings

The first few moves set the whole game's character. These openings have been studied for over a century and still appear at the top level today.

Italian Game 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 One of the oldest recorded openings. White's bishop aims straight at f7, the weakest square in Black's camp, and both sides develop quickly.
Ruy Lopez (Spanish) 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 The most deeply analyzed opening in chess history, named for a 16th-century Spanish priest. White pressures the knight that defends Black's e5 pawn.
Sicilian Defense 1. e4 c5 Black's most popular reply to 1.e4 at every serious level. It creates an unbalanced, asymmetrical fight instead of a mirrored center.
French Defense 1. e4 e6 Black builds a solid pawn chain and accepts a slightly cramped position early in exchange for a structure that is very hard to attack.
Queen's Gambit 1. d4 d5 2. c4 White offers a wing pawn to pull Black's d-pawn out of the center. Black almost never keeps the free pawn — it's rarely a gambit in practice.
King's Indian Defense 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 Black lets White build a big pawn center, then counterattacks it later with pawn breaks and a fianchettoed bishop on g7.
Skill Building

What Is a Blunder — and How to Avoid One

What Counts as a Blunder

A blunder is a move that throws away a large amount of material, the game, or a winning position — much worse than a normal inaccuracy. Even world champions blunder under pressure. Common types include:

  • Hanging a piece: moving to, or leaving a piece on, a square an enemy piece can simply capture for free.
  • Missing a tactic: walking into a fork, pin, or skewer that wins material.
  • Ignoring a threat: making your own plan while the opponent's last move set up checkmate or a winning capture.
  • The overloaded defender: moving a piece that was the only thing guarding something else.

The CCT Habit

Before playing any move, scan for Checks, Captures, and Threats — yours and your opponent's. This one habit prevents most blunders.

1 · Checks

Can either king be put in check right now, by either side?

2 · Captures

What can be captured on the board — including the piece you're about to move?

3 · Threats

What did your opponent's last move threaten? Answer that before starting your own plan.

Slow down in time pressure and after your opponent's move especially — that is when most blunders happen.

Championship History

World Championship Eras

An unbroken lineage of champions stretches back to 1886 — one of the longest continuous title lines in any sport or game.

  • 1886 — Wilhelm Steinitz: Becomes the first officially recognized World Champion, holding the title for eight years.
  • 1894–1921 — Emanuel Lasker: Holds the title for 27 years, the longest reign of any World Champion.
  • 1927 — Alexander Alekhine: Defeats José Raúl Capablanca in a marathon 34-game match in Buenos Aires.
  • 1948 — Mikhail Botvinnik: Wins a tournament to fill the title after Alekhine's death, opening decades of Soviet dominance.
  • 1972 — Bobby Fischer: Defeats Boris Spassky in Reykjavik — the Cold War "Match of the Century" — ending 24 years of Soviet control of the title.
  • 1985 — Garry Kasparov: Becomes champion at 22 after a legendary rivalry with Anatoly Karpov, then the youngest champion ever.
  • 2013 — Magnus Carlsen: Defeats Viswanathan Anand and begins a decade of dominance, later becoming the highest-rated player in history.
  • 2023 — Ding Liren: Wins the title after Carlsen declines to defend it, defeating Ian Nepomniachtchi in tiebreaks.
  • 2024 — Gukesh Dommaraju: Defeats Ding Liren at age 18, becoming the youngest undisputed World Champion in history.

Timeline

Chess Across Time

The board stayed recognizable for fifteen centuries. The rules evolved into a faster, sharper game.

c. 6th century Chaturanga

An Indian war game with infantry, cavalry, elephants, chariots, and a ruler — echoed in today's six piece types.

7th–10th centuries Shatranj

Persian and Arabic scholars preserve, study, and spread the game. The words check and checkmate enter European languages.

15th century Modern Power

The queen and bishop gain long-range movement, making attacks far quicker and the opening phase much more critical.

19th century onward Standard Play

Wilhelm Steinitz became the first official World Champion in 1886. Clocks, tournaments, and eventually computer chess shaped the modern game and its global community.

CheckThe king is under direct attack by at least one enemy piece.
CheckmateThe king is in check and has no legal move to escape. The game ends.
StalemateA player has no legal move but the king is not in check. The game is a draw.
CastlingA single-turn king-and-rook swap used to improve king safety and develop the rook.
PromotionA pawn reaching the far rank may become any piece — almost always a queen.
En passantA special pawn capture available immediately after an opponent's two-square pawn advance.
PinA piece that cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it to attack.
BlunderA serious mistake that loses significant material, checkmate, or a winning position — the biggest category of avoidable loss.
OpeningThe first phase of the game, roughly the first ten to fifteen moves, focused on development and center control.
Control the center Pieces placed near the center squares e4, d4, e5, d5 control more of the board and limit the opponent's options.
Develop early Move knights and bishops off the back rank in the first several moves. A piece on the starting square does nothing.
Castle early The king is vulnerable in the center. Castle within the first ten moves to keep it safe and connect the rooks.
Piece values As a rough guide: pawn = 1, knight ≈ 3, bishop ≈ 3, rook ≈ 5, queen ≈ 9. Two bishops together are often worth slightly more.
Knights before bishops Knights need a fixed number of moves to go anywhere. Bishops are fast but need open diagonals — develop knights first.
