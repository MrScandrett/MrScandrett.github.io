Rainbows

A rainbow isn't an object hanging in the sky. It's a direction. Millions of falling raindrops each send one color toward your eye, and every one of those drops sits about 42° from the shadow of your own head.

In this lesson you'll follow a single sunbeam through a single raindrop, find out why light piles up at one special angle, and then build a whole rainbow by moving the Sun.

Part 1

Follow one sunbeam through one raindrop

A falling raindrop is almost a perfect sphere of water. Sunlight that hits it does three things, in order:

  1. Bends going in. Light slows down in water, so it changes direction at the surface. That's refraction.
  2. Bounces off the back. Some of the light reflects off the inside of the far wall. That's an internal reflection.
  3. Bends again coming out. It refracts a second time as it leaves the drop and heads back toward the sunny side of the sky, where you're standing.

Water bends violet light a little more than red light. Its index of refraction is about 1.331 for red and 1.344 for violet. That small difference splits white light into colors, which is called dispersion.

Part 2

Why the light piles up at 42°

Sunlight lands evenly across the whole face of a drop, so rays strike at every distance from the center. Each one leaves at its own angle. The graph tracks that angle for every hit point. The bars on the right count how much light leaves at each angle.

The turnaround point. Near the top of each curve, the line goes flat. Rays that hit anywhere in a wide band of the drop all leave at nearly the same angle. Light from all of them stacks up in one direction, and that direction glows brightly. Physicists call it the angle of minimum deviation. René Descartes worked this out by hand in 1637, tracing rays through a glass globe filled with water.
Red peaks higher than violet. Red's peak is at about 42.4° and violet's at about 40.5°. So red forms the outer edge of the main bow and violet the inner edge. The whole bow is only about 2° wide, about the width of one finger held at arm's length.

Part 3

Paint the rainbow across the sky

Now zoom out. Stand with the Sun at your back and look at your shadow. The shadow of your head points straight at the antisolar point, the spot in the sky directly opposite the Sun. Every drop that sits 42° from that point sends you red. Drops sitting about 40.5° away send you violet. All the drops at one angle make a circle around your shadow, which is why the bow is curved.

Test a drop: click or tap anywhere in the sky to see what that one raindrop sends to your eye.

It moves with you. Walk left or right. The trees slide past, but the bow stays centered on your shadow. A different set of drops is doing the work at every step, so you can never reach the end of a rainbow.
Low Sun, tall bow. The top of the bow sits 42° minus the Sun's height above the horizon. Once the Sun is higher than 42°, the whole bow drops below the horizon. That's why rainbows show up in the morning and late afternoon, not at noon in summer.
Everyone sees their own. The person next to you has a different shadow, so their bow is made by different drops. Two people never see exactly the same rainbow.

Part 4

Double rainbows and the dark band

Some light bounces twice inside the drop before it leaves. Its curve in Part 2 bottoms out near 51°, so it makes a second, larger bow outside the first.

  • Fainter. Each bounce lets some light escape out the back of the drop, so less is left for the second bow.
  • Colors flipped. The extra reflection mirrors the order. Red is on the inside of the secondary bow and violet on the outside.
  • Wider. The secondary spreads its colors over about 3.3°, compared with 2° for the primary.

Between the two bows is a strip of sky that looks darker. This is Alexander's dark band, named for Alexander of Aphrodisias, who described it around 200 CE. Look at the graph again. One-bounce light never leaves at more than 42°, and two-bounce light never leaves at less than 51°. Drops in between have no path to send sunlight to your eye, so that part of the sky stays dim. Inside the primary bow, all the rays that weren't at the peak still arrive, which is why that sky looks brighter.

Higher-order bows exist too. The third and fourth bows form around the Sun, not the antisolar point, and they are so faint against the bright sky near the Sun that they were first photographed only in 2011.

Part 5

Make (and find) your own

🌿 Garden-hose bow

On a sunny day, stand with the Sun behind you and spray a fine mist ahead. Find your head's shadow, then look about two hand-spans (roughly 42°) away from it in any direction. Spray higher and lower to complete more of the circle.

🥛 Glass-of-water spectrum

Set a full, clear glass of water on a sunny windowsill and put white paper on the floor. Move the paper until a strip of colors appears. The curved glass of water is refracting and dispersing light the same way a raindrop does.

📓 Rainbow journal

Every time you spot a rainbow, write down the time, which way you were facing, the Sun's height, and whether a secondary bow or dark band was visible. Over a season, check your notes against the "Sun higher than 42°" rule.

Big idea
A rainbow is a set of directions, not a place. Each color comes from the drops that sit at that color's special angle from your antisolar point.
Key terms
RefractionLight bending as it crosses from one material into another, because its speed changes.
Index of refractionHow much a material slows light. Water is about 1.33, so light moves 1.33 times slower in water than in a vacuum.
DispersionDifferent colors refracting by different amounts, which splits white light into a spectrum.
Internal reflectionLight bouncing off the inside surface of the drop.
Angle of minimum deviationThe exit angle where many rays bunch together. It makes the bright bow at 42°.
Antisolar pointThe point in the sky directly opposite the Sun, where the shadow of your head points.
Alexander's dark bandThe darker sky between the primary and secondary bows.
Supernumerary bowsFaint stripes inside the primary bow, caused by wave interference.
