What makes an eruption explosive?

Predict how magma will behave, test one change at a time, then explain your evidence. You will compare eruption styles, interpret VEI, and separate historical evidence from model outputs.

Viscosity means resistance to flow. More silica generally makes molten magma more viscous; cooler magma and more crystals also resist flow. Gas can escape more readily from fluid magma. In viscous magma, trapped gas can expand and fragment the melt during ascent. Chemistry alone does not determine an eruption.

Choose Exploration to unlock the viscosity slider. The slider changes illustrative flow resistance; it does not calculate silica chemistry or gas pressure. A shield volcano grows mostly from fluid lava; a stratovolcano builds layers of lava and fragmented material. A caldera is a large depression formed when the ground collapses after magma withdrawal.

The VEI scale

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) describes explosive eruptions using ejecta volume and other evidence. From VEI 2 upward, successive volume bands increase by about ten times. VEI 0–1 have different thresholds. VEI is not a death toll, a lava-flow speed, or a forecast. Compare the colored VEI bar with the historical ejecta estimate; bulk tephra (fragmented erupted rock) and dense-rock-equivalent volumes are different measures.

Volcanic hazards reach beyond lava

Ashfall can damage lungs, machinery and roofs. A pyroclastic density current is a fast, ground-hugging mixture of hot gas and particles. A lahar mixes water with volcanic sediment and can travel down valleys long after an eruption. Krakatoa’s 1883 tsunamis caused widespread deaths; a lava animation cannot represent that hazard.

Tambora’s 1815 eruption sent sulfur gases into the stratosphere. Sulfate aerosols reduced incoming sunlight and contributed to the cold conditions of 1816, the “Year Without a Summer,” including crop failures in parts of Europe and North America. Climate effects depend on gas release and atmospheric processes, not just VEI.

Safety and model limits

This is a classroom model, not an eruption forecast. Real volcanic behavior depends on changing seismic activity, local topography, magma chemistry, groundwater interaction, and meteorological conditions that are simplified here.

Phase order follows published geological sources. Radial valley incisions steer ground-hugging pyroclastic flows down ravines; Mount St. Helens illustrates the catastrophic directed lateral blast; Tambora and Krakatoa illustrate summit caldera subsidence; Eyjafjallajökull includes ice cap retreat with phreatomagmatic steam generation; and atmospheric wind advection drives ash drift.

Fine-scale blast dynamics, true debris avalanche mass-wasting, tsunami propagation, lava tube systems, and atmospheric chemical conversion are not numerically simulated. Smoke, ash, and ground particles are educational visualizations, not certified hazard zone boundaries.

A magma chamber is a subsurface region containing melt, crystals and gas, rather than an empty tank. A crystal mush contains crystals and interconnected melt; brighter melt lenses are schematic. A dike cuts across rock layers; a sill runs along them. Exsolution releases dissolved gas; the fragmentation level marks where ascending magma breaks into particles. A cryptodome is an intrusion inside a volcanic edifice. DRE removes pore space from erupted volume estimates. The vertical exaggeration label gives the ratio of vertical to horizontal scale. Rock pressure uses ρgz with crust density 2,700 kg/m³; column buoyancy uses a 250 kg/m³ density contrast. Neither is chamber overpressure. All geometry, colours, zone depths and stage interpolation are teaching assumptions. Melt fractions are source estimates only where marked inferred; elsewhere they are illustrative. Reservoir and melt volumes use a 3D spheroid model, except the dated Yellowstone seismic volumes. Erupted DRE is independently estimated from deposits; unavailable values remain unavailable. Use official observatory information for real hazards.
