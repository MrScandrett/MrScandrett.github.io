Before you decide a map is “wrong”

A projection is a tool, not a neutral photograph. Ask what job it was made to do.

  • Navigation: Mercator keeps local angles, helping a constant compass bearing plot as a straight line.
  • Comparing land area: equal-area maps such as Gall–Peters preserve relative area, not familiar shape.
  • Classroom reference: compromise maps balance several errors; no balance makes them distortion-free.
  • Distance from one place: an azimuthal map can show true distances or directions from its center, depending on the type.
Society matters: map conventions affect which places look central, large, near, or important. Projection choice does not create inequality, but it can reinforce habits of attention. Name the projection, its center, and its purpose.

What each family gives up

There are infinitely many projections and many named examples. These are representative families—not a ranking from “most correct” to “least correct.”

Cylindrical

Imagine wrapping a cylinder around Earth. Mercator greatly enlarges high latitudes; Greenland looks comparable to Africa even though Africa is about fourteen times larger.

Equal-area

Every equal-area projection preserves relative area across the map. It necessarily changes shapes, angles, distances, or all three.

Conic & azimuthal

Conic maps work especially well across middle-latitude regions. Azimuthal maps radiate from a chosen center and are useful for polar or route-focused views.

Prediction check

Before checking: which looks larger in real surface area—Africa or Greenland?

Map outlines are simplified teaching silhouettes, not political boundaries. Continents are a geographic convention: Europe and Asia are often taught separately, while Afro-Eurasia is one continuous landmass. Projection principles follow standard cartographic definitions; compare maps with their stated purpose and projection information.
