How coal, oil & natural gas form

All three fossil fuels begin when dead organic matter is buried faster than decomposers can recycle it. From there, heat, pressure, and time — and which organisms were buried — split the story into three distinct fuels.

The one idea underneath all three fuels

Burial beats decay

Left exposed to oxygen, dead organisms are broken down by fungi and bacteria within years to centuries, returning carbon to the air as CO₂. Fossil fuels only form where organic debris sinks into oxygen-poor mud — a waterlogged peat bog, stagnant lake bed, or anoxic seafloor — and gets sealed beneath accumulating sediment before decay finishes.

1

Produce

Plants and marine algae capture sunlight and CO₂ to build energy-rich carbon molecules.

2

Bury

Dead biomass settles into anoxic sediment and is buried faster than microbes can oxidize it.

3

Cook

Rising overburden pressure and Earth's geothermal heat transform that carbon over millions of years.

Geological Scenarios

From land plants

Coal: a multi-stage squeeze

Coal forms from terrestrial plants — giant clubmosses, ferns, and woody trees — buried in swampy wetlands. As layers of mud and sand pile overhead, rising pressure and temperature squeeze out water and volatile gases (CO₂ and CH₄), dramatically compacting the seam and concentrating the remaining carbon.

Why Carboniferous coal is so abundant: Vast tropical swamp forests, wet and low in oxygen, buried enormous volumes of woody plant debris. Whether that debris also resisted decay because lignin-eating fungi hadn't evolved yet is a genuinely contested question among scientists — see The Evolution of Wood for both sides of that debate.

From marine microorganisms

Oil & natural gas: cooked kerogen

Oil and natural gas mostly come from a completely different source: microscopic marine algae and plankton, not land trees. When plankton die, they sink into oxygen-poor seafloor mud and mix with fine clay. Buried miles underground, that organic mud turns into a waxy solid called kerogen. Between roughly 60 °C and 150 °C (the oil window), kerogen cracks into liquid crude oil; deeper and hotter (150–225 °C, the gas window), chains crack further into methane (CH₄).

Math & deep time · "Buried Sunshine"

Millions of years to cook, centuries to burn

Fossil fuels are literally concentrated ancient sunlight captured by photosynthesis hundreds of millions of years ago. Ecologists estimate that a single liter of modern gasoline represents roughly 25,900 kg of ancient marine plankton or plant matter (Jeffrey Dukes, 2003). Three fuels, three origins

Common myth, corrected: Oil does not come from dinosaurs. Dinosaurs were far too rare—and lived mostly on land—to form giant marine basins of petroleum. The overwhelming majority of crude oil's carbon came from microscopic marine algae and plankton.
