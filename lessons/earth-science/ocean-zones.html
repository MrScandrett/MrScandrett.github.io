Section 2

Five layers, sorted by light

Oceanographers split the open ocean into layers mainly by how much sunlight reaches them. Light controls whether plants can grow, and that decides where food comes from. The Greek-based names all end in -pelagic, meaning “open sea.”

Boundaries are averages. In murky coastal water the photic zone may end after 20 m; in the clearest tropical water, useful light reaches about 200 m.

Section 3

How deep is deep?

Drawn to scale, the sunlit layer is a thin skin — less than 2% of the way to the bottom of Challenger Deep. More than 90% of the ocean’s water lies below it, in permanent darkness. Select a landmark to compare it.

Section 4

Where do the colors go?

Sunlight is a mix of colors. Water absorbs them at different rates: red is absorbed first, then orange and yellow, while blue travels farthest in clear water. That’s why the deep sea looks blue — and why a bright red animal looks black at depth.

Why so many deep-sea animals are red. Below a few tens of metres, almost no red light is left to bounce off them, so red is camouflage. Many deep shrimp, jellies, and fishes are red or black for exactly this reason.

Section 5

The weight of water

Every 10 metres of seawater adds about one more atmosphere of pressure — the weight of all the water stacked above you. Air at the surface already pushes with 1 atm, so at 10 m you feel 2 atm. This is called hydrostatic pressure.

Push on a thumbnail (1 cm²)2 kg
Balloon volume (Boyle’s law)500 mL

Gas squeezes, water doesn’t. Air spaces shrink with depth (Boyle’s law: double the pressure, half the volume). Water and tissue barely compress at all. That’s why research crews send down foam coffee cups — the air in the foam collapses and the cups come back shrunken.
So why aren’t deep-sea fish crushed? They have no air spaces to collapse — many lack a gas-filled swim bladder, and their bodies are mostly water at the same pressure inside and out. The real problem is chemistry: pressure warps proteins. Deep fish store a molecule called TMAO that helps proteins keep their shape. Fish need more TMAO the deeper they live, and around 8,200–8,400 m the amount needed would pull in too much water — a likely reason no fish has been found deeper.

Section 6

Cold, colder, coldest: the thermocline

The Sun only warms the top of the ocean, and wind mixes that warm water into a surface mixed layer. Below it, temperature drops fast through the thermocline. Under about 1,000 m, nearly all ocean water is between 0 and 4 °C — cold water that sank long ago near the poles.

The mixed layer never sits still

Wind and the turning Earth push the surface water into currents that carry heat from the tropics toward the poles. This NASA visualization traces two and a half years of real surface currents. Everything you see moving lives in the sunlight zone; the midnight zone below feels almost none of it.

Section 7

Food from above: marine snow

Below the sunlight zone, nothing can photosynthesize. Most deep animals eat food that started at the surface: dead plankton, fecal pellets, mucus, and shed skins that clump together and sink as marine snow. Animals eat most of it on the way down, so less and less reaches each deeper layer.

Travel time from 100 m9 days

The biological pump. Carbon that phytoplankton pull from the air sinks with marine snow. Some is buried in seafloor mud for thousands of years. Together with daily migrating animals, this biological pump helps the ocean store carbon away from the atmosphere.
Feasts and exceptions. A sunken whale can feed seafloor life for years. At hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, microbes make food from chemicals instead of light — chemosynthesis. These are rich oases, but rare and small compared with the vast abyss.

Model: the “Martin curve,” a widely used fit to sediment-trap data (food flux ∝ (depth ÷ 100 m)−0.86). Real values vary a lot by region and season.

Section 8

Built for the dark

Deep-sea animals face darkness, scarce food, cold, and pressure. Many make their own light — bioluminescence — to hunt, hide, or signal; some even glow to hide their shadow, a trick called counterillumination. Section 9

Who has been down there?

Between 1960 and 2019, only three people reached Challenger Deep — fewer than the twelve who walked on the Moon. As of 2024, only about a quarter of the seafloor had been mapped in high detail. Select a dive to send the simulator to that depth.
