What Is the Water Cycle?

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water across Earth's surface, atmosphere, underground rock, and living organisms. Rather than moving in a single fixed loop, water travels along many branching routes: solar energy lifts water by warming and evaporation, while gravity pulls precipitation, mountain runoff, and groundwater downhill.

This system has no beginning or end. The water molecules in today's oceans have cycled through clouds, snowflakes, rivers, aquifers, and living cells for billions of years.

Key Idea: Scientists track Earth's water using pools (places where water is stored, like oceans, glaciers, and aquifers) and fluxes (processes that move water from one pool to another, like evaporation, precipitation, and runoff).

Stage 1: Evaporation

Solar energy warms liquid water in oceans, lakes, rivers, and moist soil. Fast-moving surface molecules break free of their neighbors and enter the air as invisible water vapor. About 86% of global evaporation comes directly from the oceans.

Driver: ☀️ Solar radiation & dry air gradient What happens: Liquid water + heat energy → invisible rising water vapor

Stage 2: Condensation

As warm, moist air rises, it expands and cools. Once the air reaches its dew point (100% relative humidity), water vapor condenses onto microscopic dust, salt, or pollen particles, forming millions of tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals that appear as clouds and fog.

Driver: ❄️ Cooling air below the dew point What happens: Invisible vapor → visible liquid droplets or ice crystals

Stage 3: Precipitation

Inside clouds, droplets collide and merge — or ice crystals grow — until updrafts can no longer hold them aloft. Gravity pulls them down to Earth as rain, snow, sleet, or hail, depending on the temperature profile between the cloud and the ground.

Driver: ⬇️ Gravity overcoming updrafts What happens: Heavy droplets or crystals → rain, snow, sleet, or hail

Stage 4: Collection & Runoff

Water that reaches Earth's surface gathers in oceans, lakes, wetlands, glaciers, and snowpack. On land, gravity pulls excess rain and snowmelt downhill across slopes and through streams and rivers back toward lakes and the sea.

Driver: ⬇️ Gravity across land slopes What happens: Surface water → streams, rivers, lakes, glaciers, and oceans

Stage 5: Infiltration & Groundwater

When rain or snowmelt lands on porous soil and fractured rock, gravity pulls it downward through tiny spaces — a process called infiltration. Deeper down, it recharges underground aquifers, where groundwater slowly flows for months, decades, or millennia before emerging at springs, rivers, or coastlines.

Driver: ⬇️ Gravity through porous soil & rock What happens: Surface water → soil moisture → slow-moving aquifers

Stage 6: Transpiration

Plants draw water from soil through their roots, pull it upward through microscopic xylem tubes, and release invisible water vapor through tiny leaf pores called stomata. Combined with surface evaporation, scientists call this total flow evapotranspiration.

Driver: ☀️ Sun-driven leaf evaporation & plant stomata What happens: Soil water → roots & xylem → vapor diffused through leaves

Bonus processes: Water doesn't always melt or boil its way between states. Snow and ice can turn straight into vapor without ever becoming liquid — that's called sublimation. The reverse happens too: water vapor can freeze straight into ice crystals in a process called deposition (forming frost on cold mornings or ice crystals inside high clouds). Source: NOAA Education, "The water cycle."

Official USGS Description

Introduction

The water cycle describes where water is on Earth and how it moves. Water is stored in the atmosphere, on the land surface, and below the ground. It can be a liquid, a solid, or a gas. Liquid water can be fresh, saline (salty), or a mix (brackish). Water moves between the places it is stored — at large scales and at very small scales, naturally and because of human actions. Human water use affects where water is stored, how it moves, and how clean it is.

Pools

Pools store water. Ninety-six percent of all water is stored in oceans and is saline. On land, saline water is stored in saline lakes. Fresh water is stored in liquid form in freshwater lakes, artificial reservoirs, rivers, and wetlands. Water is stored in solid, frozen form in ice sheets and glaciers, and in snowpack at high elevations or near the Earth's poles. Water vapor is a gas and is stored as atmospheric moisture over the ocean and land. In the soil, frozen water is stored as permafrost and liquid water is stored as soil moisture. Deeper below ground, liquid water is stored as groundwater in aquifers, within cracks and pores in the rock.

Fluxes

Fluxes move water between pools. As it moves, water can change form between liquid, solid, and gas. Circulation mixes water in the oceans and transports water vapor in the atmosphere. Water moves between the atmosphere and the surface through evaporation, evapotranspiration, and precipitation. Water moves across the surface through snowmelt, runoff, and streamflow. Water moves into the ground through infiltration and groundwater recharge. Underground, groundwater flows within aquifers. It can return to the surface through natural groundwater discharge into rivers, the ocean, and from springs.

Human Use and Water Availability

Humans alter the water cycle. People redirect rivers, build dams to store water, and drain water from wetlands for development. People use water from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and groundwater aquifers to supply homes and communities, for agricultural irrigation and livestock grazing, and for industrial activities such as thermoelectric power generation, mining, and aquaculture. The amount of water that is available depends on how much water is in each pool (water quantity), when and how fast water moves (water timing), how much water is used (water use), and how clean the water is (water quality).

Water Quality

Humans affect water quality. In agricultural and urban areas, irrigation and precipitation wash fertilizers and pesticides into rivers and groundwater. Power plants and factories return heated and contaminated water to rivers. Runoff carries chemicals, sediment, and sewage into rivers and lakes. Downstream from these sources, contaminated water can cause harmful algal blooms, spread diseases, and harm habitats. Climate change is affecting the water cycle's quality, quantity, timing, and use — causing ocean acidification, sea level rise, and more extreme weather. Understanding these impacts helps people work toward using water sustainably.

Three States of Matter on Earth

Solid (Ice & Snow)

Below 0°C (32°F) at sea-level pressure

H₂O molecules lock into an open hexagonal crystal lattice and vibrate in place. That open spacing makes solid ice ~9% less dense than liquid water, so icebergs and lake ice float — insulating aquatic life below.

Liquid (Fresh & Salt Water)

0°C to 100°C (32°F to 212°F)

Molecules slide and tumble closely past one another, flowing in oceans, rivers, clouds, and aquifers. Even at cool temperatures, the fastest molecules at a water surface regularly escape into the air as vapor.

Gas (Water Vapor)

Boils at 100°C (212°F) — evaporates at any temperature

Invisible individual H₂O molecules spread far apart and move rapidly through the atmosphere. Steam you see above a kettle and clouds in the sky are actually tiny liquid droplets that already condensed back out of invisible vapor.

Phase Changes: Melting (solid → liquid), Freezing (liquid → solid), Evaporation & Boiling (liquid → gas), Condensation (gas → liquid), Sublimation (solid → gas), and Deposition (gas → solid). Every transition absorbs or releases thermal energy!

Two Engines: The Sun & Gravity

Without two opposing forces working together, water would stall in place. Solar energy supplies the heat that lifts water against gravity and powers global winds, while gravity pulls condensed water and surface runoff back downhill toward the sea.

☀️ Solar Energy: Lifts & Changes State

  • Heats oceans, lakes, leaves, and snowpack to drive evaporation, transpiration, and sublimation.
  • Creates uneven heating across Earth, driving atmospheric pressure differences, winds, and moisture transport.
  • Stores huge amounts of latent heat in vapor; about 75% of heat transfer into the atmosphere happens through evaporation and condensation.

⬇️ Gravity: Pulls Water Downhill

  • Pulls heavy cloud droplets and ice crystals to the ground as rain, snow, sleet, and hail.
  • Drives mountain snowmelt, streams, rivers, and surface runoff back toward lakes and oceans.
  • Pulls rainwater down through soil pores (infiltration) and moves groundwater slowly through deep aquifers.

    Where Water Goes

    Almost all water on Earth — 96.5% in oceans plus 0.9% in saline groundwater and salt lakes — is too salty to drink. Only 2.5% is freshwater, and nearly all of that is frozen in glaciers or tucked deep underground.     Vocabulary

    Filter by water-cycle processes or reservoirs and system terms to review key science vocabulary.

    Evaporation

    The process by which liquid water turns into invisible water vapor and rises into the atmosphere. Heat from the Sun powers this process.

    Condensation

    The process by which water vapor cools below its dew point and changes back into liquid water droplets or ice crystals, forming clouds and fog.

    Precipitation

    Water that falls from clouds to Earth under gravity in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

    Collection

    The gathering of water in oceans, lakes, rivers, ice, and soil. Collected water stays stored until it evaporates, runs off, or infiltrates.

    Runoff

    Rain or melted snow flowing downhill over the land surface into streams, rivers, lakes, and eventually the ocean.

    Infiltration

    The process by which water soaks into the soil and moves downward through pores and cracks to become soil moisture or groundwater.

    Transpiration

    The process by which plants absorb soil water through roots and release water vapor through leaf pores (stomata). Combined with evaporation, it's called evapotranspiration.

    Sublimation & Deposition

    Direct solid-to-gas (sublimation, e.g., dry-air snow loss) and gas-to-solid (deposition, e.g., frost) transitions that skip the liquid phase.

    Groundwater

    Liquid water stored beneath Earth's surface inside saturated soil and rock layers, feeding springs, rivers, and wells.

    Aquifer

    An underground layer of permeable rock, sand, or gravel that holds and transmits usable amounts of groundwater.

    Water Vapor

    The invisible gas form of water. It floats in the atmosphere and becomes visible clouds only after cooling and condensing into droplets or crystals.

    Dew Point

    The temperature to which air must cool (at constant pressure) to reach 100% relative humidity and begin condensing into dew, fog, or clouds.

    Pool & Flux

    USGS hydrologic terms: a pool is a place where water is stored (ocean, glacier, aquifer), and a flux is a process moving water between pools.

    Residence Time

    The average length of time a water molecule remains in a reservoir — from ~9 days in the atmosphere to thousands of years in oceans and deep aquifers.

    Wonder Zone: Beyond the Basics

    The water cycle connects directly to weather, climate, ecosystems, and even planetary science. Explore these big ideas next:

    🧊 Glaciers: Frozen Storage

    Glaciers are massive reservoirs of freshwater. As Earth's climate warms, mountain glaciers and ice sheets melt faster, returning ancient water to the oceans and altering sea levels and seasonal river flows.

    💧 Aquifers: Underground Reservoirs

    Groundwater fills hidden aquifers beneath our feet. Some deep aquifers took thousands of years to fill and provide crucial freshwater for drinking and farming — pumping them faster than rain recharges them depletes them for generations.

    ⛈️ Severe Weather

    When water vapor condenses inside storm clouds, it releases stored latent heat back into the air — fueling hurricanes, thunderstorms, and updrafts. Warmer oceans supply extra water vapor and energy to storms.

    🌍 Climate Change & Drought

    Warming temperatures can intensify both drought and heavy rainfall: warmer air can hold ~7% more water vapor per °C before saturating, pulling moisture from dry soils while feeding heavier downpours when storms finally form. (Source: NOAA Education)

    🪐 Water on Other Worlds

    Scientists study ancient river deltas on Mars and subsurface oceans beneath the icy crusts of moons like Europa and Enceladus. Earth's active liquid-ice-vapor surface cycle remains rare in our solar system.

    🧬 Water in Living Things

    You are about 60% water, and leafy plants are up to 95% water! Living organisms are themselves temporary pools inside the water cycle, taking in and releasing water every single day.
