Section 2 · Why Continents Move

Four Big Ideas & Three Lines of Evidence

Alfred Wegener noticed in 1912 that continents looked like torn pieces of a single newspaper. Explore the evidence that convinced geologists Earth’s crust really moves.

Concept 01

Pangaea was not permanent

Supercontinents assemble and break apart over hundreds of millions of years because tectonic plates keep moving beneath our feet.

Concept 02

Oceans can open

When crust stretches and splits, magma rises and cools into new oceanic basalt—building basins like the Atlantic Ocean.

Concept 03

Continents can collide

India detached from Gondwana, raced north across the Tethys Ocean, and crashed into Asia, crumpling the crust into the Himalayas.

Concept 04

Motion continues today

The Atlantic is still widening by ~2.5 cm/yr and Australia moves ~7 cm/yr north. Today’s world map is just one frame in an ongoing movie.

Evidence 01

Matching coastlines & rock layers

South America and Africa fit best along their submerged continental shelves, and ancient rock belts match across the ocean gap.

Evidence 02

Fossils on separate continents

Freshwater reptiles and heavy-seeded ferns span continents now divided by wide oceans—clear proof those landmasses were once joined.

Evidence 03

Magnetic stripes on the seafloor

Earth’s magnetic field flips periodically. New basalt at mid-ocean ridges records those flips like a two-sided geological tape recorder.

Section 5 · The Tectonic Engine

Why Plates Move: The Physics Behind Wegener’s Idea

In 1912, meteorologist Alfred Wegener assembled compelling fossil, rock, and glacial clues for continental drift. Yet geologists initially rejected his hypothesis because he proposed continents plowed directly through solid ocean basalt powered by lunar tidal forces—a mechanism physicists proved was far too weak.

The 1960s discovery of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics revealed the true planetary engine: continents do not plow through oceanic crust; they ride passively atop rigid lithospheric plates moving over Earth’s ductile asthenosphere, driven by three forces:

1. Mantle Convection

Deep Thermal Engine

Heat from Earth’s core and radioactive decay drives slow creeping convection cells in the ductile mantle (~1–10 cm/yr), circulating heat toward the surface.

2. Ridge Push

Gravitational Sliding

Mid-ocean ridges stand 2–3 km higher than abyssal plains because hot newly formed crust is buoyant. Gravity pushes elevated lithosphere down and outward from ridge crests.

3. Slab Pull (Dominant)

Sinking Subduction Anchor

As oceanic lithosphere cools and ages away from ridges, it grows denser than the asthenosphere below. Sinking into subduction trenches, the cold slab pulls the entire plate behind it.

    Key Terms

    Plate Tectonics Vocabulary

    • Pangaea: Supercontinent uniting most of Earth’s land from ~335 to 175 million years ago.
    • Laurasia & Gondwana: The northern (North America, Eurasia) and southern (South America, Africa, India, Antarctica, Australia) supercontinent halves after initial rifting.
    • Continental drift: Wegener’s 1912 insight that continents move slowly across Earth’s surface over geologic time.
    • Plate tectonics: The unifying theory explaining rigid lithospheric plates moving atop the ductile asthenosphere.
    • Seafloor spreading: Upwelling magma forms new oceanic crust at mid-ocean ridges, pushing plates apart and recording magnetic reversals.
    • Slab pull: Cold, dense oceanic crust sinking at subduction zones, acting as the primary pull on tectonic plates.
