Start with a scientist's definition

What counts as an insect?

3

Body regions

Every adult insect has a head, thorax, and abdomen.

6

Jointed legs

All six attach to the thorax. Eight legs means it is not an insect.

1

Pair of antennae

These sensory organs detect chemicals, touch, vibration, and more.

Words matter: In everyday speech, “bug” can mean any small crawling animal. In science, insects are one class within the arthropods—and a true bug is specifically an insect in the order Hemiptera, such as a cicada, aphid, or stink bug.

Structure → function

An insect is a three-part machine

01

Head: sensory center with eyes, antennae, brain, and specialized mouthparts.

02

Thorax: movement center; all six legs and any wings connect here.

03

Abdomen: contains much of the digestive, respiratory, and reproductive systems.

04

Exoskeleton: a tough outer covering that supports, protects, and reduces water loss.

Holometabolous

Larvae and adults often use different foods or habitats, which can reduce competition between young and mature insects.

Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult

Hemimetabolous

There is no pupal stage. Each nymph resembles the adult, then becomes larger and more developed after every molt.

Outdoor investigation

Run a 10-minute bug biodiversity survey

Choose

Mark a small habitat: under a shrub, beside a wall, or in a patch of grass.

Observe

Look without touching. Record body regions, legs, wings, color, and behavior.

Count

Tally each kind you see. Photograph from a respectful distance if permitted.

Compare

Survey a second habitat. Graph the counts and explain what might cause differences.

Field ethics: Leave organisms and habitat as you found them. Do not handle animals you cannot identify, disturb nests, or collect specimens without teacher permission.
