# Inside Classic Pong — Historical Photographs & Technical Diagrams

All historical photographs and technical diagrams in `lessons/computer-science/graphics-and-games/game-tutorials/pong.html` are open-source or public-domain archival materials configured to open in the classroom lightbox (`data-zoomable`).

## Historical Hardware & Arcade Photographs (`data-lightbox-group="pong-history"`)

- `tennis-for-two-1958.jpg`: **William Higinbotham's *Tennis for Two* (October 1958)**. Visitors playing the first interactive electronic video game during Visitors' Day at Brookhaven National Laboratory (Upton, New York). Powered by an analog computer (Donner Model 30 operational amplifier unit) calculating air resistance, bounce velocity, and ballistic gravity trajectories. Photograph by Brookhaven National Laboratory, **Public Domain (U.S. Federal Government)**. [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tennis_For_Two_in_1958.jpg).
- `tennis-for-two-oscilloscope.jpg`: **DuMont Lab Oscilloscope Type 304-A Display Screen (1958)**. Shows the 5-inch cathode ray tube displaying the side-view tennis court, vertical net line, and the electron-beam tennis ball bouncing across the net. Photograph by Brookhaven National Laboratory, **Public Domain (U.S. Federal Government)**. [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tennis_For_Two_on_a_DuMont_Lab_Oscilloscope_Type_304-A.jpg).
- `ralph-baer-brown-box.png`: **Ralph Baer's "Brown Box" Multi-Game Prototype (1968)**. The father of video games, Ralph H. Baer, built the first home video game console prototype housed in woodgrain adhesive vinyl at Sanders Associates. It ran vacuum tube and transistorized diode-matrix logic to generate two movable squares and a bouncing ball on standard CRT televisions. Smithsonian National Museum of American History, **CC0 1.0 Universal (Public Domain Dedication)**. [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ralph_H._Baer_-_%22Brown_Box%22_prototype.png).
- `magnavox-odyssey-console.jpg`: **1972 Magnavox Odyssey Home Console, Controllers & Game Cards**. The commercial production release of Baer's Brown Box. Lacking a microprocessor, games were selected by inserting printed circuit cards that rewired internal diode-transistor logic paths, paired with translucent color screen overlays. Photograph by Evan-Amos (The Vanamo Online Game Museum), **Public Domain**. [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Magnavox-Odyssey-Console-Set.jpg).
- `atari-pong-arcade-cabinet.jpg`: **1972 Atari *Pong* Coin-Operated Arcade Cabinet**. Designed by Allan Alcorn for Nolan Bushnell's newly founded Atari Inc. Features a modified 19-inch black-and-white television in a custom yellow pedestal cabinet with rotary potentiometer paddle knobs. Preserved at The Strong National Museum of Play, Rochester, NY. Photograph by Rob DiCaterino, **CC BY 2.0 Generic**. [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Atari_Pong_arcade_game_cabinet.jpg).
- `ti-sn7400n-ttl-chip.jpg`: **Texas Instruments SN7400N Quad 2-Input NAND Gate DIP IC**. Representative of the 66 off-the-shelf 7400-series Transistor-Transistor Logic (TTL) integrated circuits Al Alcorn used to build *Pong*. Alcorn wire-wrapped sync generators, counters, and flip-flops without a CPU, ROM, or software. Photograph by Stefan506, **CC BY-SA 3.0 / GFDL**. [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:TI_SN7400N.jpg).
